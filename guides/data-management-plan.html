Planning · Data

How to write a data management plan that is useful, not just compliant

Funders, universities and ethics committees increasingly ask for a data management plan before research starts. Written well, it also saves you from the classic disasters: lost files, unreadable spreadsheets, consent forms that forbid sharing. This guide walks through each section with an example.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer A data management plan describes what data you will collect, how you will document, store and protect it, who can access it, and what happens to it after the project. Most templates have six parts: data description, documentation and metadata, ethics and legal issues, storage and backup, sharing and preservation, and responsibilities and resources. Keep it short, specific and updated as the project changes.

Who needs a data management plan

Almost every funded research project now needs one. Many national funders and research councils require a DMP at application or soon after award. In the United States, the National Institutes of Health has required a Data Management and Sharing Plan for most funded research since January 2023; its scientific data sharing site sets out the elements. European funders generally expect plans that follow the FAIR principles.

Even without a requirement, a DMP is worth an hour of a PhD student’s time. It is the document that makes you decide, before you collect anything, where files live, how they are named and what participants agree to.

The six sections, explained

1. Data description

List each type of data you will collect or reuse, its format and its rough size. Be concrete: “semi-structured interview audio (MP3, about 30 files, 2 GB), transcripts (DOCX, about 30 files), survey responses (CSV, about 400 rows)”. Say which existing datasets you will reuse and under what licence.

2. Documentation and metadata

Explain how someone else, or you in two years, will understand the data. That means a README file per dataset, a codebook defining every variable and its values, file naming conventions, version control, and any discipline metadata standard you will follow.

3. Ethics and legal compliance

Cover consent, anonymisation or pseudonymisation, data protection law that applies (such as GDPR for personal data from people in the EU or UK), intellectual property and any restrictions from data providers. The key check: your consent form must allow everything the DMP promises, especially sharing.

4. Storage and backup

Say where active data is stored, how it is backed up, and how sensitive data is protected. Use your institution’s managed storage rather than personal cloud accounts for anything identifiable. The common rule is 3-2-1: three copies, on two kinds of storage, one of them off site.

5. Sharing and preservation

State which data will be shared, where (a discipline repository or your institution’s repository), when, under what licence, and with what access controls. Explain what will be kept long term and what will be destroyed, and when. If you cannot share some data, say why.

6. Responsibilities and resources

Name who is responsible for each task and list costs, such as storage, transcription, repository fees or staff time. Funders often allow these costs in the budget, but only if you ask for them.

FAIR data in plain English

Many funders ask how your data will be FAIR. The FAIR principles mean:

PrincipleMeaningWhat you do
FindablePeople can discover the dataDeposit in a repository that issues a DOI; write good metadata
AccessiblePeople can get it, or know how to request itState access conditions; use open protocols
InteroperableIt works with other data and toolsUse open formats (CSV, not proprietary); use standard vocabularies
ReusableOthers can understand and legally reuse itInclude a codebook, README and clear licence

FAIR does not mean open. Sensitive data can be FAIR if its metadata is public and access is controlled.

A short worked example

Here is a condensed DMP for a mixed-methods master’s project on teachers’ use of AI tools:

Data: online survey of about 300 teachers (CSV, under 5 MB); 20 interviews (audio, about 3 GB; transcripts, DOCX).

Documentation: codebook for all survey variables; README per folder; files named project_type_ID_date_v01.

Ethics: consent covers anonymised data sharing; names and schools removed from transcripts; audio deleted after transcription is checked.

Storage: university encrypted drive with nightly backup; no personal data on laptops or personal cloud.

Sharing: anonymised survey data and codebook deposited in the university repository with a CC BY licence within 6 months of the end of the project; transcripts not shared because of re-identification risk.

Responsibilities: student manages data; supervisor approves the plan; no additional costs.

What changes for different kinds of project

Qualitative projects

Interviews, focus groups and field notes are the hardest data to anonymise, because people can be identified from stories as well as names. Plan how you will pseudonymise transcripts (a key file stored separately and securely), who transcribes (a professional service needs a confidentiality agreement), and whether audio is kept after transcripts are checked. Sharing is often limited to controlled access or to the codebook and interview guide.

Quantitative and survey projects

Focus on the codebook, version control of cleaned datasets and analysis scripts. Keep raw data read-only and do all cleaning in a script so every step can be repeated. Watch for indirect identifiers: age, job title and postcode together can identify a person even without a name.

Lab and computational projects

Describe instruments, file formats and volumes, which can be large. Say where code lives (a version-controlled repository), how environments are recorded, and which data are raw versus derived. Funders increasingly expect code to be shared alongside data.

Secondary data projects

If you only reuse existing data, the DMP is short but not empty. Record the source, version, licence and access conditions, any agreement you signed, and whether you are allowed to share derived data. Some licences forbid it.

How to write yours in an afternoon

  1. Find your template. Your funder or university almost certainly has one. DMPonline, run by the UK’s Digital Curation Centre, hosts templates for many funders and lets you share drafts with your supervisor.
  2. List every data type in a table first. The rest of the plan follows from it.
  3. Talk to your ethics office and library. Research data librarians review DMPs for free and know local storage options.
  4. Align with your consent form. Read them side by side.
  5. Keep it short. Two pages is typical. Specific beats long.
  6. Review it at milestones, such as after ethics approval and before analysis.

Common mistakes

  • Promising open sharing when the consent form does not allow it.
  • Saying “data will be stored securely” without naming where.
  • Forgetting reused data and its licence terms.
  • No codebook, so the data cannot be understood later.
  • Planning to share data “on request” with no process or contact that will outlast the project.
  • Writing the plan once and never updating it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a data management plan?

A data management plan is a short document describing what data a project will collect, how it will be documented, stored and protected, who can access it, and how it will be shared and preserved after the project. Most funders and many universities require one.

How long is a data management plan?

Usually one to three pages. Some funders set a limit, often two pages. A short, specific plan that names formats, storage locations, repositories and responsibilities is better than a long general one.

Do PhD students need a data management plan?

Many universities require one at registration or before data collection, and it is good practice even when not required. It forces early decisions about storage, naming, consent and sharing that are hard to fix later.

What does FAIR data mean?

FAIR stands for findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. It means data is deposited with good metadata and a persistent identifier, has clear access conditions, uses open formats and standards, and comes with documentation and a licence that allow reuse.

Where should I deposit research data?

Use a trusted repository that issues persistent identifiers such as DOIs. A discipline repository is usually best because the right people will find your data there; otherwise use your institution’s repository or a general one. Check whether your funder names preferred repositories, and avoid personal websites, which disappear.

Can I share qualitative data such as interviews?

Sometimes. It depends on consent, the risk of re-identification and the sensitivity of the topic. Many projects share anonymised transcripts under controlled access, and some share none. Plan this before collecting data and match your consent form to the plan.
