How to give a conference presentation people remember

Most conference talks are 12 to 15 minutes. That is time for one idea, explained well. This guide gives you a structure, slide rules and a rehearsal plan that work for first-time presenters and experienced researchers alike.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Build the talk around one main message you could say in a sentence. Spend about a fifth of your time on why the question matters, a fifth on what you did, two fifths on what you found, and the rest on what it means. Use roughly one slide per minute, with one point per slide and figures instead of text. Rehearse out loud at least three times with a timer, finish a minute early, and prepare answers to the five questions you are most likely to be asked.

Start with one message

Before you open slide software, finish this sentence: “If the audience remembers one thing from my talk, it should be that…”. Everything in the talk either supports that sentence or is cut.

This is harder than it sounds because your paper contains many results you care about. A short talk cannot carry them all. Choose the result that answers your main question most directly, and mention the others in a single slide or leave them for questions.

The well-known “Ten Simple Rules for Making Good Oral Presentations” (Bourne, 2007, PLoS Computational Biology) makes the same point first: talk to the audience you have, and give them less than you think, said more clearly.

The opening

Skip the outline slide and the long list of thanks. Open with the problem in terms anyone in the room cares about: a puzzle, a surprising number, a practical consequence. Then state your question in one sentence. Within the first minute the audience should know what you asked and why.

The methods

Show just enough for the audience to trust the results: the design, the data, the sample and the key measure. A simple diagram of the study design often replaces three slides of text. Detail belongs in the paper and in your answers to questions.

The results

Present results in the order that builds your argument, not necessarily the order in the paper. For each result slide, say what the figure shows before you say what it means: “The horizontal axis is age, the vertical axis is reaction time. Notice that the two groups separate after age sixty.”

The close

End with your main message, in words, on a slide that stays up during questions. Add one sentence on what comes next. Do not end on a slide that just says “Thank you” or “Questions?”; that is the slide people photograph.

A sample 15-minute slide plan

  1. Title slide: title as a statement, your name, affiliation, co-authors.
  2. The problem: one image or number that shows why it matters.
  3. What we know: the two or three findings your work builds on.
  4. The gap and the question: one sentence each.
  5. Study design: a diagram of who, what and how.
  6. Data and measures: the sample and the key outcome.
  7. Analysis: the approach in plain words.
  8. Main result: the single figure that answers the question.
  9. Supporting result: the check that rules out the obvious alternative.
  10. Second result: a subgroup or secondary finding, if it strengthens the message.
  11. Robustness: one slide on sensitivity analyses or limitations.
  12. What it means: implications for theory, practice or policy.
  13. Next steps: what you will do next and what others could do.
  14. Main message: your one sentence, contact details and a link or QR code to the paper.

Fourteen slides for fifteen minutes leaves a minute of slack. Drop slide 10 if you run long in rehearsal.

Slide rules that work

  • One point per slide. Make the slide title a full sentence stating that point (“Exposure doubled after the policy change”), not a label (“Results 2”).
  • About one slide per minute. Fewer if each slide has a complex figure.
  • Figures over text. Redraw figures from the paper with larger fonts, fewer panels and only the comparisons you discuss.
  • Large type. Body text no smaller than about 24 points; axis labels readable from the back of the room.
  • High contrast and colour-blind-safe palettes. Avoid red–green contrasts as the only way to tell groups apart.
  • No full paragraphs. If you need the audience to read it, you need to stop talking, and you will not.
  • Number your slides so people can refer to them in questions.

The W3C’s guidance on making presentations accessible covers contrast, captions, describing visuals aloud and sharing slides in advance. Following it improves the talk for everyone, not only for people with disabilities.

Rehearsal: the part most people skip

  1. First run, alone, out loud, with a timer. You will be over time. Cut slides, not speaking speed.
  2. Second run, recorded. Watch it once. Note filler words, places where you read from the slides, and transitions that do not work.
  3. Third run, to an audience. Colleagues, a lab meeting, or a friend outside your field. Ask them to tell you your main message afterwards. If they cannot, your talk does not yet make it clear.
  4. Final run, the day before, on the laptop and slide format you will use.

Aim to finish one minute early. Talks always run longer live, and chairs are increasingly strict about cutting speakers off.

Handling questions

Questions are where the audience engages most with your work. Prepare for them like a short exam.

  • List the five most likely questions and rehearse a 30-second answer to each. They are usually about sample size, a confounder, generalisability, a competing explanation and what you will do next.
  • Prepare backup slides after your final slide for detailed questions about methods or extra analyses.
  • Repeat or rephrase the question before answering, so the room hears it and you have a moment to think.
  • It is fine to say “I don’t know”. Follow it with how you would find out, or offer to talk afterwards.
  • If someone makes a long comment, not a question, thank them, respond briefly to the main point, and move on.

Our guide to peer reviewing a paper lists the questions reviewers ask of methods and results; the same questions come up after talks.

Online and hybrid talks

Online talks lose the room’s energy, so they need more structure. Look at the camera when you make your key points, use slightly fewer words per slide, and pause after each main result. Test screen sharing and audio beforehand, and have a PDF of your slides ready in case the software fails. If the talk is pre-recorded, record it in short sections so a mistake does not mean starting again.

Talk or poster?

A poster is not a lesser version of a talk. It suits detailed methods, many results, and one-to-one conversation with the people most interested in your work. A talk suits a single clear finding for a broad audience. If you are offered a poster, plan a two-minute spoken summary for visitors and bring a QR code linking to the paper or preprint.

After the talk: share your slides (or a link to the paper) the same day, follow up with people who asked questions, and note what confused the audience. Those questions usually point to the paragraph in your paper that needs rewriting. If the work is heading for a journal, see our guide on choosing a journal.

Frequently asked questions

How many slides do I need for a 15-minute presentation?

About 12 to 15 slides, at roughly one per minute. Use fewer if your slides contain complex figures that need time to explain. Keep backup slides for questions after your final slide.

How do I structure a research presentation?

Spend about 20% of your time on why the question matters, 20% on methods, 40% on results and 20% on conclusions and what comes next. Build the whole talk around one main message.

How do I stop being nervous before a conference talk?

Rehearsal is the most reliable cure. Practise out loud at least three times, once to an audience, and know your first two minutes almost by heart. Arrive early to check the room and equipment.

What should be on the last slide of a presentation?

Your main message, stated as a sentence, plus your contact details and a link to the paper or preprint. Leave it up during questions instead of a slide that only says thank you.

What if I do not know the answer to a question?

Say so honestly, explain how you would find out or what your data can and cannot show, and offer to continue the conversation afterwards. Audiences respect a clear “I don’t know” more than a guess.
