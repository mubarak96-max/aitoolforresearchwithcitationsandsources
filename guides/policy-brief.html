How to write a policy brief decision makers will actually read

A policy brief is research translated for someone with ten minutes and a decision to make. It is shorter than a report, more direct than a paper, and judged on whether the reader knows what to do next. Here is a structure that works.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Write for one audience and one decision. Open with a key messages box that states the problem, the evidence and your recommendation in three to five bullet points. Follow with a short problem statement, the evidence (what is known, how strong it is), the policy options with their trade-offs, and clear, actionable recommendations. Keep it to two to four pages, use plain language, cite your sources, and put methods detail in an annex or a linked report.

What a policy brief is (and is not)

A policy brief summarises evidence on a specific issue and, usually, recommends what to do about it. Its readers are ministers, civil servants, advisers, board members, NGO staff or funders. They are not specialists in your field, they read many documents, and they need to act.

It is not a shortened research paper. A paper explains how you know something; a brief explains what it means for a decision. Methods, literature and caveats still matter, but they move from the centre to the margins.

Good models exist. The UK Parliament’s Parliamentary Office of Science and Technology (POST) publishes short, balanced briefings on research topics for legislators; reading two or three is the fastest way to calibrate tone and length.

Advocacy or objective?

Before you write, decide which kind of brief you are producing.

  • Objective briefs set out the evidence and the options neutrally and let the reader choose. Parliamentary and civil service briefings are usually this type.
  • Advocacy briefs argue for a particular option. NGOs, think tanks and research groups with a clear finding often write these.

Both are legitimate. What is not legitimate is an advocacy brief disguised as an objective one. Say which you are writing, and represent the evidence for the options you reject fairly.

The structure

SectionLengthWhat it does
TitleOne lineStates the issue or the recommendation, not the research topic
Key messages3–5 bulletsThe whole brief in 30 seconds
The problem1–2 paragraphsWhy this needs attention now, with one or two numbers
The evidence3–5 paragraphsWhat is known, how strong it is, and where it is uncertain
Policy optionsA short table or listTwo to four options with benefits, costs and risks
Recommendations3–5 numbered pointsSpecific actions, who should take them, and when
SourcesShort listThe key references and a link to the full report

Writing the key messages box

Write this last but put it first. Many readers will read nothing else. Each bullet should be a complete statement a reader could repeat in a meeting.

  • Weak: “Air quality and health.”
  • Strong: “Children at schools next to main roads are exposed to markedly higher levels of nitrogen dioxide than children at schools on quieter streets.”

The strong version names who, what and how much. Replace “markedly higher” with the actual figure from your evidence.

Presenting evidence to non-specialists

Policymakers need to know how confident they can be, not how the regression was specified.

  • Lead with the finding, then the source. “Three large reviews agree that…” is more useful than a list of individual studies.
  • Say how strong the evidence is, in plain words: consistent findings from several high-quality studies; mixed findings; or early evidence from one study. Our guide to AI-assisted literature reviews covers how to grade and synthesise evidence quickly.
  • Use absolute numbers where you can. “Reduces risk from 4 in 100 to 3 in 100” is clearer than “25% relative reduction”.
  • One chart, not five. A single well-labelled figure that makes the main point is better than several that need explanation.
  • Be honest about uncertainty and say what would reduce it.

Options and recommendations

Show that you have considered alternatives. A small table with two to four options and their likely benefits, costs, risks and feasibility makes your recommendation more credible, even in an advocacy brief.

Recommendations must be actionable. Each one should say who should do what, by when, and ideally at what cost. “More research is needed” is not a recommendation unless you say what research, by whom, and what decision it would inform.

A fill-in template

[TITLE: the issue or recommendation in under 12 words]

KEY MESSAGES
• [The problem, with one number]
• [The main finding from the evidence]
• [How strong the evidence is]
• [The recommended action]

THE PROBLEM
[Who is affected, how many, why now. 100–150 words.]

WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS
[Main findings, strength of evidence, key uncertainties. 250–400 words.]

POLICY OPTIONS
Option | Benefits | Costs / risks | Feasibility

RECOMMENDATIONS
1. [Actor] should [action] by [date / milestone].
2. ...

SOURCES AND FURTHER READING
[3–8 key references; link to full report]
[Authors, organisation, date, contact]

A worked example

Here is the opening of a hypothetical brief for a city council deciding whether to close streets outside primary schools to traffic at drop-off and pick-up times. The figures are illustrative, not real data.

School streets: closing roads at the school gate cuts children’s exposure to traffic pollution

  • Around 40 of the city’s 120 primary schools sit on roads with nitrogen dioxide levels above the annual guideline.
  • Pilot closures at six schools last year reduced levels at the gate during drop-off by roughly a fifth, with no measurable increase on surrounding roads.
  • The evidence is consistent across pilots in several cities but mostly short-term; long-term health effects have not yet been measured directly.
  • Recommendation: extend timed closures to the 40 most exposed schools over two school years, with air quality monitoring at each site.

Each bullet carries one idea. The third bullet is the honest one: it tells the reader how far to trust the second. The recommendation names the scope, the timeline and the monitoring that will show whether it worked. A reader who stops here knows what is proposed and why.

The rest of the brief then expands each bullet: the problem section gives the exposure data and who it affects, the evidence section summarises the pilots and wider studies, and the options table compares timed closures with alternatives such as anti-idling enforcement or moving school entrances.

Style rules

  • Two to four pages. If it is longer, it is a report.
  • Short sentences, active voice, no jargon. The US federal plain language guidelines are a good free reference.
  • Headings that say something (“Most exposure happens at the school gate”), not labels (“Results”).
  • Bold the one sentence in each section you most want remembered.
  • Date the brief and name a contact person.
  • Cite sources, and keep the full reference list in a linked report if space is short.

Common mistakes

  • Writing for your peers. If a colleague in your field would enjoy it, a policymaker probably will not.
  • No recommendation. Even an objective brief should state what the evidence implies for each option.
  • Overclaiming. One study rarely justifies a national policy. Say what the evidence can support.
  • Wrong timing. A brief that arrives after the decision is made is wasted. Find out when the decision point is.

Getting it read: a brief is rarely read because it was published. It is read because someone sent it to the right person at the right time. Plan distribution as carefully as the writing: who needs it, through which channel, and before which meeting.

Turning a research paper into a brief

  1. Write down the one decision your findings inform and who makes it.
  2. Turn your discussion section’s implications into three to five recommendations.
  3. Replace the literature review with two or three sentences on the wider evidence. Read our guide on identifying research gaps if you need to say clearly what remains unknown.
  4. Move methods to a single sentence and a link.
  5. Ask someone outside your field to read it and tell you what they would do. If they cannot answer, revise.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a policy brief be?

Usually two to four pages, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 words. Some organisations use a one-page format. If you need more space, write a full report and keep the brief as its summary.

What is the difference between a policy brief and a white paper?

A policy brief is short and focused on a decision, with recommendations for a specific audience. A white paper is longer, explains an issue or approach in more depth, and may be aimed at a broader professional audience.

Should a policy brief include citations?

Yes. Cite the key sources for each main claim, using a short reference list or footnotes. Decision makers and their advisers need to be able to check the evidence, and citations build credibility.

What makes a good policy recommendation?

It is specific, actionable and realistic: it names who should act, what they should do, by when, and ideally what it will cost. It follows directly from the evidence presented in the brief.
