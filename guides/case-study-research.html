Case study research design: when to use it and how to do it well

A case study looks closely at one or a few real instances of something, such as an organisation, a policy, a classroom or a patient pathway, in its real setting. Done well, it explains how and why things happen. Done badly, it is a description with a literature review attached.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20266 min read
Short answer Use a case study when you want to understand how or why something happens in a real-life setting you cannot control, and the boundary between the phenomenon and its context is blurry. Define the case precisely, choose single or multiple cases for a stated reason, collect evidence from at least two or three sources, and analyse it against your research question or a set of propositions, not just by retelling events.

What a case study is, and what it is not

A case study is an in-depth investigation of a bounded system: one school, one merger, one community health programme, one software team. The “case” is the unit you study; the “study” is the systematic collection and analysis of evidence about it. Case studies can be qualitative, quantitative or mixed, though most draw heavily on interviews, documents and observation.

Two things are often confused with case studies:

  • A teaching case written for a business-school class. These are narratives designed for discussion, not research.
  • An example. Mentioning a company in a literature review does not make it a case study. A case study has its own question, data and analysis.

For a broader view of how case studies sit beside experiments, surveys and ethnography, see the AI for Research overview of types of research design.

When to choose a case study

Case studies fit questions that start with how or why, about events you cannot manipulate, in settings where context matters. They are a good choice when:

  • You want to explain a process, such as how a hospital adopted a new triage system.
  • The phenomenon is rare or new, so there are not enough cases for a survey.
  • Context is part of the answer, such as why the same policy worked in one district but not another.
  • You have privileged access to a setting that others cannot study.

They are a poor choice when you want to estimate how common something is, or test whether an intervention causes an outcome across a population. Those questions need surveys or experiments.

Types of case study design

DesignWhat it doesExample
Single, holisticStudies one case as a wholeHow one city introduced congestion charging
Single, embeddedOne case with several units of analysis inside itOne university, comparing three faculties
Multiple (comparative)Several cases chosen to compare or replicate findingsFour schools with different results from the same reform
ExploratoryMaps a new area before a larger studyEarly use of AI tools in one newsroom
ExplanatoryTests or builds an explanation of how or whyWhy a merger failed to deliver expected savings
DescriptiveGives a detailed, structured account of a caseHow a rural clinic organises community outreach

A single case is justified when it is critical (it tests a theory), extreme or unusual, typical (it represents many others), revelatory (it was previously inaccessible) or longitudinal (you can follow it over time). Multiple cases are usually stronger for explanation, because a finding that repeats across cases is harder to dismiss.

Seven steps to a rigorous case study design

1. Write a precise question

“A case study of Company X” is not a question. “How did Company X’s shift to remote work change the way new engineers learn the codebase?” is. The question tells you what evidence matters and what to leave out.

2. Define the case and its boundaries

State what is inside the case and what is not: which organisation or unit, which people, which period. Unclear boundaries are the most common weakness examiners point to.

3. State propositions or a framework

For explanatory studies, write down what you expect to find and why, based on theory or earlier research. For exploratory studies, state the framework that will guide what you look at. Either way, this keeps analysis from turning into storytelling. Our guide to the research gap helps show why this case adds something to the literature.

4. Select cases on purpose

Explain why these cases and not others. In multiple-case designs, choose cases either to replicate (you expect similar results) or to contrast (you expect different results for reasons you can predict). Convenience is a real constraint, but name it as a limitation.

5. Collect evidence from several sources

Strong case studies triangulate. Common sources include semi-structured interviews, documents and records, observation, archival data and quantitative indicators. If interviews are central, our guide to writing an interview guide covers question design and probing.

6. Keep a case study database and protocol

Record what you collected, from whom, when and how. A written protocol (questions, sources, procedures) and an organised evidence log let someone else follow your chain of evidence from data to conclusion.

7. Analyse systematically

Common techniques include pattern matching (comparing what you found with what your propositions predicted), explanation building, time-series or process tracing, and cross-case synthesis for multiple cases. For interview and document data, a coding approach such as thematic analysis gives the analysis a visible structure.

How examiners judge a case study

Case study research is often criticised as anecdotal. You answer that criticism by showing quality on four fronts:

  • Construct validity: several sources of evidence, a clear chain of evidence, and key informants reviewing a draft of the case report where appropriate.
  • Internal validity (explanatory studies): pattern matching, explanation building and dealing with rival explanations.
  • External validity: generalising to theory rather than to a population, and replication logic across multiple cases.
  • Reliability: a case study protocol and a database that would let another researcher repeat your steps.

Two widely cited open-access introductions are worth reading before you design your study: Crowe and colleagues’ “The case study approach” in BMC Medical Research Methodology (2011), and Baxter and Jack’s guide to qualitative case study methodology in The Qualitative Report (2008).

Common trap: generalising statistically from a case. A case study of one hospital cannot tell you what percentage of hospitals do something. It can tell you how a mechanism works and when it might apply elsewhere.

Writing up a case study

Most case study reports follow a familiar structure: introduction and question, literature and framework, methods (case selection, sources, analysis), the case description, analysis and findings, discussion and conclusion. Keep the description proportionate. A long narrative with a short analysis is the most common reason case study dissertations lose marks. Ask of every paragraph in the description: does the analysis use this?

Anonymise carefully. A “large bank headquartered in a small European capital” may be identifiable. Agree with participants and the organisation what can be named, and record that agreement in your ethics paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

What is case study research design?

It is a research strategy that studies one or a few bounded cases, such as an organisation, programme or event, in depth and in their real-life context. Evidence usually comes from several sources, such as interviews, documents and observation, and is analysed to explain how or why something happened.

How many cases do I need for a case study?

There is no fixed number. One case can be justified if it is critical, unusual, typical, revelatory or followed over time. Multiple-case studies often use between two and about ten cases, chosen to replicate or contrast findings. Depth matters more than count.

Is a case study qualitative or quantitative?

It can be either, or both. Many case studies rely mainly on qualitative data, such as interviews and documents, but they can include quantitative records, survey results or performance indicators as additional sources of evidence.

Can you generalise from a case study?

Not statistically to a population. Case studies generalise analytically: findings can support, refine or challenge a theory, and replication across several cases strengthens the claim that a mechanism works under similar conditions.

What is the difference between a case study and an ethnography?

Ethnography is long-term immersion in a culture or group to understand its meanings and practices, usually through participant observation. A case study is defined by its focus on a bounded case and can use many methods, including some ethnographic ones, often over a shorter period.
