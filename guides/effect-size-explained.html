Effect size explained: how big is the difference, really?

A p-value tells you whether an effect is distinguishable from zero. An effect size tells you how large it is. Readers, reviewers and meta-analysts need the second far more than the first.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer An effect size is a number that describes the magnitude of a difference or relationship. For two group means, use Cohen’s d (difference divided by the pooled SD). For relationships, use r. For binary outcomes, use a risk ratio, odds ratio or risk difference, ideally with the number needed to treat. Always report it with a confidence interval, and interpret it against what matters in your field rather than only Cohen’s “small, medium, large” labels.

Why effect sizes matter

With a large enough sample, almost any difference becomes statistically significant. A study of 20,000 students might find that a new app raises exam scores with p < 0.001, yet the gain is half a point out of 100. Without an effect size, readers cannot tell an important finding from a trivial one.

Effect sizes also make studies comparable. A meta-analysis cannot pool p-values from studies with different scales, but it can pool standardised effect sizes. And they are what you need to plan the next study: the expected effect size drives the sample size calculation.

For a clear, citable overview, see Sullivan and Feinn’s “Using effect size, or why the P value is not enough” and Lakens’ practical primer “Calculating and reporting effect sizes” in Frontiers in Psychology.

The two families of effect size

  • The d family (standardised differences): Cohen’s d, Hedges’ g, Glass’s delta. Used for comparing means between groups.
  • The r family (strength of association): Pearson’s r, R², eta squared, partial eta squared, Cramér’s V. Used for relationships and for the proportion of variance explained.

Binary outcomes have their own measures: risk difference, risk ratio, odds ratio, and the number needed to treat. Unstandardised effects, such as “4.1 points per hour of study” or “a 6 mmHg drop in blood pressure”, are effect sizes too, and often the most useful when the units mean something to readers.

Cohen’s d, worked through

Cohen’s d is the difference between two means divided by the standard deviation:

d = (mean₁ − mean₂) / pooled SD

Example 1, equal SDs. A writing course group scores a mean of 78 (SD 10); a comparison group scores 72 (SD 10). The difference is 6 points, and 6 ÷ 10 gives d = 0.60: the groups differ by six-tenths of a standard deviation.

Example 2, unequal SDs. Same means, but SDs of 8 and 12, with equal group sizes. The pooled SD is √((8² + 12²) / 2) = √((64 + 144) / 2) = √104 ≈ 10.20. Then d = 6 ÷ 10.20 ≈ 0.59.

With unequal group sizes, weight each variance by its degrees of freedom:

pooled SD = √( ((n₁ − 1)·SD₁² + (n₂ − 1)·SD₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2) )

For small samples (under about 20 per group), Cohen’s d slightly overestimates the population effect. Hedges’ g applies a small correction and is the usual choice in meta-analysis.

What d = 0.6 means in plain words

If scores are roughly normal, d = 0.6 means the average person in the course group scores higher than about 73% of the comparison group. That kind of translation, sometimes called Cohen’s U3, helps non-specialist readers far more than the bare number.

Correlation r as an effect size

Pearson’s r is already standardised: 0 means no linear relationship and ±1 a perfect one. Squaring it gives the proportion of variance shared: r = 0.30 means r² = 0.09, or 9% of the variance. Our guide to correlation vs regression works through r by hand.

You can convert between d and r when groups are of equal size: r = d / √(d² + 4). For d = 0.6, r = 0.6 / √4.36 ≈ 0.29. Conversions are handy for meta-analysis but should be stated, not hidden.

Effect sizes for yes/no outcomes

A trial gives a new reminder system to 100 patients and usual care to 100. Missed appointments: 20 in the reminder group, 30 in usual care.

Risk (usual care)30 / 1000.3030% missed
Risk difference0.30 − 0.200.1010 fewer missed per 100 patients
Risk ratio (RR)0.20 / 0.300.67A third lower risk
Odds ratio (OR)(20/80) / (30/70) = 0.25 / 0.4290.58Odds 42% lower
Number needed to treat1 / 0.1010Remind 10 patients to prevent 1 missed visit

Notice that the odds ratio (0.58) looks more impressive than the risk ratio (0.67). That gap grows as outcomes become common. Readers, and journalists, often read an odds ratio as if it were a risk ratio, so when the outcome affects more than about 10% of people, report the risk ratio or risk difference alongside it. Odds ratios remain standard in case–control studies and logistic regression, where risk ratios cannot be estimated directly.

Small, medium, large: use with care

Jacob Cohen proposed these as rough defaults for the behavioural sciences when nothing better was available, and he warned against using them mechanically. A “small” effect on mortality can be enormously important; a “large” effect on a lab task may mean little. Better anchors are the smallest effect that would matter in practice and the typical effects in your own field’s meta-analyses.

Effect size and sample size

Planning a study runs the logic backwards: choose the smallest effect worth detecting, the significance level and the power you want, then compute the sample size. For a two-group comparison at α = 0.05 and 80% power, detecting d = 0.5 needs about 64 people per group; detecting d = 0.2 needs about 394 per group. Halving the effect roughly quadruples the sample. The AI for Research team explains the trade-offs in its guide to statistical power and sample size.

Beware of “observed power”. Calculating power after the study from the effect you found adds nothing beyond the p-value. Justify sample size before data collection, and after it, report the CI.

How to report an effect size

  • Name the measure (d, g, r, RR, OR) and how it was calculated, including which SD you standardised by.
  • Give a 95% confidence interval for it.
  • Report the raw difference in real units too, when the units mean something.
  • Interpret it against a practical benchmark, not only Cohen’s labels.
  • For binary outcomes, give absolute numbers (risk difference or NNT), not only ratios.

Example: “The course group scored 6.0 points higher (95% CI 2.1 to 9.9), d = 0.60 (95% CI 0.20 to 1.00), with 50 students per group.” Then add one sentence on what that size of gain means for your readers, such as how it compares with gains reported for similar courses. For the CI side of that sentence, see our guide to p-values and confidence intervals; for how effects are combined across studies, see how to read a forest plot.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good effect size?

There is no universal answer. Cohen suggested d of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 as small, medium and large for the behavioural sciences, but what matters is the practical importance in your field. Compare your effect with the smallest effect that would change a decision and with typical effects in published meta-analyses.

How do I calculate Cohen’s d?

Subtract one group mean from the other and divide by the pooled standard deviation. With equal group sizes the pooled SD is the square root of the average of the two variances. For small samples, use Hedges’ g, which applies a small-sample correction.

What is the difference between an odds ratio and a risk ratio?

A risk ratio compares the probability of an outcome between groups; an odds ratio compares the odds. When the outcome is rare they are close, but when it is common the odds ratio is further from 1 and can exaggerate the effect if read as a risk ratio.

Do I need to report effect sizes if my result is not significant?

Yes. The effect size and its confidence interval show what the data are compatible with, and they are essential for meta-analyses. A non-significant result with a large but imprecise effect is very different from one with a precise estimate near zero.

Can I convert Cohen’s d to r?

Yes. With equal group sizes, r = d divided by the square root of (d squared plus 4). For example, d = 0.6 corresponds to r of about 0.29. State any conversions you use in your methods.
