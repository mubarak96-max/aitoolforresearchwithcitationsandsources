How to use AI for a literature review: a six-stage workflow you can defend

AI can cut the slow parts of a literature review from weeks to days. It can also fill your chapter with papers you never read. This workflow uses AI where it is strong and puts a human check at every point where it is weak.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202612 min read
Short answer Use AI to scope the question, run broad searches, find connected papers and do a first-pass summary of each source. Do the screening decisions, close reading and synthesis yourself. Keep a log of every search and every AI step, so you can describe your method in one honest paragraph.

Where AI helps and where it does not

A literature review has two kinds of work. The first is mechanical: running searches, removing duplicates, pulling key details from abstracts, formatting references. The second is judgment: deciding what counts as relevant, weighing study quality, noticing that two papers measure the same thing in different ways, and building an argument. AI is very good at the first kind and unreliable at the second.

The workflow below follows that line. Each stage says what to hand to AI, what to keep, and what to check before you move on.

Stage 1: Scope the question

A vague question produces a vague review. Start by writing your question in one sentence, then ask an AI assistant to break it into concepts and list synonyms for each. For a question like “Does remote work affect employee wellbeing?”, it should return terms such as telework, work from home, hybrid work, burnout, job satisfaction and work–life balance.

Then set boundaries yourself: years, populations, study types and languages. Write them down. These become your inclusion criteria later, and they are what an examiner will ask you to justify. If your question is still loose, a structured format such as PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) helps.

Check before moving on: can you say in one sentence what is in scope and what is not?

Stage 2: Search broadly and log everything

Run your terms in at least two scholarly indexes. A cross-disciplinary index such as OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar gives breadth. A subject index, such as PubMed for health or a discipline database your library subscribes to, gives depth.

AI research assistants are useful here because they can search several indexes at once and return cited answers you can scan quickly. A deep research report is a fast way to see the shape of a field: the main themes, the much-cited papers, the open debates. Tools such as AI for Research’s Deep Research screen hundreds of papers and read a set in full before writing a report with figures, and every claim links to its source.

Treat any report as a map, not as your review. Its job is to show you where to look.

Log each search: database, date, exact query, filters and number of results. A simple spreadsheet is enough. This log becomes the methods paragraph of your review. To write tighter queries, see our guide to Boolean search operators.

Stage 3: Snowball from the key papers

Keyword searches miss papers that use different words. Snowballing fixes that. Take your five to ten most relevant papers and look at what they cite (backward snowballing) and what cites them (forward snowballing). Repeat on new key papers until you stop finding relevant ones.

Citation mapping tools show these links as a graph, which makes clusters and bridging papers easy to see. If you have not done this before, read the AI for Research explainer on backward and forward citation searching, then try it on one paper before scaling up. Our guide to identifying a research gap shows how the same maps reveal what is missing.

Check before moving on: are the same papers appearing again and again? That is a sign your search is close to saturation.

Stage 4: Screen against your criteria

Screen titles and abstracts first, then full texts. AI can suggest a decision for each record and give a reason, which speeds up the obvious excludes. But the decision is yours. Read the reason, not just the label: AI often excludes a relevant paper because it uses an unusual term, or includes one because the abstract mentions your topic in passing.

For a formal systematic review you need two reviewers, recorded conflicts and a PRISMA flow diagram. Our systematic review software comparison covers the tools built for that. For a narrative review in a thesis, a single screener with clear, written criteria is usually acceptable, but check with your supervisor.

Stage 5: Read in two passes

You cannot read 150 papers closely. You can read 150 abstracts, 40 summaries and 15 full papers. Split your reading:

  • First pass (AI-assisted): for each included paper, extract the question, design, sample, main finding and limitations into a table. AI is good at this, especially when it answers from the PDF and quotes the page. Spot-check one row in five against the paper.
  • Second pass (yours): read the 10 to 20 papers that carry your argument, in full, with notes. These are the papers an examiner will ask about.

Our guide to reading research papers faster with AI has prompts for the first pass and a checklist for the second.

Build a literature matrix

A matrix is a table with one row per paper and one column per theme or variable. It turns a pile of summaries into something you can compare. Patterns appear fast: three studies use the same scale, two find opposite results, nobody studied a certain group. Those patterns are your synthesis. Our literature synthesis matrix guide has a template and worked example.

Paper | Design | Sample | Measure of wellbeing | Finding | Limitation
Smith 2021 | Cohort | 1,200 UK office staff | WEMWBS | Small improvement | Self-report
Lee 2023 | RCT | 340 call-centre staff | Burnout (MBI) | No effect | 6-month follow-up

Stage 6: Synthesise and write

A literature review is not a list of summaries. It is an argument about what is known, how well it is known, and what is not known yet. Organise by theme, method or debate, not paper by paper.

AI can help you test an outline: ask it to group your matrix rows into themes, then compare its grouping with yours. Where they differ, think about why. It can also draft a paragraph from papers you have already read, which you then rewrite in your own voice. The AI for Research literature review generator builds a first draft from real, cited papers, which is a useful starting point to argue with. For the craft side, their guide on writing a literature review step by step is thorough.

Final check: pick any sentence that cites a paper. Could you explain what that paper did and found without looking? If not, read it again.

Five mistakes that weaken AI-assisted reviews

  1. Starting with a report instead of a question. A deep research report on a vague topic gives you a vague map. Sharpen the question first.
  2. Citing the summary, not the paper. If a claim came from an AI summary, open the paper before you cite it. Summaries flatten caveats and sometimes get the direction of an effect wrong.
  3. One search, one database. AI tools search large indexes, but coverage differs by field. Always add a subject database your discipline relies on.
  4. No log. Without a search and screening log, you cannot write your method or repeat the review later. Keep it from day one.
  5. Losing your voice. Reviews written from AI drafts often read as a list of findings with no argument. Write the synthesis paragraphs yourself, then use AI to check them for unsupported claims.

How to describe your AI use

Write a short, factual statement in your methods section or acknowledgements. Name the tool, what you used it for, and how you checked its output. For example:

“AI tools were used to suggest search terms, run initial searches and produce first-pass summaries of included studies. All screening decisions were made by the author. All cited studies were read by the author, and summaries were checked against the original papers.”

Check your rules: policies vary by university, course and journal, and some ban AI-generated text in assessed work. Our guide to AI rules in academic writing explains the common positions.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI write my literature review for me?

AI can draft a structured overview from real papers, but it cannot make the judgments a literature review is marked on: which studies matter, how strong they are, and where they disagree. Use an AI draft as a map of the field, then read the key papers and write the synthesis yourself.

Is it allowed to use AI for a literature review?

Most universities allow AI for finding and summarising sources, and many restrict AI-written text in submitted work. Check your course or journal policy, keep a record of how you used AI, and disclose it where required.

Which AI is best for a literature review?

Choose a tool that searches scholarly indexes and cites only retrieved papers, lets you filter by year and source type, answers from your own PDFs with page numbers, and exports into your reference manager. General chatbots without search are the riskiest choice because they can invent references.

How long does an AI-assisted literature review take?

AI shortens searching and first-pass reading from weeks to days. Reading key papers closely, synthesising and writing still take most of the time. For a thesis chapter, plan on several weeks even with good tools.
