How to paraphrase without plagiarizing: six steps and real examples

Changing a few words in someone else’s sentence is not paraphrasing. It is the most common form of accidental plagiarism. Here is a method that produces genuinely new wording, examples of what goes wrong, and the rules for using AI to help.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Read the passage until you understand it, close the source, and write the idea from memory in your own sentence structure and words. Then compare with the original, change anything still too close, keep technical terms that have no synonym, and cite the source. A paraphrase still needs a citation, because the idea is not yours.

What counts as plagiarism

Plagiarism is presenting someone else’s words or ideas as your own. Most students know that copying a paragraph without quotation marks is plagiarism. Fewer realise that the following also count:

  • Patchwriting: keeping the original sentence structure and swapping in synonyms.
  • Uncited paraphrase: rewording an idea well but not saying where it came from.
  • Mosaic plagiarism: stitching phrases from several sources into one paragraph.
  • Self-plagiarism: reusing your own earlier work without permission or citation, which many universities and journals treat as misconduct.

Similarity checkers catch the first and third easily. Instructors catch the second because the style suddenly changes. The fix for all of them is the same: understand the idea, write it fresh, and cite it.

The six-step method

  1. Read for meaning. Read the passage, then the paragraph around it. You cannot paraphrase what you do not understand, and a misunderstood paraphrase misrepresents the author.
  2. Note the core idea in a few words. Write three to six keywords or a short phrase on paper. This is the idea stripped of the author’s wording.
  3. Close the source. Put the paper away or switch windows. This is the single most effective step. If the original is in front of you, its structure will leak into yours.
  4. Write from your notes. Explain the idea as you would to a classmate. Start the sentence from a different place than the original, for example with the result rather than the method.
  5. Compare and fix. Reopen the source. Underline any run of three or more words that match. Rewrite those parts. Check that you have kept the meaning, including any limits or hedges the author used.
  6. Cite it. Add the in-text citation and the reference. Include a page number if your style asks for one for paraphrases.

Technical terms are the exception to rewording. “Randomised controlled trial”, “photosynthesis” and “confidence interval” have no honest synonym. Keep them; change the sentence around them.

Good and bad examples

Original passage (invented for this guide):

Students who slept fewer than six hours on the night before an exam performed significantly worse on recall questions, although their performance on problem-solving questions was largely unaffected.
VersionTextVerdict
PatchwritingStudents who got under six hours of sleep the night before a test did much worse on memory questions, but their problem-solving performance was mostly unaffected.Plagiarism. Same structure, synonyms swapped.
Changed meaningLack of sleep before exams harms student performance (Author, 2024).Cited but wrong. It drops the key distinction between question types.
Good paraphraseShort sleep before an exam seems to hurt memory more than reasoning: in one study, students with under six hours scored lower on recall items but about the same on problem-solving items (Author, 2024).New structure, same meaning, cited.

Notice that the good version keeps “six hours” and “problem-solving”. Precise numbers and terms are facts, not style. It also leads with the idea, which is how you would actually use the source in an argument.

Paraphrase, summary or quotation?

These three ways of using a source do different jobs:

  • Paraphrase restates a specific passage in your words at roughly the same length. Use it for a key finding or argument.
  • Summary condenses a longer section or a whole paper into a sentence or two. Use it to place a source in context.
  • Quotation reproduces exact words in quotation marks. Use it sparingly, as described below.

In most academic writing, summary and paraphrase should do most of the work. A paper made of quotations reads like a collage and shows little of your own understanding.

When to quote instead

Quote when the exact words matter:

  • A definition you are adopting or arguing against.
  • A phrase that is famous or that you will analyse closely, common in humanities writing.
  • Wording so precise that a paraphrase would distort it, such as a legal text or a policy statement.
  • A claim you want to show the author made in their own words, often because it is surprising or controversial.

Keep quotations short, introduce them with the author’s name, and always follow them with your own comment. For more help, the Purdue OWL and the UNC Writing Center both publish clear, free guidance on quoting and paraphrasing.

AI paraphrasing tools

AI paraphrasers rewrite text on request. They are useful for one honest job: helping you rephrase your own clumsy sentences. They are risky for a second job: rewording a source so it no longer matches. That second use is still plagiarism if you do not cite the source, and many universities treat undisclosed AI rewriting of others’ work as misconduct.

Three rules keep you safe:

  1. Start from your understanding, not the source. Write your own rough paraphrase first, then let a tool smooth it.
  2. Always cite the idea. A reworded idea is still someone else’s idea.
  3. Check the meaning. AI rewrites often drop hedges such as “may” or “in this sample”, which turns a careful claim into an overclaim.

How paraphrasing differs by field

The same principles apply everywhere, but disciplines use sources differently.

  • Sciences and medicine paraphrase almost everything and rarely quote. Findings are summarised with numbers and cited, often by number in Vancouver or IEEE style. Precision matters more than elegance: keep the exact values and the direction of an effect.
  • Social sciences mostly paraphrase too, but quote definitions and key concepts. APA style encourages page numbers for paraphrases of specific passages.
  • Humanities quote more often, because the exact wording of a text is often what is being analysed. Paraphrase is still used for secondary sources and for summarising arguments.
  • Law and policy quote statutes and official text exactly; paraphrasing a legal provision can change its meaning.

A five-minute practice exercise

Paraphrasing gets faster with practice. Try this with any paper in your field:

  1. Pick one paragraph from the results or discussion.
  2. Read it twice, then close the paper and set a timer for two minutes.
  3. Write the main point in one or two sentences without looking.
  4. Reopen the paper, underline matching phrases in your version, and rewrite them.
  5. Check one thing only: would the author agree that your sentence says what they meant?

After a dozen rounds, most people find they stop reaching for the author’s phrasing at all.

A related habit helps even more: take notes in your own words from the start. If your reading notes are already paraphrased, with the source and page beside each one, you will never have to paraphrase under deadline pressure, and you will not mistake a copied phrase in your notes for your own wording months later.

A self-check before you submit

  • Every paraphrase has an in-text citation and a reference.
  • No run of three or more words matches the source, apart from technical terms and names.
  • Sentence structure differs from the original.
  • The meaning, including hedges and limits, is unchanged.
  • Quotations are short, exact, in quotation marks and introduced.
  • You could explain each paraphrased idea out loud without the source.
  • Any AI help is disclosed if your course or journal requires it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to cite a paraphrase?

Yes. Paraphrasing changes the wording, not the ownership of the idea. Every paraphrase needs an in-text citation and a reference list entry. Some styles, such as APA, encourage a page number for paraphrases too.

How many words can I copy without quoting?

There is no safe number. As a rule of thumb, any run of three or more distinctive words taken from a source should either be rewritten or put in quotation marks. Technical terms and proper names are exceptions.

Is using a paraphrasing tool plagiarism?

Using a tool to reword a source and presenting it as your own thinking without a citation is plagiarism. Using a tool to improve your own sentences is usually acceptable, but many courses require you to disclose AI help. Always cite the original source of the idea.

Can a similarity checker detect paraphrasing?

Similarity checkers are good at finding close paraphrase and patchwriting, because many words and their order still match. They are weaker at detecting heavily reworded text, but instructors often notice a change in style or a claim that does not fit the argument.
