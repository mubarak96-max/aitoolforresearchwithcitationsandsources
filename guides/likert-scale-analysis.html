How to analyze Likert scale data without starting a methods argument

Likert data starts more arguments between supervisors and students than almost any other kind. Most of them go away once you decide one thing first: are you analysing single items or a scale built from several items?

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Treat a single Likert item as ordinal: report the median and the percentage in each category, and compare groups with Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis or Wilcoxon tests. Treat a multi-item scale score (the sum or mean of several items that measure one idea) as approximately interval once its reliability is acceptable: report the mean and SD, and use t-tests, ANOVA or regression. Say which choice you made and why.

Likert items and Likert scales are not the same thing

A Likert item is one statement with ordered response options, such as “The feedback I received was useful”, answered from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A Likert scale, in Rensis Likert’s original sense, is a set of such items that together measure one construct, like “satisfaction with feedback”. You add or average the items into one score per respondent.

The distinction matters because the two behave differently:

  • A single item has only five (or seven) possible values. The distance between “agree” and “strongly agree” is not necessarily the same as between “neutral” and “agree”. That makes it ordinal.
  • A scale score from, say, eight items can take dozens of values and tends to be roughly continuous. Many methodologists accept treating it as interval data, especially with a reasonable sample size.

Sullivan and Artino’s widely cited guide in the Journal of Graduate Medical Education, “Analyzing and interpreting data from Likert-type scales”, makes the same split and is a good reference to cite when an examiner asks why you used parametric tests.

Step 1: prepare and code the data

  1. Code responses as numbers in a consistent direction: 1 = strongly disagree up to 5 = strongly agree.
  2. Reverse-code negatively worded items. If an item reads “I found the feedback confusing”, agreement means lower satisfaction. On a 1–5 scale the reversed score is 6 minus the original (on a 1–7 scale, 8 minus the original).
  3. Decide how to handle “don’t know” or “not applicable”. These are not the midpoint. Code them as missing, not as 3.
  4. Check missing data per item and per respondent. A common rule is to compute a scale score only if a respondent answered most items (for example at least 80%), and to average the answered items rather than summing, so missing items do not drag the score down.

Step 2: describe single items properly

For each item, report the distribution, not just one number. Take ten responses to the feedback item:

5, 4, 4, 3, 5, 2, 4, 4, 5, 3
Response12345
Count01243
Percent0%10%20%40%30%

Sorted, the values are 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, so the median is 4 (the average of the 5th and 6th values, both 4) and the mode is also 4. Seventy percent chose 4 or 5. The mean is 39 ÷ 10 = 3.9. Reporting “median 4, 70% agree or strongly agree” is clearer to a reader than “mean 3.9”, and it does not assume equal spacing between categories.

For charts, a diverging stacked bar (disagreement to the left of a centre line, agreement to the right) shows many items at once far better than a column of means.

Step 3: check reliability before you build a scale score

Adding items together only makes sense if they measure the same thing. The usual check is Cronbach’s alpha:

α = k / (k − 1) × (1 − Σ item variances / variance of total score)

Suppose a 4-item scale has item variances that sum to 3.2, and the variance of the total score is 9.6. Then α = 4/3 × (1 − 3.2/9.6) = 1.333 × 0.667 ≈ 0.89.

Rough conventions: around 0.70 is often treated as acceptable for research use, 0.80 or above as good. Very high values (above about 0.95) can mean items are near-duplicates. Alpha also rises with the number of items, so a long scale can look reliable while still mixing two constructs. If you are developing a new scale, add an exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis rather than relying on alpha alone.

Using an established scale? Use its published scoring rules, including which items are reversed and whether it is summed or averaged. Report alpha in your own sample anyway, because reliability is a property of scores in a sample, not of the questionnaire.

Step 4: choose the test

Match the test to the type of variable and the design. This table covers most survey projects:

QuestionSingle item (ordinal)Scale score (treated as interval)
Do two independent groups differ?Mann–Whitney UIndependent t-test (Welch)
Do three or more groups differ?Kruskal–Wallis, then pairwise Dunn testsOne-way ANOVA, then post hoc tests
Did the same people change (before vs after)?Wilcoxon signed-rankPaired t-test
Are two variables related?Spearman’s rhoPearson’s r
What predicts the outcome?Ordinal (proportional odds) logistic regressionLinear regression
Is agreement linked to a category?Chi-square on collapsed categories (e.g. agree vs not)—

Two practical notes. First, Welch’s t-test is a safer default than the classic Student’s version because it does not assume equal variances. Second, collapsing five categories into two (“agree” vs “not agree”) throws information away; do it for a clear headline, not as your main analysis.

If you are unsure which test fits, the AI for Research team keeps a longer decision guide to statistical tests, and the UCLA statistical consulting group’s “What statistical analysis should I use?” table shows the same tests with code in several packages.

The “can I use the mean?” debate, settled for your project

You will find strong opinions on both sides. Here is a position you can defend:

  • Single items: lead with medians and distributions. If you also show means for comparison across many items, say so and do not over-interpret small differences such as 3.8 vs 3.9.
  • Scale scores: means, SDs and parametric tests are standard practice, particularly with acceptable alpha and samples of about 30 or more per group. Check for floor or ceiling effects (many people at the minimum or maximum), which distort means.
  • When in doubt: run both the parametric and non-parametric test. If they agree, report one and mention the other in a footnote. If they disagree, report both and discuss why.

Step 5: report it so a reader can check it

A good results paragraph names the measure, the reliability, the statistic and an effect size. For example:

Satisfaction with feedback (four items, α = .89) was higher among students who received written comments (M = 4.1, SD = 0.6) than among those who received grades only (M = 3.5, SD = 0.8), Welch’s t(53.8) = 3.29, p = .002, d = 0.85.

(Those numbers are consistent for 30 students per group: the difference of 0.6 divided by its standard error of about 0.18 gives t ≈ 3.29, and 0.6 divided by the pooled SD of about 0.71 gives d ≈ 0.85.)

For a single item, report the median, the interquartile range or category percentages, and the non-parametric test with its effect size (for Mann–Whitney, the rank-biserial correlation or r = Z/√N). Our guide to effect sizes explains which one to use, and p-values vs confidence intervals covers how to word the uncertainty.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting to reverse-code negatively worded items before computing alpha or totals.
  • Coding “not applicable” as the scale midpoint.
  • Running a separate t-test on each of 30 items without correcting for multiple comparisons.
  • Building a scale from items that were never designed to go together, then reporting a low alpha as a “limitation”.
  • Reporting only means, so readers cannot see that responses were polarised (many 1s and 5s).
  • Claiming a cause from a cross-sectional survey: “students who got written comments were more satisfied” is fine; “written comments increased satisfaction” needs a design that supports it.

If you are designing the questionnaire now rather than analysing it, plan the analysis at the same time. It is far easier to add a reverse-coded item or a clear midpoint label before you collect the data than to explain its absence afterwards. Frequently asked questions

Is Likert scale data ordinal or interval?

A single Likert item is ordinal: the categories are ordered but the gaps between them are not guaranteed to be equal. A scale score made by summing or averaging several reliable items is commonly treated as approximately interval, which is why t-tests and ANOVA are standard for scale scores.

Should I use the mean or median for Likert data?

Use the median and category percentages for single items. Use the mean and standard deviation for multi-item scale scores. If you report means for single items, say that you are doing so for comparison and avoid interpreting small differences.

What test do I use to compare two groups on a Likert item?

Use the Mann–Whitney U test for two independent groups and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the same people measured twice. For a multi-item scale score, an independent or paired t-test is usually appropriate.

What is a good Cronbach’s alpha for a Likert scale?

Around 0.70 is often treated as the minimum for research use and 0.80 or above as good. Values above about 0.95 can mean the items are redundant. Report alpha from your own sample, not only the value published for the questionnaire.

Can I run a regression with Likert data?

Yes. Use linear regression when the outcome is a multi-item scale score, and ordinal logistic regression when the outcome is a single Likert item. Likert predictors are often entered as scale scores, or as categories if there are few of them.
