How to write a budget justification for a grant proposal

Reviewers rarely reject a strong project because of its budget, but a vague or padded budget raises doubts about everything else. A good justification shows that each cost is necessary, reasonable and tied to a specific task in your work plan.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer For every budget line, say what it is, how you calculated it, and why the project needs it, linking it to a specific aim or task. Group costs in the funder’s categories (usually personnel, equipment, travel, materials and supplies, participant costs, other direct costs and indirect costs). Show the arithmetic, use your institution’s official rates, follow the funder’s rules on what is allowed, and make sure the numbers match the budget table exactly.

What a budget justification is for

The budget table says how much. The justification says why. Reviewers use it to judge whether the plan is realistic: whether you have enough people and time to do what you propose, and whether you are asking for things you do not need. Grants officers use it to check that each cost is allowed under the funder’s rules.

For US federal awards, the principles come from the Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR Part 200, which says costs must be allowable (permitted by the rules and the award), allocable (benefit this project in proportion to what is charged) and reasonable (what a prudent person would pay). Most funders elsewhere apply similar tests even if they use different words. Write every line so it obviously passes all three.

Before you start: three documents

  1. The funder’s guidance. Read the call text and the funder’s application guide for eligible and ineligible costs, caps on salaries or equipment, and required format. For NIH applications, the NIH grants website publishes the application guide and budget instructions.
  2. Your institution’s rates. Salary scales, fringe benefit rates, indirect (overhead) rate and travel policy. Your research office or grants officer holds these; using unofficial numbers is one of the most common reasons budgets are sent back.
  3. Your work plan. Every cost should connect to a task in your timeline. If a cost does not support any task, it should not be in the budget.

How to justify each category

Personnel

Usually the largest part of a research budget. For each person, give the role, the effort (as a percentage of full time or as person-months per year), the salary basis and what they will do.

Postdoctoral researcher (TBN), 100% effort, years 1–3.
Salary of $[X] in year 1 per the institutional scale, with a [3]%
annual increase, plus fringe benefits at the institutional rate of
[Y]%. The postdoc will run the field data collection (Aim 1), lead
the statistical analysis (Aim 2) and draft two manuscripts.

Reviewers check that effort matches responsibility. A principal investigator at 2% effort leading three aims looks unrealistic; a research assistant at 100% with no clear tasks looks padded.

Equipment

Name the item, give a quote or price source, and explain why the project needs it and why existing institutional equipment cannot be used. Funders define equipment by a cost threshold and useful life; items below the threshold usually go under supplies.

Travel

Separate fieldwork travel from conference travel. For each trip, state the purpose, destination, number of travellers, number of days and the basis for the cost (airfare estimate, per diem rate, accommodation). Link conference travel to dissemination of specific results.

Materials and supplies

Group small items into sensible categories (laboratory consumables, survey software licences, field supplies) and give the basis for the estimate, such as cost per sample multiplied by the number of samples.

Participant costs

Incentives, travel reimbursement or refreshments for study participants. Give the amount per participant and the number of participants, and confirm that the incentive was approved or will be reviewed by your ethics committee.

Other direct costs

Publication fees, transcription, data storage, consultants, subcontracts, translation, and access to data sets. For open access fees, give the expected number of papers and an estimated fee per paper. For consultants and subcontracts, name the organisation, the work and the basis for the rate.

Indirect costs

Indirect costs (also called overhead or facilities and administrative costs) cover shared institutional expenses. Usually you only state the rate and the base it applies to; your research office will tell you which. Check whether the funder caps or excludes indirect costs.

Grant writing

Multi-year budgets, cost sharing and collaborators

Year-by-year changes

If costs change between years, say why. Equipment usually falls in year 1; participant costs peak during data collection; publication and conference costs rise towards the end. A reviewer who sees a jump in year 3 should find the reason in one sentence: “Year 3 travel increases because the team presents final results at two international conferences.” Apply salary increments and inflation at the rates your institution specifies, and state them once at the top.

Cost sharing

Some funders require or welcome contributions from your institution or partners, such as staff time, equipment use or cash. Describe each contribution, its value and how it was calculated, and confirm it is committed in writing. Do not offer cost sharing the funder does not ask for without advice from your research office, because it can become a binding obligation.

Partners and subawards

For collaborators at other institutions, each partner usually prepares its own budget and justification under its own rates. Explain in your justification what each partner does, why they are needed, and how their costs relate to their tasks. Collect partner budgets early; they are the most common cause of last-minute delays.

Mistakes that get budgets cut

  • Numbers in the justification that do not match the budget table.
  • Round lump sums (“Supplies: $10,000”) with no calculation.
  • Costs that appear nowhere in the work plan.
  • Effort levels that do not match responsibilities.
  • Asking for equipment the institution already has, without explaining why it cannot be used.
  • Ineligible costs, such as items the funder explicitly excludes.
  • No inflation or salary increments in multi-year budgets.
  • Leaving the budget to the last day, so the research office cannot check it before the deadline.

Neither padded nor too lean

New applicants often under-budget, hoping a cheaper project looks more fundable. It usually does the opposite: reviewers notice when a project cannot be done with the people and time requested. Budget what the work genuinely needs, explain it, and let reviewers suggest cuts if they think you can do it for less.

If the funder has a hard cap, adjust the scope instead of squeezing the costs. Drop an aim, reduce the sample with a power calculation to justify it, or shorten the timeline. A smaller, well-funded project beats a large, underfunded one.

Work with your research office early. Grants officers know the funder’s rules and your institution’s rates, and they often have to approve the budget before submission. Send them a draft at least two weeks before the deadline.

How the budget fits the rest of the proposal

Reviewers read the budget alongside the aims, methods and timeline. Make cross-checking easy: use the same aim numbers and task names in all sections, and make sure the people named in the budget are the people named in the work plan. Frequently asked questions

What is a budget justification in a grant proposal?

It is the narrative that explains each cost in the budget: what it is, how it was calculated, and why the project needs it. It lets reviewers judge whether the budget is realistic and grants officers check that costs are allowed.

How long should a budget justification be?

It depends on the funder and the size of the budget. Some funders set a page limit; others expect one to three pages for a typical project. Every line needs a calculation and a purpose, however short.

What are indirect costs in a grant?

Indirect costs, also called overhead or facilities and administrative costs, cover shared institutional expenses such as buildings, utilities and administration. They are usually charged at a negotiated rate on a defined base. Check whether your funder limits them.

What costs are usually not allowed in grants?

It varies by funder, but common exclusions include entertainment, alcohol, general office equipment for non-project use, and costs incurred before the grant starts. Always check the funder’s list of eligible and ineligible costs.

Can I use AI to write a grant budget justification?

Some funders allow AI help with drafting, while others restrict it, especially for reviewers. If allowed, use it to structure the narrative, but always enter the official rates and calculations yourself and have your research office check them.
