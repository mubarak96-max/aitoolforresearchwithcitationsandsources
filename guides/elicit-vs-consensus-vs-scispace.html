Elicit vs Consensus vs SciSpace: which AI research tool for which job

These three tools are often compared as if they were rivals. They are not, quite. Each was built around a different research job, and the right choice depends on what you need to hand in at the end.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202611 min read
Short answer Use Consensus for a fast, cited answer to a yes-or-no evidence question. Use Elicit when your output is a table: data extracted from many papers, or records screened for a review. Use SciSpace to read and understand individual papers. If the research has to end in a written document, add a workspace that writes with live citations.

A note on how we compared

Features and prices in this space change every few months. Rather than freeze a feature list that will be out of date by spring, we compare the tools by the job each was designed for, describe the strengths that have stayed stable, and give you a short test to run yourself. Check each vendor’s current pricing page before you pay; all three have had free tiers with limits as of late 2026.

Consensus: fast answers to evidence questions

Consensus is built around a single question: what does the research say about X? You type something like “Does creatine improve cognitive performance?” and get a short synthesis, a list of studies with key findings, and, for yes-or-no questions, an indicator of how many studies lean each way.

Where it wins

  • Speed. For a quick evidence check it is one of the fastest tools to a useful answer.
  • Framing. Showing how studies split pushes you away from cherry-picking a single result.
  • Accessibility. Non-specialists, such as journalists, clinicians and policy staff, find it easy to use.

Where it stops

  • It is weaker on open “how” and “why” questions that have no yes-or-no shape.
  • A count of studies is not a weighting of evidence. Three small observational studies do not outweigh one large trial. Read the designs, and use a critical appraisal checklist on the studies that matter.
  • It is not a writing or review environment. The answer is a starting point, not a section of your paper.

For a longer look, see the AI for Research team’s honest review of Consensus, and their page on alternatives to Consensus for longer research.

Elicit: tables, extraction and screening

Elicit treats papers as rows and the things you want to know as columns. Ask a question, get a list of relevant papers, then add columns such as “sample size”, “intervention”, “main outcome” or “study design”. It reads the papers and fills in the cells, with the supporting quote for each. Its paid plans add a workflow for systematic review screening and longer research reports.

Where it wins

  • Structured extraction across many papers. This is its core strength and still the reason most researchers use it.
  • Traceability. Each extracted value links back to the passage it came from.
  • Scale. Teams doing evidence synthesis can process far more papers than by hand.

Where it stops

  • Extraction from tables, figures and supplementary files can miss or misread values. Spot-check a sample of cells against the PDFs.
  • The heavier review features sit on paid plans, which can be expensive for individual students.
  • Like Consensus, it does not take you into drafting and formatting the final document.

Read more in the Elicit review covering features, pricing and limits. If your main need is screening, our systematic review software comparison puts Elicit beside Covidence, Rayyan and others.

SciSpace: reading and understanding papers

SciSpace started as a tool to help people understand research papers, and that is still where it shines. Open a PDF, highlight a dense paragraph, an equation or a table, and ask for an explanation. It has grown a broad set of tools around that core, including literature search, a literature review table and writing aids.

Where it wins

  • Explaining difficult papers, especially outside your field or in a second language.
  • Follow-up questions about a specific passage while you read.
  • A wide toolkit for students who want many small helpers in one place.

Where it stops

  • Breadth comes at the cost of depth: some tools in the suite feel lighter than the specialists.
  • Explanations of a passage are paraphrase, not verification. Check numbers against the original.
  • For formal reviews you will still need documented searches and screening software, to the standard set out in the Cochrane Handbook.

The SciSpace alternative comparison covers where it fits against end-to-end tools.

Run this 30-minute test yourself

Your field matters more than any review. Use the same three prompts in each tool and compare:

  1. A yes-or-no question you know the answer to, for example a well-studied intervention in your area.
  2. An extraction task: “List the sample size and design of the main studies on [topic] since 2020.”
  3. A PDF question: upload a paper you know and ask for the exclusion criteria and the main effect size, with the page.

For each, open every citation and check it. Our 9-test checklist for AI research assistants has a scorecard you can reuse.

The gap all three share: writing

Most research ends in a document: a literature review chapter, a proposal, a manuscript. None of these three tools is primarily a place to write that document with live citations in your required style, and moving results from a table or chat into Word is where many errors creep in. That is why many researchers pair one of them with a writing workspace.

Using two tools together

In practice, many researchers do not pick one tool. They use two, each for what it does best, and move between them at clear handover points. Three combinations work well:

  • Consensus, then a writing workspace. Use Consensus to get a quick sense of whether the evidence leans one way, then do the proper search, reading and writing elsewhere. Do not cite the Consensus summary itself; cite the papers behind it, after reading them.
  • Elicit for extraction, then a writing workspace for synthesis. Build your extraction table in Elicit, export it, and write the synthesis in a tool where citations stay linked to the papers. This is a common pattern for evidence reviews and literature review chapters.
  • SciSpace while reading, a reference manager for keeping. Use SciSpace to understand difficult papers, but keep your master list of sources in one library, such as Zotero or a research workspace, so nothing gets lost between tools.

The weak point in every combination is the handover. When you copy a finding from one tool to another, you can lose the link to the exact passage it came from. Two habits help. First, always carry the DOI with the finding, not just the author and year; a Crossref lookup will recover the full record later. Second, before you submit, re-open the passage behind every key claim and check it still says what your sentence says.

A note on cost

Running two paid tools adds up quickly for a student. Start every tool on its free plan, run the test above, and only upgrade the one you use daily. If one workspace covers search, cited answers, reading and writing well enough for your field, a single subscription is often the cheaper and less error-prone choice. Compare plan limits on the jobs you actually do, such as reports per month or papers screened, rather than on headline features.

Which should you pick?

  • You need a quick evidence check: Consensus.
  • You need a data table across dozens or hundreds of papers: Elicit.
  • You struggle to understand the papers you are reading: SciSpace.
  • You need to turn reading into a cited, formatted document: a writing workspace such as AI for Research, plus one of the above if its specialism matters to you.

Whichever tool you choose: a real citation can still be used for a claim the paper does not make. Read our guide to spotting hallucinated and misused citations before you rely on any AI answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is Elicit better than Consensus?

For different jobs. Elicit is stronger when the output is a table: extracting the same data points from many papers, or screening records for a review. Consensus is stronger for a quick, cited answer to a yes-or-no question and a sense of how studies split.

Is SciSpace good for literature reviews?

SciSpace is useful for the reading side of a literature review: understanding papers, asking questions of PDFs and building a first list of sources. For a formal systematic review you will still want dedicated screening software and a documented search strategy.

Are Elicit, Consensus and SciSpace free?

All three have had free plans with usage limits, and paid plans for heavier use. Limits and prices change often, so check each tool's pricing page before you commit.

Which tool is best for writing a thesis?

None of the three is built mainly as a writing environment. If your goal is a thesis chapter or manuscript, consider a workspace that combines cited search with an editor, citation styles and export, such as AI for Research, and keep using Elicit or Consensus for the jobs they do best.

Do these tools make up citations?

All three retrieve papers from real indexes rather than inventing references, which makes fabricated citations much rarer than with a general chatbot. The risk that remains is a real paper cited for a claim it does not quite make, so read the cited passages.
