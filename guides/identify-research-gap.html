How to identify a research gap: five methods that hold up in a viva

“There is limited research on this topic” is the weakest sentence in most proposals. A strong gap is specific, sourced and worth filling. These five methods help you find one, and the test at the end tells you whether it will survive an examiner.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202611 min read
Short answer Read the limitations and future-work sections of recent papers, look for populations, settings or methods that are missing from the literature, map how papers cite each other to see thin areas, and look for results that conflict. Then confirm the gap with a systematic search and write it as a specific, cited statement.

The five kinds of research gap

Naming the type of gap helps you describe it precisely and choose the right method to fill it.

TypeWhat is missingExample
Evidence gapResults conflict or are inconclusiveStudies on a teaching method report opposite effects
Population gapA group has not been studiedFindings exist for adults but not adolescents
Context gapA setting or region is missingEvidence comes only from high-income countries
Methods gapOnly weak or one kind of design has been usedOnly cross-sectional surveys, no longitudinal data
Theory gapNo framework explains the findingsEffects are known but the mechanism is not

Method 1: Mine limitations and future-work sections

Authors tell you where their work stops. Take 15 to 20 recent, well-cited papers on your topic and copy every sentence from their limitations and “future research” sections into one document. Then group them. If five papers say “our sample was limited to urban schools”, you have found a context gap, and you have five citations to support it.

This is the method examiners trust most, because the gap comes from the field itself, not from your impression of it.

Method 2: Read recent reviews

Systematic reviews, scoping reviews and meta-analyses usually end with a list of what is not yet known. A review from the last three to five years saves you weeks, because someone has already searched the field. Searching PubMed with the “Systematic review” filter, or Europe PMC, finds them quickly in health and life sciences. Check whether newer studies have filled the gaps it lists, then use the ones still open.

If there is no recent review on your topic, that itself may be a gap. A scoping review is a legitimate first study for a thesis; our systematic review software guide covers the tools that make one manageable.

Method 3: Build a literature matrix

Make a table with one row per study and columns for population, setting, design, measures and main finding. Fill it for 30 to 50 studies. Empty cells and repeated values show you the gaps directly:

  • Every row says “USA” or “UK”: a context gap.
  • Every design is cross-sectional: a methods gap.
  • No row includes older adults: a population gap.
  • The findings column alternates between positive and null: an evidence gap.

AI tools can fill a first draft of the matrix from abstracts or PDFs quickly. Check a sample of rows against the papers, because extraction errors create false gaps. Our AI literature review workflow shows how to build the matrix safely, and the literature synthesis matrix guide has a ready-made template.

Method 4: Map the citation network

Papers that cite each other form clusters. A citation map shows you dense areas (well studied), thin areas, and pairs of clusters that rarely cite each other. Those bridges are often where new questions live: two communities working on related problems with different methods, unaware of each other.

Start from three to five key papers and expand. Open citation data from OpenAlex covers most fields if you want to explore the links yourself. Look for:

  • Clusters that stop growing after a certain year, which may signal an abandoned or settled question.
  • Highly cited old papers with few recent follow-ups.
  • Clusters in neighbouring fields that solve a similar problem.

The Citation Map in AI for Research builds this graph from a starting paper, and its research landscape view shows how topics in a field have grown or faded over time, which makes thin areas easy to spot.

Method 5: Look for conflict

Contradictory findings are among the most valuable gaps, because resolving them moves a field forward. When two studies disagree, compare their populations, measures, designs and analysis. Often the difference explains the conflict, and testing that explanation is a study in itself.

Ask your supervisor and colleagues too. People in a field know which debates are live, which questions everyone avoids, and which results nobody believes. Conference talks and recent preprints show where the field is moving before it reaches journals.

A worked example, start to finish

Here is how the five methods combine on a real-sounding topic: peer tutoring and maths anxiety in secondary schools.

  1. Limitations sections. Of 18 recent studies, seven note that follow-up ended at the end of the school term, and five note that samples came from a single school. Two kinds of gap already: duration and generalisability.
  2. Recent reviews. A meta-analysis from four years ago finds a small average reduction in anxiety, with high heterogeneity, and calls for studies that separate same-age from cross-age tutoring. A search shows only two studies since have done so.
  3. Matrix. Filling the matrix shows almost every study used self-report questionnaires, none measured attainment alongside anxiety, and only one came from outside Europe or North America.
  4. Citation map. The education studies rarely cite a cluster of psychology papers on anxiety and working memory, which suggest a mechanism no tutoring study has tested.
  5. Conflict. Two well-designed trials disagree: one finds a clear effect, one finds none. The first used cross-age tutors, the second same-age peers.

The gap that emerges is specific: does cross-age peer tutoring reduce maths anxiety more than same-age tutoring, and does the effect last beyond one term? It is supported by a review’s explicit call, a conflict between two trials and repeated limitations. That is a gap an examiner can check, and it already suggests a design: a comparison of two tutoring types with a longer follow-up.

Notice what the example does not say: “there is limited research on peer tutoring.” There is plenty. The gap is in one comparison and one time frame, and naming them precisely is what makes it defensible.

Is your gap worth filling? A five-question test

Not every gap is a good research question. Some are empty for a reason. Test yours:

  • Is it real? You searched at least two databases, plus preprints and theses, and found nothing that answers it.
  • Is it cited? You can point to published papers that state the gap or show it.
  • Does it matter? Filling it would change practice, policy or theory, and you can say for whom.
  • Is it feasible? You can get the data, participants or access within your time and budget.
  • Is it yours? It fits your skills, your supervisor’s expertise and the methods you can learn in time.

If you fail the first question, keep looking. If you fail the fourth, narrow the gap: one population, one setting, one outcome.

How to write the gap statement

A good gap statement has three moves, each with citations:

  1. What is known: “Remote work has been linked to higher job satisfaction among office workers (Author, Year; Author, Year).”
  2. What is not: “However, almost all studies sample white-collar staff in North America and Western Europe, and none follow employees for more than a year (Author, Year).”
  3. What you will do: “This study follows hybrid-working healthcare administrators in Kenya over 18 months.”

That statement then shapes your proposal’s background and aims. Our guide to research proposal structure shows where it goes. For more examples of gap statements by field, the AI for Research team’s article on how to find a research gap is worth reading alongside this one.

Avoid this trap: “No one has studied X” is a strong claim. If an examiner finds one study that did, your argument weakens. Say “few studies have…” or “no study we found…”, and describe your search so the claim is checkable.

Frequently asked questions

What is a research gap?

A research gap is a question that the existing literature has not answered well enough. It may be unstudied, studied only in some groups or settings, studied with weak methods, or studied with conflicting results.

How do I know if my research gap is real?

Search systematically for studies that already answer it, including recent preprints, theses and work in neighbouring fields. If you can cite papers that explicitly call for the work, or show that existing studies disagree or exclude your group, the gap is well supported.

Can AI find a research gap for me?

AI can suggest candidate gaps by summarising what a set of papers covers and where they report limits or disagree. It cannot confirm a gap exists, because it may miss papers. Treat AI suggestions as leads and verify each one with your own search.

How do I write a research gap statement?

State what is known, with citations; state what is not known and why it matters; then state how your study addresses it. Keep it to three or four sentences and make every claim about the literature traceable to a source.
