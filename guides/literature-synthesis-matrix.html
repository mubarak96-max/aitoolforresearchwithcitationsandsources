Literature synthesis matrix: from a pile of papers to an argument

Most weak literature reviews describe one paper after another. A synthesis matrix fixes that by making you compare sources side by side before you write. This guide shows how to set up the columns, fill the rows, read the grid for themes, and write paragraphs from it.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer A synthesis matrix is a table with one row per source and one column per theme or question you care about, such as method, sample, main finding and limitation. Fill each cell in your own words with a page number. When you read down a column, you see where studies agree, disagree or leave gaps, and each column becomes a section or paragraph of your review.

Why a matrix beats a stack of summaries

Summaries are organised by author: “Smith found X. Jones found Y.” A literature review has to be organised by idea: “Studies agree on X, but they disagree on Y, and none has tested Z.” The matrix is the bridge between the two. It forces you to break each paper into the same parts, so the comparison is already done when you start writing.

Information systems researchers Jane Webster and Richard Watson made the same point in their widely used 2002 guidance, “Analyzing the Past to Prepare for the Future: Writing a Literature Review” in MIS Quarterly: a review should be concept-centric, not author-centric, and a concept matrix is the tool they recommend. The idea works in every discipline.

Choose your columns

Columns come in two groups. The first describes each study. The second captures what it says about your themes. Start with a small set and add columns as themes appear. Too many columns on day one turns the matrix into a data entry chore.

A worked example

Suppose your review asks how remote work affects employee wellbeing. After reading eight papers, three themes keep coming up: autonomy, isolation and work–life boundaries. Here is part of the matrix:

SourceDesignAutonomyIsolationBoundaries
Study A (2021)Survey, n = 2,400, UKHigher autonomy linked to higher wellbeing (p. 7)Not measuredLonger hours reported (p. 9)
Study B (2022)Interviews, n = 32, tech sectorValued, but felt monitored (p. 14)Strong theme, especially new staff (p. 16)Blurred, worse with children at home (p. 18)
Study C (2023)Panel data, 3 waves, GermanySmall positive effectRose over time (p. 11)Not addressed

Reading down the columns, the argument appears on its own. Autonomy has consistent positive evidence. Isolation is measured in fewer studies but looks worse for new staff and over time. Work–life boundaries are reported as a problem, but mostly in qualitative work, which points to a gap in quantitative evidence.

How to fill it in without copying

  • Write in your own words. Paraphrasing at this stage means you understood the paper, and it protects you from accidental plagiarism later.
  • Put direct quotes in quotation marks with a page number, so you can tell them apart months later.
  • Write “not addressed” rather than leaving cells blank. A blank is ambiguous; “not addressed” is a finding.
  • Add page numbers to every claim. They make checking and citing far faster.
  • Fill one paper at a time but revisit early rows when you add a new theme column.

Good matrix rows come from good reading notes. Our guide to taking notes on research papers shows a note template that maps directly onto these columns.

From columns to themes

When you have 10 to 20 rows, read each column from top to bottom and write a one-line summary under it. Use these prompts:

  1. Agreement: which findings recur across designs and settings?
  2. Disagreement: where do results conflict, and do differences in method or sample explain it?
  3. Strength: is the evidence for this theme mostly strong designs or mostly small, descriptive ones?
  4. Gaps: which populations, settings, methods or time periods are missing?
  5. Change: has the picture shifted over time?

The gaps row often becomes the justification for your own study. If that is your goal, our guide on identifying a research gap explains how to show a gap is real rather than a search failure.

Write paragraphs from the matrix

Each theme column becomes a section. Each one-line summary becomes a topic sentence. The cells become the evidence. A paragraph built this way looks like this:

Greater autonomy is the most consistent benefit of remote work, reported in survey, interview and panel studies alike (A, B, C). Its effect appears smaller in longitudinal data than in cross-sectional surveys (C), and qualitative work suggests monitoring can offset it (B).

Notice what the paragraph does: it states a claim, groups the sources that support it, and notes how the evidence differs by design. That is synthesis. The AI for Research team’s guide to the synthesis essay covers the same moves for coursework.

Adapting the matrix to your review type

The basic grid works everywhere, but the columns change with the kind of review you are writing.

  • Course essay or narrative review: keep it light. Citation, main argument, evidence, and one column per theme is enough. Ten to twenty sources fit on one screen.
  • Thesis literature review: add columns for theoretical framework, key concepts and definitions, and how each study relates to your research question. Examiners look for how you position your study, and these columns supply the material.
  • Systematic review: the matrix becomes a data extraction form, agreed in the protocol before you start, with fixed fields for population, intervention, comparison, outcomes, follow-up, results and risk of bias. Two reviewers often extract independently and compare.
  • Qualitative evidence synthesis: add columns for context, participants’ own words (quotes with page numbers) and the authors’ interpretation, kept separate so you can see where your synthesis adds a layer.
  • Policy or industry evidence review: add columns for setting, cost, scale and who produced the evidence, because decision makers ask about transferability and independence.

Whatever the type, colour coding helps when reading down columns: one colour for agreement, one for conflict and one for “not addressed”. Patterns jump out faster than they do from text alone.

Spreadsheet, document or software?

  • Spreadsheet: best for sorting and filtering by year, design or theme. Freeze the first column and wrap text.
  • Word processor table: fine for fewer than 15 sources and easy to share with a supervisor. The Purdue Online Writing Lab has free guidance on synthesis and literature reviews if you want a second explanation.
  • AI-assisted extraction: fastest for large sets; check every cell before you rely on it. Our AI literature review workflow shows where in the process to do the checks.

Common trap: a matrix full of quotes is still a pile of summaries. If you cannot write a one-line summary under a column, you have not yet synthesised that theme.

Frequently asked questions

What is a synthesis matrix?

A synthesis matrix is a table that organises sources by theme. Each row is a source and each column is a theme, question or study feature. It lets you compare what different sources say about the same idea, which is the basis of a synthesised literature review.

What is the difference between a literature matrix and an annotated bibliography?

An annotated bibliography summarises each source separately, one after another. A matrix breaks each source into the same parts and lines them up so you can compare across sources. The bibliography is author-centric; the matrix is theme-centric.

How many sources should a literature review matrix have?

As many as your review covers. A course paper might have 10 to 20 rows, a thesis chapter 40 to 100. If the matrix grows beyond what you can read in one view, split it by theme or keep it in a spreadsheet with filters.

Can AI fill in a literature matrix?

AI can draft the descriptive columns, such as design, sample and main findings, quickly. Check every cell against the paper and keep page numbers. Write the theme summaries and gap analysis yourself, because that is the judgement your review is assessed on.
