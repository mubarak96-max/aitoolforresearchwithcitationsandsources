How to write an abstract: structure, word counts and a worked example

Most readers decide in under a minute whether your paper is worth opening, and they decide from the abstract. Here is a structure that works in every field, a worked example built sentence by sentence, and the checks to run before you submit.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer An abstract is a stand-alone summary of your whole study in 150 to 300 words. Write it last, in five moves: the problem, the gap or aim, what you did, what you found (with numbers), and what it means. Use the past tense for what you did and found, avoid citations and undefined abbreviations, and follow your journal’s word limit and headings exactly.

What an abstract has to do

An abstract has three readers. The first is a search engine or database, which indexes its words. The second is a busy researcher scanning thirty results, who reads it to decide whether to open the paper. The third is an editor or conference committee, who reads it to decide whether to send the paper for review at all.

All three want the same thing: an accurate picture of the study in as few words as possible. That means the abstract must make sense without the paper. A reader should finish it knowing what question you asked, how you answered it, what you found and why it matters. If they need the paper to understand the abstract, it has failed.

It also means the abstract must not promise more than the paper delivers. Reviewers compare the two. A finding stated more strongly in the abstract than in the results is one of the fastest ways to lose their trust.

The four types of abstract

Check which type your journal, university or conference wants before you write a word.

TypeWhat it looks likeWhere you see it
StructuredLabelled headings, often Background, Methods, Results, ConclusionsMedicine, health sciences, many life science journals
Unstructured (informative)One paragraph covering the same moves without headingsMost science, engineering and social science journals
DescriptiveSays what the paper covers, without resultsHumanities, review essays, some book chapters
Conference abstractShort, sometimes with preliminary results; judged on its ownConference submissions, posters, talks

Clinical trial abstracts often follow the CONSORT extension for abstracts, and systematic review abstracts follow the PRISMA 2020 abstract checklist. You can find both through the EQUATOR Network, which collects reporting guidelines by study type.

The five-move structure

Nearly every good informative abstract makes the same five moves, whether or not it uses headings. Give each move one or two sentences.

  1. Context. What is the problem, and why does it matter? One sentence, specific to your topic. Skip “Since the dawn of time” openings.
  2. Gap or aim. What is not known, and what did you set out to do? State the research question or hypothesis plainly.
  3. Method. What did you do? Name the design, the sample or data and the main analysis. Include the number of participants, cases or documents.
  4. Results. What did you find? Give the main result with numbers: effect sizes, percentages, confidence intervals. This is the move most often left vague.
  5. Implication. What does it mean, and for whom? One sentence that follows from the results, without overstating them.

A useful split for a 250-word abstract is roughly 15% context and gap, 25% method, 40% results and 20% implications. Results deserve the most space because they are what readers cannot get anywhere else.

A worked example

Here is an abstract built move by move for an imagined study. The study is invented to show the structure; do not cite it.

Context: Undergraduate students increasingly use AI chatbots to find sources, but little is known about how often the references they collect are accurate.

Aim: We measured the accuracy of references in first-year essays written with and without AI assistance.

Method: We analysed 1,240 references from 212 essays submitted to an introductory psychology course in one semester. Two raters checked each reference against library databases and classified it as accurate, partly accurate or not found (Cohen’s κ = 0.86).

Results: In essays where students reported using a chatbot to find sources, 14% of references could not be found, compared with 3% in other essays. Partly accurate references, usually with a wrong year or author order, were common in both groups (21% and 18%).

Implication: Reference checking should be taught alongside AI use, and courses that allow chatbots for source-finding should require students to verify every reference.

That is 133 words. Notice what it does: it names the sample size, gives the reliability of the rating, reports numbers for both groups and stops at a claim the data supports. It does not cite other studies or explain the theory behind the work.

For a structured abstract, you would add the headings and stop there. For an unstructured one, join the moves into a paragraph and smooth the transitions.

Word limits by document type

Limits vary by publisher, so treat these as typical ranges and always check the author guidelines.

  • Journal article: usually 150 to 300 words. Structured medical abstracts are often around 250.
  • Conference abstract: often 200 to 500 words, sometimes a character limit instead.
  • Master’s thesis: commonly 200 to 350 words, set by your institution.
  • PhD dissertation: often up to 350 words for indexing services; some universities allow a full page.
  • Grant summary: set by the funder, and usually aimed at a wider audience than a journal abstract.

If you have room for only one more sentence, spend it on results.

Tense, voice and wording

Use the past tense for what you did and found (“We surveyed”, “Scores increased”). Use the present tense for what is generally true and for your conclusions (“These results suggest”). Many journals now prefer the active voice with “we”; check yours.

Put the words people search for into the first two sentences. If your study is about “nurse burnout in intensive care”, write those words, not a clever synonym. Search engines and databases weight the title and abstract heavily, and so do readers skimming a results list.

Define any abbreviation the first time you use it, or avoid it. Do not use an abbreviation that appears only once.

Seven common mistakes

  1. Writing it first. You do not know your findings or your argument until the paper is done. Draft a placeholder, then rewrite at the end.
  2. Copying the introduction. Three sentences of background leave no room for results.
  3. Hiding the results. “Significant differences were found” tells readers nothing. Say what differed, by how much and in which direction.
  4. Overclaiming. A cross-sectional survey cannot show that one thing caused another. Match the verb to the design.
  5. Adding new information. Nothing should appear in the abstract that is not in the paper.
  6. Citations and figures. Most journals do not allow references or figure numbers in an abstract.
  7. Ignoring the limit. Submission systems often cut text at the limit without warning.

Using AI to draft or tighten an abstract

AI is good at one abstract task: compressing a long draft into the five moves. It is bad at another: knowing your numbers. Give a tool your finished paper, not your notes, and check every figure it writes against your results section.

Pre-submission checklist

  • It follows the journal’s type (structured or not) and word limit.
  • The first two sentences contain the main search terms.
  • The research question or aim is stated in one sentence.
  • The design, sample size and main analysis are named.
  • The main result is given with numbers and direction.
  • The conclusion matches the design and does not overclaim.
  • No citations, figure numbers or undefined abbreviations.
  • Every fact in it appears in the paper.
  • A colleague outside your project can explain the study after reading it.

Writing a thesis abstract? The same structure works; you just have more room for method. And if you also need a version for non-specialists, see our guide to writing a plain language summary. For general help with academic style, the Purdue Online Writing Lab is a reliable free reference.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an abstract be?

Most journal abstracts are 150 to 300 words, conference abstracts are often 200 to 500 words, and thesis abstracts are usually up to about 350 words. The limit is set by the journal, conference or university, so always check its guidelines and stay under the number they give.

Should I write the abstract first or last?

Write it last. You need your results and conclusions before you can summarise them accurately. It helps to draft a rough version early to focus your thinking, but rewrite it once the paper is finished.

Can an abstract include citations?

Usually not. Most journals ask for an abstract without references, because it must stand alone in databases. If you must mention earlier work, describe it in words instead of citing it.

What is the difference between an abstract and an introduction?

An abstract summarises the whole study, including methods and results, in a few hundred words. An introduction sets up only the problem, the gap and the aim, and leads into the methods. The abstract tells readers what you found; the introduction tells them why you looked.

Is it acceptable to use AI to write an abstract?

Many journals allow AI help with language if you take responsibility for the content and disclose the use where required. Check your journal policy, never let a tool invent or change numbers, and compare every sentence with your results section.
