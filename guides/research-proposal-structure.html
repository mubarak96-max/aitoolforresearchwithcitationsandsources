Research proposal structure: the 9-part outline reviewers expect

A proposal has one job: to convince a reader that your question matters and that your plan can answer it. The structure below is the order in which reviewers test that, section by section.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202612 min read
Short answer A standard research proposal has nine parts: title, abstract, introduction, literature review, research questions or hypotheses, methodology, timeline, budget or resources, and references. Many programmes add ethics and expected outcomes. Whatever template you use, each section should answer one reviewer question, and the methodology must answer the research question you set out.

The logic behind the structure

Proposal templates look different across universities and funders, but they follow the same chain of reasoning. Each section earns the next one:

  1. There is a problem worth caring about (introduction).
  2. What we know about it leaves a specific gap (literature review).
  3. This question addresses the gap (research questions).
  4. This method can answer the question (methodology).
  5. The plan is feasible with this time and these resources (timeline and budget).

When a proposal is rejected, it is usually because one link in this chain is weak. The question does not follow from the gap, or the method cannot produce the answer. If you remember nothing else, read your draft once looking only for these links.

The nine sections, one by one

1. Title

State the topic, the population or setting, and, where it helps, the design. “Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year undergraduates: a prospective cohort study” tells a reviewer far more than “Sleep and learning”. Aim for 10 to 20 words and avoid questions or puns.

2. Abstract or summary

Write it last. In 150 to 300 words, give the problem, the gap, the question, the method and the expected contribution, one or two sentences each. Many reviewers read only this and the methods, so it has to stand alone.

3. Introduction and background

Open with the problem and why it matters, to people, to a field or to policy. Narrow quickly from the broad area to your specific topic. End the introduction with a clear statement of purpose: “This study will examine…”. Two to four paragraphs is usually enough in a short proposal.

4. Literature review

This is not a list of summaries. Group the literature by theme, method or finding, show where studies agree and disagree, and end on the gap your study fills. Reviewers look for three things: that you know the key papers, that you read recent work, and that the gap is real rather than a gap in your search. Our guide to finding a research gap covers how to test that, and the AI literature review workflow shows how to search faster without missing core studies.

5. Research questions, aims or hypotheses

Give one main question and two to four sub-questions at most. Each one must be answerable with the data you plan to collect. Quantitative designs add testable hypotheses; qualitative designs keep open “how” and “why” questions. Frameworks such as FINER (feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant) and PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) help you sharpen them. The AI for Research team has a detailed walkthrough on writing a research question with FINER and PICO.

6. Methodology

The longest and most scrutinised section. Cover:

  • Design: experimental, cohort, case study, survey, ethnography, mixed methods, and why it fits the question.
  • Participants or data: who or what, how many, how you will recruit or obtain them, and inclusion and exclusion criteria.
  • Data collection: instruments, procedures and where validated measures come from. For health studies, the EQUATOR Network lists the reporting guideline for each design, which tells you what reviewers will expect to see.
  • Analysis: the exact tests or coding approach, linked to each question.
  • Validity and limitations: what could bias the results and what you will do about it.
  • Ethics: consent, data protection and approval route, if your template does not give ethics its own section. Our guide to research ethics and informed consent covers what an ethics committee looks for.

A useful self-check: for each research question, can you point to the data that answers it and the analysis you will run on that data? If not, the question or the method needs to change.

7. Timeline

A table or Gantt chart by month or quarter. Include ethics approval, recruitment, data collection, analysis, writing and buffer time. Reviewers distrust plans with no slack; allow 10 to 20 percent extra for delays.

8. Budget or resources

For grants, an itemised budget with a short justification for each line. For theses, a resources paragraph: equipment, software, data access, travel and supervision. Show that you can get what you need.

9. References

Use the style your programme requires, consistently. Every in-text citation must appear in the list and vice versa. If you are writing in APA, the official APA Style site has free sample papers and reference examples. If you are unsure which style to use, our comparison of APA, MLA, Chicago and Harvard has side-by-side examples.

Typical length of each section

Use these as starting proportions for a 3,000-word proposal, then adjust to your programme’s rules.

SectionShare of wordsAbout (of 3,000)Reviewer question
Abstract5–8%200What is this, in brief?
Introduction10–12%350Why does this matter?
Literature review25–30%850What is known, and what is missing?
Questions / hypotheses3–5%120What exactly will you answer?
Methodology35–40%1,100Can this plan answer it?
Timeline and resources8–10%280Is it feasible?
Expected outcomes3–5%100What will we gain?

References, appendices and the title page usually sit outside the word count. Check your guidelines.

A fill-in outline you can copy

Title: [Topic] in [population/setting]: a [design] study

1. Abstract (write last)
2. Introduction
   - The problem: ...
   - Why it matters: ...
   - Purpose statement: This study will ...
3. Literature review
   - Theme A: what studies show ...
   - Theme B: where findings disagree ...
   - The gap: no study has yet ...
4. Research question(s)
   - RQ1: ...
   - H1 (if quantitative): ...
5. Methodology
   - Design and rationale
   - Sample, recruitment, criteria
   - Instruments and procedure
   - Analysis plan (one line per RQ)
   - Validity, limitations, ethics
6. Timeline (table by month)
7. Resources / budget
8. Expected outcomes and contribution
9. References

How the structure changes by proposal type

  • PhD application: shorter (often 1,500–3,000 words), with more weight on fit with the supervisor and department and less detail on the analysis.
  • Master’s or doctoral thesis proposal: the full nine parts, often with a chapter plan and a fuller literature review defended before a committee.
  • Grant proposal: adds specific aims, significance and innovation, impact, a detailed budget and sometimes a data management plan. Follow the funder’s headings exactly; reviewers score against them.
  • Qualitative proposal: replaces hypotheses with open questions, describes sampling to saturation, and adds a positionality or reflexivity statement.

Seven mistakes that sink proposals

  • A topic instead of a question (“social media and teenagers”).
  • A literature review that summarises papers one by one but never names a gap.
  • Methods that cannot answer the question, such as a cross-sectional survey for a causal claim.
  • No sample size reasoning, or a sample you cannot realistically reach.
  • An analysis plan that says “data will be analysed” with no named method.
  • A timeline with no time for ethics approval or delays.
  • References that do not exist or do not support the claim, a growing problem with AI-drafted text. See how to spot hallucinated citations.

A practical writing order

Do not write in the order the sections appear. This order saves the most rewriting:

  1. Draft the research question and sub-questions.
  2. Sketch the methodology: one line per question on data and analysis.
  3. Search and write the literature review, ending on the gap.
  4. Revise the question so it fits the gap exactly.
  5. Write the introduction, timeline and resources.
  6. Write the abstract and title last.

Then read an annotated example before you submit. The research proposal example with notes on every section is a good benchmark for tone and depth.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main parts of a research proposal?

Most research proposals have nine parts: title, abstract, introduction and background, literature review, research questions or hypotheses, methodology, timeline, budget or resources, and references. Ethics and expected outcomes are often added as their own sections.

How long should a research proposal be?

It depends on the purpose. A PhD application proposal is often 1,500 to 3,000 words, a master's thesis proposal 2,000 to 4,000 words, and a doctoral proposal defended before a committee can run to 10,000 words or more. Always follow the length your programme or funder sets.

What is the most important section of a research proposal?

The research question and the methodology carry the most weight, because reviewers judge whether the question is worth answering and whether your method can answer it. A strong literature review supports both by showing the gap the question fills.

Can I use AI to write a research proposal?

You can use AI to outline, find literature and get feedback, if your programme allows it. The question, design and argument should be yours. Use tools that cite real papers, check every reference, and disclose AI use where your institution requires it.

Do I need a hypothesis in a research proposal?

Only in hypothesis-testing designs, usually quantitative ones. Qualitative and exploratory proposals use research questions instead. Mixed-methods proposals often have both: hypotheses for the quantitative strand and open questions for the qualitative one.
