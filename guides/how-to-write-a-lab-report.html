How to write a lab report: format, sections and a worked example

A lab report tells a reader what you tested, how, what happened and what it means, clearly enough that they could repeat the experiment. The format is standard across most sciences. Here is what goes in each section, how to present your data, and the mistakes that cost marks.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20266 min read
Short answer Most lab reports follow IMRaD: a title and abstract, an Introduction with background and hypothesis, Methods in enough detail to repeat the work, Results with tables and figures but no interpretation, and a Discussion that interprets the results, explains errors and compares with expected values, followed by a conclusion and references. Write methods in the past tense and passive or first person as your course requires.

The standard format

Lab reports follow the same logic as journal articles, usually called IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion). Your course may add or rename sections, so check the brief. A typical report has:

SectionAnswers the questionTypical share of length
TitleWhat was tested?One line
AbstractWhat is the whole report in brief?100–200 words
IntroductionWhy, and what did you expect?15%
MethodsHow exactly did you do it?20%
ResultsWhat happened?25%
DiscussionWhat does it mean, and how reliable is it?30%
Conclusion and referencesWhat is the answer, and what did you rely on?10%

Section by section

Title

Specific and informative. “Effect of Temperature on the Rate of Catalase Activity in Potato Tissue” tells a reader far more than “Enzyme Lab”.

Abstract

Write it last. In a few sentences: the aim, the method, the main result with numbers and the conclusion. Our guide to writing an abstract covers the five-move structure, which works for lab reports too.

Introduction

Give the scientific background the reader needs, with citations to your textbook or primary sources. Explain the principle behind the experiment, such as the reaction, law or biological process. End with the aim and a testable hypothesis, ideally with a prediction: “If catalase activity depends on temperature, the rate of oxygen production will increase up to about 37 °C and then fall.”

Methods

Write enough detail for someone else to repeat the experiment: materials and concentrations, equipment and its precision, the procedure, the number of repeats, and how you analysed the data. Write in paragraphs, not the numbered list from the lab sheet, unless your instructor allows it. Use the past tense: “The solution was heated to 40 °C” or “We heated the solution to 40 °C”. Do not include results here.

Results

Report what you observed and measured, without explaining it. Lead with a sentence summarising the main trend, then refer to tables and figures: “The rate of oxygen production increased with temperature up to 35 °C and fell sharply above 45 °C (Figure 1).” Include processed data such as means and standard deviations; raw data usually goes in an appendix.

Discussion

This is the most heavily weighted section in most marking schemes. Interpret the results: do they support the hypothesis? Explain the trend with the underlying science. Compare with expected or published values and calculate percentage error where relevant. Identify sources of error, say whether each is random or systematic, and estimate its effect. Suggest specific improvements.

Conclusion

Two or three sentences: the answer to the aim, with the key value. No new information.

References

Cite your lab manual, textbook and any papers you used, in the style your department requires. Our guide to citation styles compared shows the common ones; many science departments use APA, Harvard, ACS or Vancouver.

Presenting data: tables and figures

  • Number and title everything. Tables get titles above (“Table 1. Mean reaction rate at each temperature”); figures get captions below.
  • Units in headings, not in every cell: “Temperature (°C)”.
  • Choose the right chart. A line or scatter plot for continuous variables, a bar chart for categories. Put the independent variable on the x-axis.
  • Show variability. Error bars for standard deviation or standard error, and say which in the caption.
  • Consistent precision. Report values to the precision of your instrument, not to eight decimal places from a spreadsheet.
  • Refer to every table and figure in the text. A figure nobody mentions is a figure nobody reads.

Diagrams of your apparatus or process help in the methods. The scientific diagram tool in AI for Research can draw clean process and apparatus diagrams if hand-drawn ones are not accepted.

Writing about error and uncertainty

Markers want specific, quantified discussion of error, not a generic “human error” sentence.

  • Random error varies unpredictably between repeats, for example reading a meniscus. It shows up as spread in your data. Reduce it with more repeats.
  • Systematic error shifts every measurement in the same direction, for example a balance that reads 0.02 g high. It does not show up as spread. Reduce it by calibration or a better method.
  • Percentage error = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100. Report it and say whether your error sources explain its size and direction.
  • Percentage uncertainty of a measurement = absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100. Combine uncertainties as your course teaches.

A good error paragraph names the largest source of error, says whether it would raise or lower the result, and links that to the difference between your value and the expected one.

A worked example

A short discussion paragraph for a density experiment (values invented to show the pattern):

The mean density of the aluminium samples was 2.62 ± 0.05 g/cm³, 3.0% below the accepted value of 2.70 g/cm³. The difference is larger than the random uncertainty, which suggests a systematic error. The most likely source is the volume measurement: air bubbles clinging to the samples in the displacement cylinder would raise the measured volume and lower the calculated density, which matches the direction of the error. Tapping the cylinder to release bubbles, or measuring volume from dimensions with digital calipers, would reduce this error.

Notice it gives the value with uncertainty, quantifies the error, identifies its type, explains its direction and proposes a specific fix.

Mistakes that cost marks

  1. Interpreting in the results. Keep “because” and “this shows” for the discussion.
  2. Copying the lab sheet as the method, including instructions like “Make sure you…”.
  3. No hypothesis, or one that cannot be tested.
  4. “Human error” as the only explanation for a discrepancy.
  5. Unlabelled axes, missing units and figures without captions.
  6. Claiming the experiment “proved” a hypothesis. Experiments support or fail to support.
  7. Reporting too many decimal places.

Marking checklist

  • The title names the variables tested.
  • The introduction ends with an aim and a testable hypothesis.
  • The method could be repeated by another student.
  • Results state trends and refer to every table and figure.
  • Tables and figures are numbered, titled, labelled and have units.
  • Error bars or uncertainties are shown and explained.
  • The discussion compares results with expected values.
  • Errors are specific, typed and linked to the result.
  • The conclusion answers the aim in two or three sentences.
  • All sources are cited.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main sections of a lab report?

Title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion and references. Some courses add an appendix for raw data, combine results and discussion, or ask for a separate aims and hypothesis section. Follow your course brief.

Should a lab report be written in first or third person?

Traditionally, lab reports use the third person and passive voice, such as “the solution was heated”. Many scientific journals now accept the first person, such as “we heated the solution”. Use whichever your instructor or department prefers, and stay consistent.

What tense is a lab report written in?

Use the past tense for what you did and found, in the methods and results. Use the present tense for established scientific facts and for statements about what your results show in the discussion.

How long should a lab report be?

It depends on the course. First-year reports are often 1,000 to 2,000 words; upper-level reports can be longer. The discussion is usually the longest and most heavily marked section.
