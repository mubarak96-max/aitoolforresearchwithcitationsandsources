How to choose an AI research assistant: 9 tests before you trust one

Most AI tools sound confident. Few let you check what they say. These nine tests, which take about an hour, separate an assistant you can build a thesis on from one that will cost you a week of corrections.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202611 min read
Short answer Pick the assistant that shows where every claim came from. It should search a real scholarly index (not the model’s memory), link each citation to a paper you can open, answer from your own PDFs with page numbers, and export into the citation style and file format you submit in. Everything else is secondary.

Why “which AI is smartest” is the wrong question

Every large language model can write a fluent paragraph about your topic. That fluency is the problem: a wrong statement reads exactly like a right one. In research, the value of an assistant is not how well it writes but how quickly you can verify what it wrote.

So instead of asking which model is best, ask which tool makes checking cheap. A good research assistant does three things a general chatbot does not: it retrieves real records from scholarly databases, it ties each sentence to the record it came from, and it removes anything it cannot back up. The tests below check exactly that.

The 9 tests

1. Where do the sources come from?

Ask the vendor, or look in the help pages, for the list of indexes it searches. Look for named sources: OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar for broad coverage (each indexes well over 200 million works), PubMed and Europe PMC for medicine, and arXiv, bioRxiv and medRxiv for preprints. If the answer is “our model was trained on millions of papers”, that is memory, not search, and the tool will invent references.

2. Can every citation be opened?

Run a question in a field you know. Click every citation in the answer. Each one should resolve to a real record with a DOI (you can look one up in Crossref), PubMed ID or a working link. Count how many fail. One broken reference in ten is already too many for a thesis.

3. Does the paper say what the sentence says?

A citation can be real and still be misused. Pick three cited sentences and read the abstract or the relevant section of each paper. Does the paper support the claim, partly support it, or say something else? This is the test most tools quietly fail, and it is why our guide to spotting hallucinated citations treats “real but wrong” as its own category.

4. Can you choose where it looks?

You will want different sources for different jobs: only peer-reviewed papers for a literature review, preprints for a fast-moving field, your own Library for a chapter you are revising. Check for filters by year, citation count, open access and source type, and an option to restrict answers to your own uploaded papers. If the tool lets you write your own search strings, our guide to Boolean search operators shows how to make them precise.

5. Does it answer from your PDFs, with page numbers?

Upload a long paper you know well and ask a detail question, such as the sample size or the exclusion criteria. A good assistant quotes the passage and gives the page. A weak one paraphrases the abstract. Our guide on reading research papers faster with AI has a set of test questions you can reuse.

6. What happens after the answer?

Research does not end in a chat window. It ends in a literature review, a proposal, a thesis chapter or a manuscript. Check whether you can cite a source from the answer straight into a document, keep papers in a library with collections, import from Zotero or Mendeley, and export to Word, LaTeX or BibTeX without retyping.

7. How does it handle “no evidence”?

Ask something the literature cannot answer yet, or a question built on a false premise. A trustworthy assistant says the evidence is thin or contradicts the premise. A risky one writes a confident paragraph anyway. This single test tells you more about a tool’s design than any feature list.

8. What does it do with your data?

Unpublished chapters, interview transcripts and grant drafts are sensitive. Read the privacy policy for three things: whether your content trains models, how long uploads are kept, and whether you can delete them. If you work with patient or participant data, check your ethics approval before uploading anything.

9. What does real use cost?

Free plans often look generous until you run a long report. Price the job you will actually do, for example “a literature report each week plus daily paper questions”, and see which plan it needs. Prefer tools that show the cost of a job before it runs and refund failed jobs.

The four kinds of AI research tool

Tools in this space overlap, but most start from one of four jobs. Knowing which one a tool was built for explains most of its strengths and gaps.

  • Answer engines give a short cited answer to a question, often with a “what the studies say” summary. Good for quick checks, limited for long writing.
  • Discovery and mapping tools show how papers cite each other so you can find the ones you missed. Good for snowballing, not for writing. See our research gap guide for how to use them.
  • Extraction and screening tools pull data from many papers into a table or screen records for a systematic review. Covered in our systematic review software comparison.
  • End-to-end workspaces combine search, cited answers, a library and a writing editor, so the research flows into the document you submit.

How to run the tests in one hour

  1. 10 minutes: write three questions: one in your own field, one outside it, and one with a false premise.
  2. 20 minutes: run all three in two or three tools. Save the answers.
  3. 20 minutes: open every citation. Mark each one as real and supporting, real but misused, or not found.
  4. 10 minutes: upload one PDF and ask two detail questions. Then score the tools with the table above.

If you want a ranked list to start from, the AI for Research team keeps a comparison of the best AI tools for each research stage. Run your own tests anyway: the tool that scores best on your questions is the right one for you.

Before you submit anything: no tool removes the need to read your sources. Check your university or journal policy on AI use (our guide to AI rules in academic writing explains the common positions), and keep a record of how you used AI.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI research assistant?

An AI research assistant is a tool that searches scholarly literature, reads papers and answers questions about them, usually with citations. The good ones retrieve real records from indexes such as OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or PubMed and cite them; general chatbots often answer from memory instead.

Can I use ChatGPT as a research assistant?

For brainstorming and explaining concepts, yes. For finding and citing literature, a general chatbot is risky because it can produce references that look real but do not exist. Use a tool that pulls citations from a scholarly index and lets you open every paper.

Is there a free AI research assistant?

Yes. Several research assistants have free plans, including Elicit, Consensus, SciSpace and AI for Research. Free plans usually limit the number of long reports or AI answers per month rather than paper search itself.

How do I check if an AI research assistant is accurate?

Ask it three questions in a topic you know well, then open every cited paper and check that it exists, that it says what the answer claims, and that obvious key papers were not missed. Repeat on a topic you do not know to see how it handles uncertainty.
