Systematic review software: how to choose the right tool for your review

A systematic review lives or dies on its records: every search, every screening decision, every disagreement and every number in the PRISMA diagram. Good software keeps that record for you. This guide shows what to look for at each stage, then compares the main options.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202612 min read
Short answer Choose software that logs your searches, de-duplicates imports, supports two independent reviewers with conflict resolution and an agreement statistic, holds a custom extraction form, and builds a PRISMA 2020 diagram from live counts. If your institution licenses a dedicated tool, start there. If you also need to write the review, pick a tool that connects screening to the manuscript.

Why spreadsheets stop working

Many first reviews start in Excel. It works for a hundred records and one reviewer. It breaks when you have two thousand records, two reviewers working blind, a third person resolving conflicts, a full-text stage and a protocol that says exactly how all of this happens. The failures are quiet: a filter left on, a duplicate missed, a count in the PRISMA diagram that no longer matches the sheet. Reviewers and editors do notice.

Dedicated software removes those failure points by keeping each decision as a record with a reviewer, a date and a reason.

What you need at each stage

1. Protocol

Before searching, you write a protocol: the question, eligibility criteria, databases, screening process and planned analysis. Many reviews register it on PROSPERO or OSF, and the Cochrane Handbook sets out what a protocol should cover. Software helps if it stores the protocol with the project, so the criteria you screen against are visible at every decision.

2. Search and import

You will search several databases, such as PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science or CINAHL, and import the results. Look for:

  • Import of RIS, BibTeX, PubMed and common database export formats (if you are still writing the strings, see Boolean search operators).
  • A search log: database, date, exact string and number of hits.
  • Automatic de-duplication, with a way to review possible duplicates before removing them.

3. Title and abstract screening

This is where most of the time goes. Essential features:

  • Blind dual screening: two reviewers decide independently and cannot see each other’s votes.
  • Conflict resolution: disagreements go to a discussion or a third reviewer.
  • Agreement statistics: Cohen’s kappa or percentage agreement, often reported in the methods. If you are not sure how to calculate or interpret it, the AI for Research guide to Cohen’s kappa for screening agreement is clear and practical.
  • Keyboard shortcuts and highlights: highlighting include and exclude terms in abstracts makes screening noticeably faster.

4. Full-text screening

Records that pass go to full text. The software should let you attach PDFs, record a reason for every exclusion from a fixed list (wrong population, wrong outcome, wrong design), and count those reasons for the PRISMA diagram.

5. Data extraction and quality

You extract study details into a form: design, sample, intervention, outcomes, results. You also assess risk of bias with a tool such as RoB 2 or ROBINS-I (our risk of bias assessment guide explains which to use). Look for custom forms, dual extraction with comparison, and export to CSV for analysis in R, Stata or RevMan.

6. PRISMA diagram and reporting

The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram reports how many records you found, removed, screened, excluded and included. Building it by hand is error-prone because counts change until the last day. Software that draws it from live counts saves real time. For a walk-through with worked numbers, see this guide to making a PRISMA flow diagram.

Which tool for which situation

Most choices come down to four common situations.

A master’s or PhD student doing a review for a thesis

You may be the only screener, with a supervisor as second reviewer for a sample. Your review becomes a chapter, so the hard part is often moving from included studies to a written synthesis with correct citations. Favour a tool that is free or cheap at small scale, exports everything, and connects to your writing. Ask your library first: many universities already license a screening tool and run training on it.

A small team publishing in a journal

Two or three reviewers, a few thousand records, a registered protocol. Blind dual screening, conflict handling and a kappa figure are non-negotiable. A fast, collaborative screener works well, as long as extraction and the PRISMA counts are handled carefully. Agree on one place where the final numbers live.

A large or funded review

Tens of thousands of records, several reviewers, perhaps a guideline panel waiting on results. Audit trails, role permissions and configurable forms matter more than price. This is where institutional platforms earn their cost.

A rapid or scoping review

Lighter rules, shorter deadlines. AI-assisted screening and prioritisation help most here. Be explicit in your report about which shortcuts you took, such as a single screener with a sample checked by a second person.

Mistakes that software will not catch

  • Criteria that drift. Reviewers slowly interpret criteria differently. Re-calibrate on a shared sample every few hundred records.
  • Searches you cannot reproduce. Save the exact strings and dates, even if the software logs them, and paste them into an appendix.
  • Grey literature left out. Trial registries, theses and reports often sit outside the main databases. Decide in the protocol whether to search them.
  • Counts edited by hand. If you ever change a number outside the tool, the PRISMA diagram and your text can disagree. Make changes in one place.

Using AI in screening, responsibly

AI screening suggestions can cut screening time a lot, especially on large result sets with many obvious excludes. They also make mistakes, and a missed study is a real problem in a systematic review. Three safe patterns:

  1. AI as second reviewer: one human screens everything; AI acts as the second vote. Every conflict goes to a human.
  2. AI as prioritiser: AI ranks records by likely relevance so humans see the probable includes first. Humans still screen all records.
  3. AI as checker: after human screening, AI flags excluded records that look relevant for a second look.

Whichever you use, report it: which tool, which stage, how decisions were made and checked. Calibrate first: have both reviewers and the AI screen the same 50 to 100 records, compare results and refine your criteria before the full run.

Pre-purchase checklist

  • Does it import the formats your databases export?
  • Does it log searches and show de-duplication decisions?
  • Can two reviewers screen blind, with conflicts resolved and recorded?
  • Does it calculate an agreement statistic?
  • Can you build your own extraction and risk-of-bias forms?
  • Does it produce a PRISMA 2020 diagram from live counts?
  • Can you export everything (records, decisions, extraction) as CSV or RIS?
  • Does the pricing fit your number of reviewers, records and projects?

Frequently asked questions

What is the best software for a systematic review?

It depends on your team and scale. Large funded reviews often use Covidence or DistillerSR through an institutional licence. Rayyan is popular for fast, collaborative screening. If you also need to write up the review with citations in the same place, an end-to-end workspace such as AI for Research covers search, screening, extraction, PRISMA and writing.

Is there free systematic review software?

Yes. Rayyan has a free tier, several open-source tools exist, and AI for Research’s free plan includes one small systematic review project. Free tiers usually limit the number of records, reviewers or projects, so check current limits before you start.

Can AI screen studies for a systematic review?

AI can suggest include or exclude decisions and rank records by likely relevance, which saves time. Reporting standards still expect human reviewers to make and record decisions, so treat AI as an assistant or as a second check, and report how you used it.

Do I need two reviewers for a systematic review?

Most guidance, including the Cochrane Handbook, recommends at least two independent reviewers for screening and data extraction, with a method to resolve disagreements. Agreement is often reported with Cohen’s kappa.

Can I make a PRISMA diagram in Excel or Word?

Yes, using the PRISMA 2020 template, but you must update the counts by hand every time something changes. Software that builds the diagram from your live screening counts removes that error-prone step.
