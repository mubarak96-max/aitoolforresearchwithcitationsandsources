How to write a discussion section that reviewers trust

The results say what you found. The discussion says what it means, how sure you are and what should happen next. It is where papers are most often rejected for overclaiming, and where good ones show judgement. Here is a structure that works.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Open with your main finding in one or two sentences and answer the research question directly. Then interpret it against earlier studies, explaining agreements and differences. Discuss the limitations honestly and how they affect the conclusions. Finish with implications for practice or theory and specific directions for future research. Do not repeat results in detail or introduce new ones.

What the discussion is for

The discussion answers the question your introduction asked. If your introduction ended with “We tested whether X predicts Y”, your discussion should open with whether it did. That symmetry is what makes a paper feel like one argument rather than a set of sections.

It is also where you show judgement. Anyone can report a p-value. The discussion shows whether you understand what it does and does not establish, how your study fits with the rest of the field, and where it could be wrong.

Some journals combine results and discussion into one section, and some fields, especially qualitative research, interleave interpretation with findings. Check recent papers in your target journal before you decide.

A five-part structure

  1. Main finding. One or two sentences answering the research question. No statistics needed here; the reader has seen them.
  2. Interpretation. What the finding means. Possible explanations or mechanisms, especially for surprising results.
  3. Comparison. How the finding fits with earlier work: where it agrees, where it differs and why.
  4. Limitations. What could have biased the result or limits how far it applies, and how much it matters.
  5. Implications and future research. What follows for practice, policy or theory, and the specific next study that would help.

Then a short conclusion paragraph, or a separate Conclusions section if your journal has one, restating the take-home message.

For a paper with several findings, repeat parts 2 and 3 for each finding, from the most important to the least. Do not discuss every secondary result; discuss the ones that matter for the question.

Comparing with earlier studies

This is the part that separates a strong discussion from a weak one. Three patterns cover most cases.

SituationWhat to write
Your result agrees with earlier workSay so briefly, then say what your study adds: a new population, a stronger design, a larger sample or a mechanism.
Your result differsOffer specific reasons: differences in sample, measure, setting, timing or analysis. Do not assume your study is right and theirs wrong.
Earlier work is mixedShow where your result falls and whether it helps explain the disagreement.

Cite the studies you compare with, and make sure each says what you claim. A discussion that misrepresents earlier work is easy for a reviewer who wrote that work to spot. If AI helped you find comparison studies, check them all; our guide to hallucinated citations explains the four ways references go wrong.

Writing limitations honestly

Every study has limitations. Reviewers do not reject papers for having them; they reject papers for hiding them or for listing them without thought. For each limitation, write three things:

  1. What it is. “Data were self-reported.”
  2. How it could affect the result. “Participants may under-report alcohol use, which would bias the association toward zero.”
  3. Why it does not overturn the conclusion, or how far it does. “Because under-reporting would weaken rather than create the association, the true effect may be larger.”

Common limitations to consider: sample size and power, selection bias, measurement, confounding, missing data, generalisability, and the study design itself. A cross-sectional study cannot establish the order of events; say so. Our guide to risk of bias assessment covers the main sources of bias reviewers look for.

Strengths belong here too. A sentence on what your design does well, such as a large sample, objective measures or a pre-registered analysis, helps readers weigh the limitations.

Language that fits the evidence

Overclaiming is the most common reason reviewers ask for major revisions to a discussion. Match the verb to the design.

DesignFair wordingOverclaim
Randomised experiment“X reduced Y”, “X caused a reduction in Y in this sample”“X cures Y”
Cohort or longitudinal“X predicted Y”, “X was followed by Y”“X causes Y”
Cross-sectional“X was associated with Y”“X leads to Y”
Qualitative“Participants described…”, “suggests that…”“Users prefer…” (as a general claim)

Hedges such as “may”, “suggests” and “in this sample” are not weakness; they are accuracy. But do not hedge every sentence until nothing is claimed. State your main finding clearly, then qualify where the evidence requires it.

Statistical significance is not practical importance. Discuss the size of an effect, not just whether it passed a threshold. See our guides to effect sizes and p-values and confidence intervals.

A short example

The study below is invented to show the structure.

[Main finding] In this cohort of 3,400 adults, people who walked at least 7,000 steps a day had a lower risk of hospital admission over five years than those who walked fewer than 4,000.

[Comparison] This agrees with earlier cohorts that measured steps with wearable devices, and extends them to adults over 75, a group earlier studies included in small numbers.

[Interpretation] The association was strongest for cardiovascular admissions, which fits known effects of regular moderate activity on blood pressure.

[Limitation] Because step counts were measured once at baseline, we cannot rule out that people already becoming unwell walked less. Excluding admissions in the first year made little difference to the estimates, which suggests this reverse causation does not explain the whole association.

[Implication] A trial testing whether raising daily steps in inactive older adults reduces admissions would establish whether the relationship is causal.

Discussions for qualitative and mixed methods studies

Qualitative discussions follow the same logic but make different kinds of claims. Instead of effect sizes and generalisation to a population, you discuss how your themes extend, challenge or refine existing theory, and how far they may transfer to similar settings.

  • Link themes to theory. Say which concepts your findings support, which they complicate and what new concept they suggest.
  • Discuss transferability, not generalisability. Describe the setting and participants clearly enough that readers can judge whether the findings apply elsewhere.
  • Reflect on your position. Many qualitative journals expect a note on how the researchers’ background may have shaped the analysis.
  • Mixed methods: discuss where the qualitative and quantitative findings agree, where they diverge, and what the combination shows that neither could alone.

Our guide to thematic analysis covers how themes are developed in the first place.

Writing the final paragraph

End with a short paragraph, or a Conclusions section if your journal has one, that states the take-home message in two to four sentences. It should match the conclusion in your abstract word for word in meaning. Avoid ending on “more research is needed”; end on what your study changes, even if the change is modest.

Unexpected or null results deserve the same clarity. A well-designed study that found no effect is useful: say so plainly, discuss whether it had enough power to detect a meaningful effect, and explain what the null result rules out.

Before you submit

  • The first paragraph answers the research question from the introduction.
  • No new results appear; every number discussed is in the results.
  • Each main finding is compared with cited earlier work.
  • Differences from earlier studies are explained, not ignored.
  • Each limitation states its likely effect on the result.
  • Causal language matches the study design.
  • Effect sizes, not only significance, are discussed.
  • Future research is specific, not “more research is needed”.
  • The conclusion matches what the abstract says.

Many fields have reporting guidelines with a discussion item, such as CONSORT for trials and STROBE for observational studies. The EQUATOR Network lists them all, and the ICMJE recommendations describe what medical journals expect from each section of a manuscript. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between results and discussion?

The results section reports what you found, with numbers and without interpretation. The discussion explains what the findings mean, compares them with earlier work, sets out limitations and draws implications. Some journals combine the two into one section.

How long should a discussion section be?

Often about as long as the introduction or slightly longer, around 15 to 25% of the paper, or roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words in a 6,000-word article. Check recent papers in your target journal for the norm.

Should I include limitations in the discussion?

Yes. Most journals expect a limitations paragraph or subsection in the discussion. For each limitation, explain how it could affect the result and how far it changes your conclusions. Reviewers trust papers that discuss limitations openly.

Can I cite new studies in the discussion that were not in the introduction?

Yes. The discussion often cites studies that help interpret or compare your results, which may not have been needed in the introduction. Just make sure every cited study is relevant and accurately described.
