How to choose a journal to publish in

Desk rejection is usually a scope problem, not a quality problem. Choosing the journal before you finish writing saves months, because you write for its readers, its word limit and its format from the start.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Start from the papers you cite most: the journals that published them are your first candidates. Then check each candidate for scope (does it publish this type of study on this topic?), audience, article type and word limit, open access options and fees, typical time to decision, and indexing. Shortlist three journals in order of preference, confirm each is legitimate, and format for the first one.

Choose the journal earlier than you think

Many researchers finish a paper and then look for somewhere to send it. That order costs time. Journals differ in word limits, structure, reference style and what they expect in the introduction. A paper written for a broad general journal reads very differently from one written for a specialist journal.

A good moment to choose is when your results are clear but before you draft the introduction and discussion. At that point you know what you can claim, and you can frame it for the right readers.

Step 1: build a long list of candidates

Use three sources and aim for eight to twelve journals.

  • Your own reference list. Count where your key references were published. If five of your most important sources appeared in one journal, it is an obvious candidate.
  • Where similar papers appear. Search for recent studies with a similar design and topic and note their journals.
  • Your colleagues. Supervisors and co-authors know which journals are respected in your sub-field, which are slow, and which editors are fair.

For open access options, the Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) lists vetted open access journals with their fees and licences.

Step 2: judge each journal on seven criteria

Scope

Read the aims and scope page, then read the titles of the last two issues. The scope statement says what the journal hopes to publish; recent issues show what it actually publishes. If nothing similar to your paper appeared in the last year, think hard before submitting.

Audience

Who should read your paper? Specialists who will build on the method, practitioners who will change what they do, or a broad audience that needs the big picture? Pick the journal those readers open.

Article type and length

Check that the journal accepts your article type (original research, short communication, review, case report, methods paper) and that your paper fits its word, figure and reference limits.

Open access and cost

Is the journal subscription-based, fully open access, or hybrid? What is the article processing charge, and does your funder or institution cover it? Many funders require open access; check their policy before you submit. The Sherpa Romeo service shows each journal’s policy on sharing preprints and accepted manuscripts.

Speed

Some journals publish median time to first decision and time to publication. If you need a publication before a job application or the end of a grant, speed can matter more than prestige.

Indexing

Is the journal indexed in the databases your field searches, such as PubMed for biomedicine? A paper in a journal that is not indexed where your readers look is hard to find.

Reputation and impact

Impact metrics are one signal, not the only one. Look at who sits on the editorial board and whether researchers you respect publish there. Our guide to research impact metrics explains what journal-level numbers can and cannot tell you.

Step 3: score your shortlist

Score each candidate 0 to 2 on each criterion. Weight the criteria that matter most to you; for a PhD student near a deadline, speed may count double.

Rank your top three. Submit to the first; if it rejects the paper, you already know where it goes next, and you can reformat in a day instead of starting the search again.

Step 4: confirm the journal is legitimate

An unsolicited email inviting you to submit is not a compliment. Predatory journals take fees without real peer review, and a paper published in one can be hard to republish elsewhere. Before you submit, run the Think. Check. Submit. checklist, and read our full guide on how to identify predatory journals.

Step 5: format for your first choice

Once you have chosen, read the author guidelines completely. Formatting errors are one of the most avoidable reasons for a paper to be returned before review. Check the structure, abstract format, word limits, reference style, figure resolution and required statements (ethics, funding, conflicts, data availability).

Mistakes that lead to desk rejection

  • Submitting to the highest-impact journal in the field “just to try” when the paper is clearly a specialist contribution.
  • Ignoring the article type limits, for example sending a 9,000-word paper to a journal with a 4,000-word limit.
  • An introduction that never says why this journal’s readers should care.
  • A cover letter that names the wrong journal. See our guide to writing a journal cover letter.
  • Missing required statements on ethics, data availability or funding.

Ask the editor: if you are unsure whether a paper fits, many journals accept a short pre-submission enquiry. Send the title, abstract and one sentence on why it suits the journal. A quick “not for us” saves you weeks.

Special cases

Null or negative results

Studies that find no effect are valuable and often hard to place. Look for journals that state explicitly that they judge papers on methodological soundness rather than novelty, and consider whether a registered report format would suit future studies. Make sure the paper explains why the null result is informative, for example because the study was adequately powered.

Interdisciplinary work

A paper that spans two fields risks being too specialist for one audience and too basic for the other. Decide which community you most want to reach and write the introduction for them. Mention the other field’s relevance in the cover letter so the editor can find suitable reviewers.

Review articles

Many journals only publish reviews they have invited. Check the author guidelines and, if needed, send a pre-submission enquiry with an outline. Systematic reviews are usually welcome as unsolicited submissions if they follow PRISMA; see our systematic review software guide for the tools that make reporting easier.

Practitioner audiences

If the main readers are clinicians, teachers, engineers or policymakers, a practitioner journal or a professional association’s journal may have more influence than a higher-ranked academic title. You can also turn the findings into a short policy brief alongside the paper.

A note for students and first-time authors

Your first paper does not need to be in the most famous journal in your field. A well-matched specialist journal with fair, prompt reviewers often teaches you more and gets your work read by the people who will cite it. Talk to your supervisor about the realistic target for this paper, and keep a list of second and third choices from the start.

Write down why each journal is on your list. When a rejection arrives, that note stops you from second-guessing the whole plan: you simply reformat for the next journal, adjust the cover letter, and resubmit within a week. Rejections are a normal part of publishing, and many well-cited papers were rejected somewhere first.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if a journal is right for my paper?

Read its aims and scope, then look at the last two issues. If it has recently published papers with a similar topic, design and audience to yours, it is a good candidate. If not, the scope statement alone is not enough.

Should I choose a journal by impact factor?

Impact factor is one signal among several. Scope fit, audience, speed and indexing usually matter more for whether your paper is accepted and read. Use journal metrics to compare journals that already fit your paper.

Can I submit to several journals at once?

No. Simultaneous submission to more than one journal is considered misconduct at almost every journal. Submit to one, wait for a decision, and move to the next on your list if needed.

How long does it take a journal to make a decision?

It varies widely by field and journal, from a few weeks to several months. Many journals publish their median time to first decision on their website; check it if timing matters to you.

Is it bad to publish in an open access journal?

No. Many highly respected journals are open access. The issue is predatory journals that charge fees without proper peer review. Check that an open access journal is listed in DOAJ and run the Think. Check. Submit. checklist.
