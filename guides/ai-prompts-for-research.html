AI prompts for research: 20 patterns, each with a check

A good research prompt does two things: it gets a useful answer, and it makes that answer easy to verify. This guide gives you 20 prompt patterns for each stage of research, and pairs every one with the check you should run before you rely on the output.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Give the AI a role, a task, the sources to use and the output format, and ask it to cite a source for every claim and say when it does not know. Then check: open every citation, confirm it says what the answer claims, and compare the answer with at least one source you found yourself. Prompts work best in tools that search real literature rather than answering from memory.

The anatomy of a research prompt

Most weak prompts are a single vague question. Strong research prompts have five parts:

PartExample
Context“I am writing a master’s literature review on sleep and social media use in adolescents.”
Task“Summarise what longitudinal studies have found since 2018.”
Sources“Use only peer-reviewed studies” or “use only the PDFs I attached.”
Format“A table with study, design, sample, finding and page number.”
Guardrails“Cite a source for every claim. If evidence is thin or mixed, say so. Do not invent references.”

The guardrails help, but they do not guarantee anything. A general chatbot told “do not invent references” can still invent them, because it is generating text, not retrieving records. That is why every pattern below ends with a check. Our guide on spotting hallucinated citations explains the failure in detail.

Scoping a topic

  1. Map the field. “What are the main debates and research questions in [topic] since [year]? Group them into three to five themes and cite two representative papers for each.”
    Check: open the papers; confirm each one belongs in its theme.
  2. Learn the vocabulary. “List the terms and synonyms researchers use for [concept], including older terms and spelling variants.”
    Check: search two of the terms in a database and confirm they return relevant papers.
  3. Find landmark papers. “Which highly cited papers are most often treated as foundational for [topic]? Give citation details and DOIs.”
    Check: resolve every DOI; compare against citation counts in a database.
  4. Sharpen a question. “Here is my research question: [question]. Suggest three narrower versions that are answerable in a [length] project, and explain the trade-off of each.”
    Check: run a quick search for each version to see whether evidence exists.

Searching the literature

  1. Draft a search string. “Turn this question into a Boolean search string for PubMed with MeSH terms and title/abstract synonyms: [question].”
    Check: run it, read 30 titles, and confirm known papers appear. Our Boolean operators guide shows how to fix it.
  2. Find more like this. “Find studies similar to [paper title], especially ones that reach a different conclusion.”
    Check: read the abstracts; “different conclusion” is often overstated.
  3. Find counter-evidence. “What is the strongest published evidence against the claim that [claim]?”
    Check: look at study design; a single small study is not strong evidence.
  4. Check recency. “What has been published on [topic] in the last 12 months?”
    Check: confirm dates in the records; preprints should be labelled as such.

Reading and summarising papers

  1. Structured summary. “From the attached paper only, give the aim, design, sample, measures, main findings and limitations, each with a page number.”
    Check: verify findings and sample size against the page cited.
  2. Explain a method. “Explain the statistical method in section 3 of this paper as if I know basic statistics but not this technique.”
    Check: compare with a textbook or methods paper.
  3. Extract for a matrix. “For each attached paper, fill these columns: [columns]. Write ‘not reported’ where the paper does not say.”
    Check: spot-check one row in three. See our synthesis matrix guide.
  4. Compare two papers. “Compare the methods and findings of paper A and paper B. Why might their results differ?”
    Check: confirm each difference you plan to mention in both papers.

Critique and appraisal

  1. Find weaknesses. “Act as a critical peer reviewer. List the three most serious threats to validity in this study and explain why.”
    Check: decide yourself whether each threat is real and serious.
  2. Appraise with a checklist. “Appraise this randomised trial using the main domains of the Cochrane risk of bias tool. Quote the passages you base each judgement on.”
    Check: read the quoted passages; judgements must be yours in a formal review.
  3. Test a claim. “Does the evidence in these papers support the sentence: [your sentence]? Answer supports, partly supports or does not support, with quotes.”
    Check: read the quotes in context.

Planning and drafting

  1. Outline from notes. “Here are my notes and themes. Suggest an outline for a literature review section that moves from agreement to disagreement to gap.”
    Check: make sure the outline follows your argument, not a generic template.
  2. Find missing citations. “Which sentences in this paragraph make claims that need a citation?”
    Check: add sources you have read, not ones you have not.
  3. Plain-language version. “Rewrite this abstract for a non-specialist reader in 120 words without losing the main caveat.”
    Check: confirm the caveat survived.
  4. Reviewer simulation. “What questions would an examiner ask about this chapter?”
    Check: answer them yourself, in writing.
  5. Consistency check. “List any numbers, terms or claims in this draft that are inconsistent with each other.”
    Check: fix in the source document, then re-run.

A worked session: one hour, one topic

Here is how the patterns fit together for a student starting a review on mindfulness apps for student anxiety.

  1. Minutes 0–10, scope (patterns 1 and 2). The answer groups the literature into efficacy trials, engagement and dropout, and comparisons with face-to-face programmes, and lists terms such as “mHealth”, “digital mindfulness” and “smartphone-based intervention”. The student opens the six cited papers: five fit their themes, one is about adults in the workplace and is set aside.
  2. Minutes 10–25, search (pattern 5). The student asks for a PubMed string, runs it, and finds 240 results. Two known papers are missing because the string lacks “university students”. Adding it fixes the gap.
  3. Minutes 25–45, read (patterns 9 and 11). Eight PDFs go in with a request for a matrix: design, sample, app, duration, anxiety measure, main finding, dropout. The student checks every third row against the paper and corrects one sample size.
  4. Minutes 45–55, critique (pattern 13). The tool flags high dropout and short follow-up across most trials. The student confirms these from the papers and notes them as a theme.
  5. Minutes 55–60, plan (pattern 16). A suggested outline moves from short-term benefits to the dropout problem to the gap in long-term follow-up. The student rewrites it in their own words.

Nothing in that hour produced text for submission. It produced a checked set of sources, a matrix and an outline, which is where the student’s own writing begins.

Prompts to avoid

  • “Write my literature review on X.” You get generic text you cannot defend, and in most institutions submitting it breaks the rules. Read our guide to AI policy in academic writing.
  • “Give me 10 references for this sentence.” This invites the model to fit citations to a claim rather than letting evidence shape the claim.
  • “Is this true?” without sources. A yes or no with no evidence tells you nothing.
  • Pasting confidential data into a tool whose data policy you have not read.

Verify outside the AI: to confirm a reference exists, search its title or DOI in Crossref or PubMed. Do not ask the same AI whether its own reference is real.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good AI prompt for research?

A good research prompt states your context, the task, which sources to use, the output format, and guardrails such as citing every claim and admitting uncertainty. For example: summarise longitudinal studies on X since 2018 in a table with design, sample, finding and page number, citing each study.

Can ChatGPT find research papers?

It can suggest papers, and versions with web search can find real ones, but it can also produce references that look real and do not exist. Use a tool that searches scholarly databases, and confirm every reference in Crossref, PubMed or the publisher site.

How do I stop AI making up sources?

Use a tool that retrieves records from scholarly indexes rather than answering from memory, restrict it to your own uploaded PDFs where possible, and check every citation yourself. Instructions like do not invent references reduce the problem in chatbots but do not remove it.

Is it allowed to use AI prompts for academic research?

Using AI to search, summarise and critique is allowed in many institutions, while submitting AI-written text as your own often is not. Rules differ, so check your course, university or journal policy and disclose how you used AI where required.
