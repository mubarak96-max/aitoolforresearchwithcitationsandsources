Methods · Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis steps: the six phases, with a worked example

Thematic analysis is the most widely used way to analyse qualitative data such as interviews, focus groups and open survey answers. It is flexible, which is its strength and its risk: without a clear process, it slides into a list of topics people mentioned.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Braun and Clarke’s six phases are: (1) familiarise yourself with the data, (2) code it systematically, (3) generate initial themes from the codes, (4) develop and review themes against the coded extracts and the whole dataset, (5) refine, define and name each theme, and (6) write the report with vivid extracts and an analytic story. The phases are recursive: you move back and forth between them.

What thematic analysis is

Thematic analysis is a method for identifying, analysing and reporting patterns of meaning (themes) across a dataset. It was set out as a distinct method by Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke in their 2006 paper “Using thematic analysis in psychology”, one of the most cited methods papers in the social sciences. It is not tied to one theory, so it can be used across psychology, health, education, business and more.

If you have not yet collected your data, our guide to creating an interview guide helps you design interviews that will give you material worth analysing.

Choose your approach first

“Thematic analysis” covers several approaches that differ in how they treat coding and the researcher’s role. Decide which you are using and say so in your methods section.

ApproachKey ideaTypical features
ReflexiveThemes are built by the researcher through interpretationOrganic, evolving codes; researcher subjectivity treated as a resource; no inter-rater agreement scores
CodebookA structured codebook guides analysis, often in teamsCodebook developed early; themes often partly set in advance; common in applied research
Coding reliabilityCoding should be consistent across codersFixed codebook; multiple coders; agreement measured, e.g. with Cohen’s kappa

You also choose an orientation: inductive (themes grow from the data) or deductive (theory or prior research guides what you look for), and semantic (surface meaning) or latent (underlying assumptions and ideas). Most studies combine elements. The AI for Research guide to coding qualitative data explains these choices with examples.

Braun and Clarke later clarified their own position in “Reflecting on reflexive thematic analysis” (2019), which is worth reading if you plan to cite them for a reflexive approach.

The six phases

The phase names below follow Braun and Clarke’s more recent wording, with the 2006 names in brackets.

Phase 1: Familiarisation

Read and re-read the transcripts. Listen to the recordings if you have them. Make brief notes on initial ideas, surprises and questions. Do not code yet. If you did the transcription yourself, you have already started this phase.

Phase 2: Coding (generating initial codes)

Work through the whole dataset systematically, labelling segments that are relevant to your research question. A code is a short, specific label that captures what is interesting about the extract, such as “mentor available but too busy” rather than “mentoring”. Code everything relevant, not just what supports early ideas. One extract can carry several codes.

Phase 3: Generating initial themes (searching for themes)

Group codes that share a core idea into candidate themes. Draw a thematic map: themes, possible sub-themes and the codes beneath them. Keep a “miscellaneous” pile for codes that do not fit yet.

Phase 4: Developing and reviewing themes

Check each candidate theme at two levels. First, do the coded extracts under it fit together coherently? Second, does the theme make sense across the whole dataset and answer your question? Expect to merge, split, discard and rebuild themes here.

Phase 5: Refining, defining and naming themes

Write a short definition for each theme: what it is about, its central organising concept, and its boundaries. Give it a concise, informative name. If you cannot describe a theme in a couple of sentences, it is not yet clear.

Phase 6: Writing up (producing the report)

Write an analytic narrative, theme by theme, using vivid extracts as evidence and explaining what they mean in relation to your question and the literature. Extracts illustrate your argument; they do not replace it.

The phases are recursive. You will move back from phase 4 to phase 2 to recode, or from writing up to renaming a theme. That is the method working, not failing.

A worked example

Research question: How do early-career teachers experience their first year in school?

ExtractCodes
“My mentor was lovely but I’d see her for ten minutes on a Friday, if that.”Mentor kind but unavailable; support squeezed by time
“You plan till eleven, then the photocopier’s broken at eight.”Late-night planning; small failures add up
“The other new teachers were the ones who really got me through.”Peer support replaces formal support
“I didn’t want to look like I couldn’t cope, so I didn’t ask.”Hiding struggle; fear of being judged

Candidate themes after phase 3 might be “Support on paper, not in practice” (codes about mentors and formal schemes), “Survival networks” (peer support), and “Performing coping” (hiding struggle, fear of judgement). In phase 4 you might find that “Performing coping” explains why formal support goes unused, and restructure the analysis so it becomes the central theme, with the others as related themes. That is an interpretation, not a summary: it tells the reader something the participants did not say in so many words.

What counts as a theme

  • It has a central organising concept: a single idea that unites the extracts.
  • It is about meaning, not just a topic. “Workload” is a topic; “Workload as proof of commitment” is a theme.
  • It is relevant to the research question.
  • It is supported across the data, though frequency alone does not make something important.
  • It is distinct from the other themes, with clear boundaries.

Most reports present between two and six main themes. Many more usually means themes have not been developed enough.

Common mistakes

  • Using interview questions as themes. If your themes match your guide’s sections, you have organised the data, not analysed it.
  • Topic summaries. Themes that say “participants discussed X” without saying what about X.
  • Too many extracts, too little analysis. Each extract should be followed by interpretation.
  • Mixing approaches. Claiming a reflexive approach and then reporting inter-rater kappa, or the reverse.
  • Saying themes “emerged”. Themes are developed by the researcher. Describe what you did.
  • No audit trail. Keep versions of codebooks and thematic maps, and memos explaining changes.

Using AI in thematic analysis

AI can speed up the mechanical parts: suggesting initial codes, finding all extracts that mention a concept, or summarising a long transcript so you can navigate it. It cannot do the interpretive work that makes a theme, and it can sound plausible while misreading context, sarcasm or culturally specific meaning. Check your ethics approval and data agreements before uploading transcripts to any tool, and report exactly how AI was used. Our guide to AI rules in academic writing covers disclosure.

Frequently asked questions

What are the six steps of thematic analysis?

Braun and Clarke’s six phases are familiarisation with the data, coding, generating initial themes, developing and reviewing themes, refining, defining and naming themes, and writing up. The 2006 paper called them familiarising, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report.

What is the difference between a code and a theme?

A code labels a single interesting feature of a data extract. A theme is a broader pattern of shared meaning built from several codes and organised around a central concept that helps answer the research question.

How many themes should a thematic analysis have?

There is no fixed number, but most reports present between two and six main themes, sometimes with sub-themes. Fewer, well-developed themes are usually stronger than a long list of thin ones.

Do I need two coders for thematic analysis?

It depends on the approach. Coding reliability approaches use multiple coders and measure agreement. Reflexive thematic analysis does not require a second coder or agreement scores, though discussing codes with a supervisor or colleague can help reflexivity.

Can I use software for thematic analysis?

Yes. Qualitative analysis software and AI-assisted coding tools help organise codes, extracts and themes. They do not perform the analysis for you: interpretation, theme development and the written argument remain the researcher’s responsibility.
