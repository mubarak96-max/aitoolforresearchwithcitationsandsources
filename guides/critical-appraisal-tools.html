Critical appraisal tools: choosing the right checklist for each study

Critical appraisal means judging whether a study’s results are trustworthy, meaningful and relevant to your question. Free checklists make that systematic. The skill is choosing the one that fits the study design, then using it as a prompt for judgement rather than a score.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Match the tool to the study design. For most designs, the free CASP and JBI checklists are the standard starting points. Use AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews, MMAT when a review includes qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies, and formal risk of bias tools such as RoB 2 or ROBINS-I when you are conducting a systematic review of effects. Answer each question with reasons and page references, and avoid adding up “yes” answers into a single score.

What critical appraisal is, and is not

Critical appraisal asks three questions of every study:

  1. Are the results valid? Was the design appropriate, and were bias and confounding handled?
  2. What are the results? How large is the effect, and how precise?
  3. Are the results useful here? Do the participants, setting and outcomes match your question?

It is easy to mix appraisal up with two related activities:

A well-reported study can still be badly biased, and a poorly reported one may be sound but impossible to judge. Keep the three separate in your notes. Our guide to risk of bias assessment covers the formal tools in detail.

The main appraisal toolkits

CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme)

The CASP checklists are short, free and widely taught in health and social care courses. Each has about 10 to 12 questions grouped around validity, results and applicability, with hints under each question. There are versions for randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews, cohort studies, case–control studies, qualitative studies, diagnostic studies, economic evaluations and clinical prediction rules. Answers are “yes”, “no” or “can’t tell”.

JBI critical appraisal tools

The JBI tools, from the Joanna Briggs Institute at the University of Adelaide, cover a wider range of designs, including analytical cross-sectional studies, case series, case reports, prevalence studies, quasi-experimental studies, text and opinion papers, and qualitative research. They are popular in nursing, allied health and evidence synthesis, and they are designed for use in JBI-style systematic reviews.

CEBM worksheets

The Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine at Oxford publishes critical appraisal worksheets for therapy, diagnosis, prognosis, harm and systematic reviews. They lean towards the clinical question “should this change what I do for my patient?”, which makes them good for journal clubs and evidence-based practice assignments.

AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews

AMSTAR 2 appraises systematic reviews that include randomised or non-randomised studies of interventions. It has 16 items, seven of which its authors flag as critical, such as a registered protocol, a comprehensive search and an appropriate risk of bias assessment. It rates overall confidence in a review as high, moderate, low or critically low, rather than producing a score. Use it when you are doing an umbrella review or deciding which existing review to trust.

MMAT for mixed collections

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) appraises qualitative, quantitative randomised, quantitative non-randomised, quantitative descriptive and mixed-methods studies with one framework. It suits reviews in education, public health and social science where designs vary widely.

Not sure what design a paper is? Look at how participants were assigned and when outcomes were measured, not at what the authors call it. Papers labelled “pilot RCT” or “cohort” are sometimes neither. The AI for Research team’s guide to levels of evidence and study types helps you place a study before you choose a checklist.

How to appraise a paper in seven steps

  1. Write down your question in a structured form (for example PICO: population, intervention, comparison, outcome). Relevance is judged against it.
  2. Identify the design and pick the matching checklist.
  3. Read the methods before the results. Judge validity before you know whether the findings are exciting.
  4. Answer each question with a reason and a page number, for example “Can’t tell: allocation concealment not described (p. 4)”. A bare “yes” is impossible to check later.
  5. Look at the results as effect sizes with confidence intervals, not only p-values. See our guide to effect sizes.
  6. Judge applicability: would your population, setting and resources produce similar results?
  7. Write a two- or three-sentence overall judgement naming the main strengths and the weaknesses that matter most for your question.

Avoid summing scores. Adding up “yes” answers treats a missing ethics statement as equal to failed randomisation. Many tool developers advise against a numeric total; the AMSTAR 2 authors, for example, say explicitly that it is not meant to produce an overall score. Report judgements per question and an overall rating with reasons.

Using appraisal in your writing

Appraisal is only useful if it changes what you write. In a literature review or dissertation:

  • Weight your synthesis by quality: say what the strongest studies show before mentioning weaker ones.
  • Explain disagreements between studies through design differences when you can (“the two trials with blinded outcome assessment found smaller effects”).
  • Put a summary appraisal table in an appendix, with one row per study and one column per key question.
  • In a systematic review, carry the appraisal into a certainty rating such as GRADE for each outcome.

Can AI do critical appraisal?

AI can speed up the mechanical part: finding where a paper reports randomisation, blinding, attrition or the sample size calculation, and drafting a first pass at each checklist question. It should not make the final judgement. Appraisal depends on context (is 15% attrition a problem for this outcome?) and on what the paper does not say, which language models handle poorly. Treat AI answers as page-referenced pointers to check, the same way you would treat a co-reviewer’s notes.

Common appraisal mistakes

  • Using an RCT checklist on a non-randomised study because it is the one you know.
  • Marking “yes” when the paper is silent; silence is “can’t tell”.
  • Judging a study by its journal, sample size or author reputation instead of its methods.
  • Ignoring conflicts of interest and funding sources.
  • Appraising validity but never asking whether the results apply to your setting.
  • Doing appraisal alone when it will be published; in a systematic review, two reviewers should appraise independently and resolve disagreements.

For a full systematic review, the formal tools and two-reviewer process matter more. See systematic review software compared for tools that record both reviewers’ judgements.

Frequently asked questions

What is a critical appraisal tool?

A critical appraisal tool is a structured checklist that helps you judge whether a study’s results are valid, what they show and whether they apply to your situation. Examples include the CASP and JBI checklists, CEBM worksheets, AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews and the MMAT for mixed study designs.

What is the difference between CASP and JBI checklists?

Both are free and widely used. CASP checklists are short and popular in teaching, with versions for the main study designs. JBI tools cover a wider range of designs, including prevalence studies, case series and text and opinion papers, and are built for JBI-style systematic reviews.

Is critical appraisal the same as risk of bias assessment?

No. Critical appraisal is broader: it covers validity, results and relevance. Risk of bias assessment focuses on whether a specific result is likely to be systematically wrong, using formal tools such as RoB 2 for randomised trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies.

Should I give each study a quality score?

Generally no. Summing yes answers gives equal weight to minor and major problems. Report the judgement for each question, then an overall rating with the reasons that matter most for your review question.

Which appraisal tool should I use for a qualitative study?

The CASP qualitative checklist and the JBI checklist for qualitative research are the most common choices. In a qualitative evidence synthesis, combine them with GRADE-CERQual to rate confidence in each review finding.
