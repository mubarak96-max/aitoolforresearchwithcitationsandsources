How to peer review a paper: a method you can repeat

Your first review invitation usually arrives with no training attached. This guide gives you a three-pass reading method, a checklist for each part of the manuscript, and a report structure editors find easy to act on.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Read the paper three times with different goals. First, decide what it claims and whether the claim matters. Second, check whether the methods and results can support that claim. Third, note smaller problems with presentation and references. Then write a report that summarises the paper, lists major and minor concerns separately, and gives the editor a clear recommendation in a confidential note. Be specific, be kind, and never use the manuscript for your own work.

Before you accept the invitation

Three questions decide whether you should say yes.

  • Do you have the expertise? You do not need to know every method in the paper, but you should be able to judge its central claim. If you can only judge part of it, accept and tell the editor which part.
  • Do you have a conflict of interest? Recent collaboration with the authors, a competing project, or a personal relationship are the usual ones. Declare anything that might look like a conflict and let the editor decide.
  • Can you meet the deadline? Two to three weeks is common. A late review delays someone’s career. If you cannot do it on time, decline quickly and suggest another reviewer.

The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) publishes ethical guidelines for peer reviewers that most journals follow. They take ten minutes to read and cover confidentiality, conflicts and the use of manuscripts. Read them before your first review.

The three-pass reading method

Pass 1: the big picture (30 minutes)

Read the title, abstract, introduction, figures and conclusion. Do not take notes on details yet. Answer four questions in one sentence each:

  1. What question does the paper ask?
  2. What is the main claim?
  3. What evidence is offered for it?
  4. If the claim is true, why does it matter to this journal’s readers?

If you cannot answer the first two after a careful read, that is already your most important comment: the paper does not state its contribution clearly.

Pass 2: the evidence (2 to 3 hours)

Now read the methods and results line by line. This is where most of the value of a review lies. Ask whether the design can answer the question, whether the analysis matches the design, and whether the conclusions go further than the results allow. Use the checklist in the next section.

Pass 3: presentation and references (45 minutes)

Read once more for clarity, figure quality, missing references and consistency between text, tables and abstract. Check a handful of the key citations to see whether they say what the authors claim. Note typos only if they change meaning; copy-editors handle the rest.

What to check, section by section

SectionQuestions to ask
IntroductionIs the gap real and supported by recent literature? Are important prior studies missing? Is the aim stated clearly?
MethodsCould another team repeat the study from this description? Is the sample size justified? Are inclusion criteria, measures and analysis plans stated before the results?
StatisticsDo the tests fit the data type and design? Are effect sizes and confidence intervals reported, not only p-values? Were multiple comparisons handled?
ResultsDo numbers match across text, tables and figures? Are all outcomes from the methods reported, including null results?
DiscussionDo the conclusions follow from the results? Are limitations honest and specific? Is causation claimed from correlational data?
Ethics and dataIs ethical approval and consent described? Is there a data availability statement, and is it adequate?
ReportingDoes the paper follow the reporting guideline for its design, such as CONSORT for trials or PRISMA for systematic reviews?

The EQUATOR Network lists the reporting guideline for almost every study design. Opening the right checklist next to the manuscript is the fastest way to find what is missing.

How to structure the report

Editors read many reviews. The easiest ones to act on follow the same shape every time.

1. Summary (one paragraph)

Restate the question, the approach and the main finding in your own words. This proves to the authors and editor that you understood the paper, and it often reveals where the paper was unclear.

2. Overall assessment (one paragraph)

Say what the paper does well and whether its main claim is supported. Be honest but fair: name the strongest part of the work before the weakest.

3. Major concerns (numbered)

Problems that would change the conclusions if they are not fixed: a design flaw, a missing control, an inappropriate analysis, a claim that goes beyond the data. For each one, explain the problem, why it matters and what would resolve it.

4. Minor concerns (numbered)

Clarity, missing details, figure labels, references to add. Give page and line numbers so the authors can find each one.

5. Confidential comments to the editor

Your recommendation (accept, minor revision, major revision, reject) and anything you would not say to the authors, such as a suspected ethical problem. Do not put the recommendation in the comments to authors unless the journal asks you to.

Summary: This manuscript asks whether ... Using ..., the authors find ...

Overall: The question is important and the data set is unusually large.
However, the main conclusion depends on an analysis that does not
account for ...

Major concerns
1. (p. 6, lines 112–130) The comparison groups differ in ... Please
   report ... or add ... as a covariate.
2. ...

Minor concerns
1. (Fig. 2) Axis units are missing.
2. ...

Tone: how to be critical without being harsh

Write as if you were sitting across the table from the authors. Criticise the work, never the people. Replace “the authors clearly do not understand” with “the analysis in section 3 does not account for”. Suggest a fix with every major concern. Avoid demanding citations of your own papers unless they are genuinely essential, and if you do suggest them, say so in the note to the editor.

Remember that early-career researchers often read reviews alone, late at night. A specific, respectful review helps them improve the paper. A dismissive one teaches nothing.

Can you use AI to help with a review?

Be careful. The manuscript is confidential. Uploading it to a public chatbot can break that confidentiality, and many journals and funders now forbid it outright. Check the journal’s policy first. If AI use is allowed, it is safest to use it on your own notes, for example to tidy the structure of your report, rather than on the manuscript.

Where AI is genuinely useful is before submission, on your own work. Running your own draft through a structured critique catches the issues reviewers will raise. Our guide on AI rules in academic writing covers what is usually allowed.

Choosing a recommendation

The editor makes the decision, but your recommendation guides it. Match it to your major concerns, not to your overall impression.

RecommendationWhen to use it
AcceptRare at first review. Only when no changes beyond typos are needed.
Minor revisionThe conclusions stand. Fixes are about clarity, missing detail or small extra analyses the authors can do with existing data.
Major revisionThe paper could be publishable, but at least one concern could change the conclusions: a new analysis, a reframed claim, or substantial missing information.
RejectA fatal flaw in design that revision cannot fix, a claim the data cannot support, or a paper outside the journal’s scope.

If you recommend rejection, the comments to the authors should still help them improve the work for another journal. Explain the flaw clearly and say what a stronger version of the study would look like.

Common mistakes new reviewers make

  • Rewriting the paper they would have written, instead of judging the one in front of them.
  • Listing twenty minor points and missing the one design flaw that matters.
  • Asking for new experiments that are outside the paper’s scope.
  • Giving a recommendation that contradicts the comments (“minor revision” with three fatal flaws).
  • Not checking whether the statistics match the design. See our guide to reading papers efficiently for a quick methods check.
  • Sharing the manuscript with a student “for training” without the editor’s permission.

Revisions: when a revised version comes back, check only whether your concerns were addressed and whether the changes introduced new problems. Raising brand-new major issues at the second round is frustrating for authors unless the revision itself created them.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a peer review report be?

Most useful reports are one to two pages, roughly 500 to 1,500 words. Length matters less than whether each major concern is specific, explained and paired with a way to fix it.

How long does it take to peer review a paper?

Plan for four to eight hours for a typical research article, spread over a few days. Your first reviews will take longer. Most journals give two to three weeks.

Can I decline a peer review request?

Yes. Decline promptly if you lack the expertise, have a conflict of interest or cannot meet the deadline. Suggesting another qualified reviewer is a helpful courtesy.

Can I ask a student to help review a paper?

Only with the editor’s permission, because the manuscript is confidential. Many journals allow co-reviewing if you name the co-reviewer, and it is a good way to train early-career researchers.

Do peer reviewers get paid?

Usually not. Most journal peer review is unpaid. Some publishers offer discounts or recognition, and you can record reviews on your researcher profile, for example through ORCID.
