H-index and research impact metrics, explained

Job applications, promotion files and grant forms ask for numbers. Each number measures something narrower than it seems. This guide explains how the common metrics are calculated, what they can tell you, and how to present your impact honestly.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer The h-index is the largest number h such that a researcher has h papers with at least h citations each. The journal impact factor is the average number of citations in one year to a journal’s items from the previous two years. Field-normalised scores compare a paper’s citations with similar papers in the same field and year. Altmetrics count attention outside academia. None of them measures quality directly, and all of them vary by field, career stage and database, so use them together with a narrative of what your work changed.

Why metrics need care

Citation counts are easy to compute and easy to compare, which is why they spread. But a citation only shows that someone referred to a paper. It does not show whether they agreed with it, built on it or criticised it. Citation habits also differ widely between fields: a typical paper in cell biology is cited far more often than a typical paper in mathematics or history.

Two widely endorsed statements set out how metrics should and should not be used. The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA) asks funders and institutions not to use journal-based metrics such as the impact factor as a surrogate for the quality of individual articles or researchers. The Leiden Manifesto (Hicks et al., 2015) gives ten principles for responsible metrics, including that quantitative evaluation should support, not replace, expert judgement.

Author-level metrics

The h-index

Proposed by physicist Jorge Hirsch in PNAS in 2005, the h-index combines productivity and citations in one number. A researcher has an h-index of h if h of their papers have each been cited at least h times.

How to calculate it: list your papers from most to least cited. Count down the list until the position number is larger than the paper’s citation count. The last position where citations were at least equal to the position is your h-index.

Limitations: the h-index can only grow with time, so it favours senior researchers. It ignores how highly cited your best papers are (a paper with 48 citations and one with 500 count the same). It does not account for author position or the number of co-authors, and it differs between fields. Your h-index will also differ between databases, because each indexes a different set of publications and citations.

The i10-index

Used by Google Scholar, the i10-index is the number of your publications with at least ten citations. It is simple, but shares most of the h-index’s problems and is rarely used outside Google Scholar profiles.

Total citations and citations per paper

Total citations are dominated by a few highly cited papers, especially reviews and methods papers. Citations per paper penalises researchers who publish many short pieces. Both are more meaningful when compared with researchers in the same field and at the same career stage.

Journal-level metrics

Journal impact factor

The journal impact factor for a given year is the number of citations received that year by items the journal published in the previous two years, divided by the number of citable items (usually research articles and reviews) it published in those two years. A journal with an impact factor of 4 received, on average, four citations in that year for each citable item from the previous two years.

The problem is that citations within a journal are highly skewed: a small share of papers receive most of the citations, so the average says little about any single paper. That is why DORA asks assessors not to judge individual papers by their journal’s impact factor.

Other journal metrics

Alternatives use longer citation windows, weight citations by the prestige of the citing journal, or normalise for field. They are useful for comparing journals within a field when you are choosing where to publish, but they share the same basic limitation: they describe journals, not papers or people.

Field-normalised citation metrics

Field-normalised metrics compare a paper’s citations with the average for papers of the same type, field and publication year. A score of 1.0 means the paper is cited as often as the world average for similar papers; 2.0 means twice as often. These metrics make fairer comparisons across fields and career stages, and they are increasingly used in institutional reports. Their accuracy depends on how fields are defined, which matters most for interdisciplinary work.

Altmetrics: attention beyond citations

Altmetrics track mentions of research in news stories, policy documents, social media, blogs, reference managers and patents. They appear within days of publication, long before citations accumulate, and they capture forms of impact citations miss, such as use in policy or practice.

Treat them as signals of attention, not quality. A paper can attract attention because it is controversial or wrong. Mentions in policy documents and clinical guidelines are usually more meaningful evidence of real-world use than social media counts.

Where to find your numbers

Author metrics come from citation databases. Some are subscription services your library may provide; others are free. Free options include public author profiles on Google Scholar and open bibliographic databases such as OpenAlex, which indexes hundreds of millions of works and makes its data openly available.

Whichever you use, check your profile for errors first. Common problems are papers by someone with a similar name merged into your record, your own papers split across two profiles, and missing conference papers or book chapters. Fix these before you report any number, and record the date you took the figures, because they change every week.

How to improve your impact (honestly)

The reliable way to be cited more is to be found and be useful.

  • Create an ORCID iD and link it to every publication so your work is attributed correctly across databases.
  • Share preprints and accepted manuscripts where the journal allows, so people without subscriptions can read your work.
  • Write titles and abstracts that use the terms people in your field search for.
  • Share data and code. Reusable resources are cited for years.
  • Present at conferences and write plain-language summaries for practitioners. Our guide to conference presentations can help.

Do not game it. Excessive self-citation, citation rings and coercive citation requests from reviewers or editors are treated as misconduct by many institutions and publishers. They are also increasingly easy to detect.

How to present impact in an application

Many funders and institutions now prefer a narrative CV that describes contributions in words. Even where they ask for numbers, the strongest applications put numbers in context:

  1. State the metric and the database and date you took it from.
  2. Compare with your field and career stage, for example with field-normalised scores, rather than giving a raw number.
  3. Describe two or three specific contributions and what they changed: a method others adopted, a data set reused, a finding cited in a guideline.
  4. Include contributions metrics miss: software, data, mentoring, peer review, public engagement.

If you are early in your career, a short, specific narrative almost always serves you better than a small h-index. See our PhD research toolkit for building a track record during a doctorate.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good h-index?

It depends entirely on field and career stage. An h-index that is high in mathematics may be modest in biomedicine, and any h-index grows with time. Compare only with researchers in the same field and at a similar career stage, ideally using field-normalised metrics.

Why is my h-index different on different websites?

Each database indexes a different set of publications and citations. Google Scholar usually includes more sources, such as theses and preprints, so it tends to show higher counts than curated databases. Always say which source and date you used.

What is the difference between h-index and impact factor?

The h-index describes a researcher’s publications and their citations. The impact factor describes a journal: the average citations in one year to items it published in the previous two years. Neither directly measures quality.

Do self-citations count in the h-index?

In most databases they count by default, and some let you exclude them. Moderate self-citation is normal when you build on your own work, but excessive self-citation to inflate metrics is considered poor practice.

What are altmetrics?

Altmetrics measure attention to research outside traditional citations, such as mentions in news, policy documents, blogs and social media. They show early attention and real-world use but do not measure quality.
