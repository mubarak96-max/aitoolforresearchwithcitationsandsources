APA vs MLA vs Chicago vs Harvard: citation styles compared, with examples

Four styles, one article, every difference that matters. Plus IEEE and Vancouver for the sciences, and a quick way to decide which one you should be using.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202610 min read
Short answer APA (author-date) is standard in psychology, education and the social sciences. MLA (author-page) is used in literature and the humanities. Chicago has two systems: notes-bibliography for history and the arts, author-date for the sciences. Harvard is a family of author-date styles common in UK and Australian universities. The right one is whatever your course, department or journal specifies.

Which style should you use?

You rarely get to choose. Your style is set by, in order of priority:

  1. The journal’s author guidelines, if you are submitting a paper.
  2. Your module handbook or department, for coursework and theses.
  3. The convention in your field, if nobody has told you. Purdue OWL is a reliable free reference for the main styles.

Whichever style you use, citing consistently is also your main defence against accidental plagiarism; our guide to paraphrasing without plagiarism explains where the line sits.

One article in six styles

To make the differences visible, here is the same journal article in every style. The article is made up for illustration: two authors, a volume and issue, a page range and a DOI.

Example details: Amara N. Okafor and Maja Lindqvist, “Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year students”, Journal of Learning Research, volume 18, issue 2, pages 115–132, 2024, DOI 10.1234/jlr.2024.0182 (illustrative).

APA 7

Okafor, A. N., & Lindqvist, M. (2024). Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year students. Journal of Learning Research, 18(2), 115–132. https://doi.org/10.1234/jlr.2024.0182

In text: (Okafor & Lindqvist, 2024) or Okafor and Lindqvist (2024) found… Add a page for direct quotes: (Okafor & Lindqvist, 2024, p. 117).

Note the initials only, the ampersand, sentence case for the article title, title case and italics for the journal, and the italic volume number. The AI for Research APA citation generator handles these details from a DOI.

MLA 9

Okafor, Amara N., and Maja Lindqvist. “Sleep Duration and Exam Performance in First-Year Students.” Journal of Learning Research, vol. 18, no. 2, 2024, pp. 115–32, https://doi.org/10.1234/jlr.2024.0182.

In text: (Okafor and Lindqvist 117). MLA cites the page, not the year, and uses no comma between author and page.

Full first names, title case in quotation marks, “vol.” and “no.” labels, and a shortened final page number. The list is called “Works Cited”.

Chicago author-date

Okafor, Amara N., and Maja Lindqvist. 2024. “Sleep Duration and Exam Performance in First-Year Students.” Journal of Learning Research 18 (2): 115–32. https://doi.org/10.1234/jlr.2024.0182.

In text: (Okafor and Lindqvist 2024, 117).

The year moves up to just after the authors. In the notes-bibliography system, the same source would appear in a footnote such as: 1. Amara N. Okafor and Maja Lindqvist, “Sleep Duration and Exam Performance in First-Year Students,” Journal of Learning Research 18, no. 2 (2024): 117, https://doi.org/10.1234/jlr.2024.0182. Chicago’s 18th edition (2024) changed some details from the 17th, so follow your department’s edition.

Harvard (Cite Them Right version)

Okafor, A.N. and Lindqvist, M. (2024) ‘Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year students’, Journal of Learning Research, 18(2), pp. 115–132. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1234/jlr.2024.0182.

In text: (Okafor and Lindqvist, 2024) or (Okafor and Lindqvist, 2024, p. 117).

“Harvard” is not one standard. This example follows Cite Them Right, which many UK universities use, but your library may publish its own guide with small differences in punctuation. If your university uses Cite Them Right, the Harvard referencing generator follows it.

IEEE

[1] A. N. Okafor and M. Lindqvist, “Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year students,” J. Learn. Res., vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 115–132, 2024, doi: 10.1234/jlr.2024.0182.

In text: a bracketed number, such as “as shown in [1]”. References are numbered in the order they are first cited.

Vancouver

1. Okafor AN, Lindqvist M. Sleep duration and exam performance in first-year students. J Learn Res. 2024;18(2):115-32. doi:10.1234/jlr.2024.0182

In text: a number in superscript or brackets, depending on the journal. Note the absence of full stops in initials and the compact date, volume and page block.

Citing other source types

Journal articles are the easy case. Books, chapters, web pages and preprints cause most of the mistakes. Here they are in APA 7, with the MLA 9 version for the book, using made-up sources for illustration.

A book

APA 7: Okafor, A. N. (2023). Learning and sleep: A practical guide. Example Press.

MLA 9: Okafor, Amara N. Learning and Sleep: A Practical Guide. Example Press, 2023.

APA 7 no longer includes the publisher’s location. MLA puts the year at the end and uses title case.

A chapter in an edited book

APA 7: Lindqvist, M. (2022). Measuring sleep in field studies. In R. Patel (Ed.), Methods in sleep research (pp. 45–68). Example Press.

Cite the chapter author, not the editor, and give the chapter’s page range. Editors’ initials come before their surname.

A web page from an organisation

APA 7: National Sleep Council. (2024, March 5). Sleep advice for students. https://www.example.org/sleep-advice

When the author and the site name are the same, APA leaves the site name out. Add a retrieval date only for pages designed to change over time, such as live data dashboards.

A preprint

APA 7: Okafor, A. N., & Lindqvist, M. (2025). Napping and memory consolidation in students [Preprint]. PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.1234/example.preprint

Label it as a preprint and check, just before you submit, whether a peer-reviewed version has been published since. If it has, cite the published version.

Output from an AI tool

Some styles, including APA, publish guidance on citing generative AI output, usually treating the tool’s maker as the author and describing the prompt in your text. Rules differ by style and change often, so check the current guidance from your style’s publisher. Better still, cite the original papers the AI drew on, after reading them.

Common citation mistakes

  • Mixing styles, often after pasting references from different sources.
  • Every in-text citation must match a reference entry, and the reverse.
  • Missing or broken DOIs. Paste the DOI into a browser to check it resolves.
  • Wrong capitalisation: APA and Harvard use sentence case for article titles; MLA and Chicago use title case.
  • Citing a source you have not read, or one that does not exist. AI-generated references are a growing cause; see how to spot hallucinated citations.
  • Ordering the list wrongly: alphabetical for author-date styles, numerical for IEEE and Vancouver.

Switching styles without retyping

If you have to change style late, say a journal rejects you and the next one uses Vancouver, do not edit references by hand. Keep all sources in a reference manager or citation-aware editor from the start, cite with its tool rather than typing, and switch the style setting. Then spot-check ten entries against the new style guide; automated formatting is good but not perfect, especially for unusual source types like reports, datasets and preprints.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between APA and Harvard?

Both are author-date styles. APA 7 is one fixed standard published by the American Psychological Association; Harvard is a family of similar styles that varies by university. In practice, Harvard versions usually join authors with 'and' instead of an ampersand, put article titles in single quotation marks, and write 'Available at:' before a URL or DOI.

Which citation style is used most in science?

There is no single one. Psychology, education and many social sciences use APA. Medicine and health use Vancouver or AMA. Engineering and computer science use IEEE. Chemistry uses ACS. Many journals have their own house variant, so check the author guidelines.

Is Chicago the same as Turabian?

Almost. Turabian is a version of Chicago style adapted for students writing papers, theses and dissertations. The citation formats are essentially the same; Turabian adds guidance on formatting student papers.

Can I mix citation styles in one paper?

No. Use one style consistently for every in-text citation and every reference entry. Mixing styles is one of the most common reasons markers and editors return a paper for correction.

Do I need to include a DOI?

APA 7 asks for a DOI whenever one exists, written as a full https://doi.org/ link. MLA, Chicago and Harvard recommend including a DOI or URL for online sources. IEEE and Vancouver usually include the DOI at the end. Check your guidelines if in doubt.
