How to write survey questions that measure what you intend

A survey is only as good as its questions. Small wording choices, such as a loaded word, two ideas in one item, or a missing “don’t know”, can change the answers more than your sample size ever will. These rules, with examples, help you avoid the usual traps.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Start from what you need to measure, not from questions you like. Use short, specific, neutral wording; ask about one thing per question; give a time frame; offer balanced, mutually exclusive answer options; and order questions from general to specific, with sensitive items later. Then pilot the questionnaire with five to ten people from your target group before you launch.

Start from what you need to measure

Before you write a single question, list the concepts your research question needs, and how you will analyse each one. For example, if your question is whether commuting time is associated with job satisfaction, you need a measure of commuting time, a measure of job satisfaction, and a few control variables such as hours worked. Each concept becomes one or more questions. Anything that does not map to the analysis plan should be cut.

Where a validated scale already exists for a concept, such as wellbeing, anxiety or self-efficacy, use it rather than writing your own. Validated scales come with evidence of reliability and let you compare your results with other studies. Cite the source and keep the wording and scale exactly as published.

12 rules, with bad and good examples

1. Ask about one thing at a time

Bad: How satisfied are you with your salary and benefits?
Good: Two questions, one on salary and one on benefits. A “double-barrelled” question gives you an answer you cannot interpret.

2. Use neutral wording

Bad: Do you agree that the excellent new library has improved studying?
Good: How, if at all, has the new library changed the way you study?

3. Avoid leading assumptions

Bad: How often do you exercise to manage stress?
Good: Ask how often people exercise, then ask separately about reasons.

4. Be specific about time

Bad: Do you often drink coffee?
Good: In the past 7 days, on how many days did you drink coffee?

5. Use words your respondents use

Replace jargon (“modality”, “remuneration”, “comorbidity”) with everyday words, unless your sample is specialists who use them.

6. Keep it short

Aim for questions under about 20 words. If you need a definition, put it before the question, not inside it.

7. Avoid double negatives

Bad: Do you disagree that students should not pay for parking?
Good: Should students pay for campus parking?

8. Make answer options exhaustive and exclusive

Bad: 0–5, 5–10, 10–20 hours. (Where does 5 go? What about 25?)
Good: 0–4, 5–9, 10–19, 20 or more hours.

9. Offer “don’t know” or “prefer not to say” where they are real answers

Forcing a choice on someone who has no opinion produces noise. For sensitive items such as income, a “prefer not to say” option usually reduces dropouts.

10. Balance your scales

Bad: Poor, Fair, Good, Very good, Excellent (one negative, four positive).
Good: Very poor, Poor, Neither, Good, Very good.

11. Ask sensitive questions carefully

Normalise the behaviour (“Many people find it hard to…”), use ranges rather than exact figures, and place these items later, after trust is built.

12. Do not ask what you can find elsewhere

If administrative data already holds a variable, such as course enrolment, use that instead. Every question you remove raises completion rates.

The non-profit Pew Research Center’s guide to writing survey questions shows how much wording and order effects can shift results in real national surveys, and is worth reading alongside these rules.

Response scales

For rating questions, a few decisions matter:

  • Number of points. Five- and seven-point scales are the most common. More points rarely add useful precision for general audiences.
  • Midpoint. Include a neutral midpoint when neutrality is a real position. Leaving it out forces a lean, which can be useful but should be a deliberate choice.
  • Labels. Label every point, not just the ends. Fully labelled scales are easier to interpret.
  • Consistency. Keep the direction (negative to positive) the same throughout, so respondents do not have to re-read every scale.

How you analyse rating data depends on whether you treat it as ordinal or interval, and on whether items form a scale. Plan that before you collect data.

Question order and length

  1. Start easy and relevant. The first questions should be simple and clearly about the topic in your invitation.
  2. Go from general to specific. Asking about a specific issue first can colour answers to a general question that follows.
  3. Group by topic and use short section introductions.
  4. Put demographics and sensitive items near the end, unless you need them for screening or routing.
  5. Use routing (skip logic) so people only see questions that apply to them.
  6. Watch the length. Completion falls as surveys get longer. If it takes more than about 10–15 minutes, cut.

Check your numbers too: a well-worded survey with too few responses still cannot answer your question. Our guide to survey sample size shows how to work out how many responses you need.

Borrow from established surveys

You rarely need to write every question from scratch. Large, carefully tested surveys publish their questionnaires, and reusing their wording has two advantages: the questions have already been tested on thousands of people, and you can compare your results with theirs.

  • National and cross-national surveys. The European Social Survey publishes full questionnaires on attitudes, trust, wellbeing and more, in many languages. National statistics offices publish census and labour force questions, which are a good source for standard demographic items.
  • Validated scales. For psychological and health concepts, search the literature for a validated instrument, check its licence (some are free for research, some are not) and cite the original paper.
  • Previous studies in your area. Methods sections and appendices often include full instruments. Reusing them, with credit, makes your study easier to compare.

If you adapt a borrowed question, for example by changing the time frame or translating it, say so and pilot the new version: small changes can affect how people answer.

Pilot before you launch

A pilot is the cheapest quality check in research. Use two kinds:

  • Think-aloud interviews with three to five people from your target group: they read each question aloud and say what they think it means. Misunderstandings appear quickly.
  • A small soft launch to 10–30 people: check completion time, dropouts, skipped items, “other” answers that should have been options, and whether the data export works with your analysis.
  • Revise, and record what you changed and why. Examiners like to see this in the methods chapter.

If you are combining a survey with interviews, our guide to writing an interview guide shows how to design the qualitative part so the two sets of data answer the same question. And because surveys collect personal data, read informed consent for research before you launch.

Frequently asked questions

How do you write a good survey question?

Ask about one thing, in short and neutral words your respondents use, with a clear time frame. Give answer options that are balanced, cover every possibility and do not overlap, and include “don’t know” or “prefer not to say” where those are genuine answers. Then test the question with people from your target group.

How many questions should a survey have?

As few as your analysis needs. Many research surveys aim for 10–15 minutes to complete; beyond that, dropouts rise. Cut any question that does not map directly to your research question or analysis plan.

What is a leading question in a survey?

A leading question suggests the answer you expect, through loaded words, assumptions or praise. “How much did you enjoy the excellent workshop?” is leading; “How would you rate the workshop?” with a balanced scale is not.

Should a Likert scale have a neutral option?

Include a neutral midpoint when having no opinion or a balanced view is a real position for your respondents. Leaving it out forces people to lean one way, which can be useful in some designs, but should be a deliberate and reported choice.

Do I need to pilot my survey?

Yes. Even a small pilot with a handful of people from your target group catches confusing wording, missing answer options and technical problems with routing or export. Most supervisors and ethics committees expect you to describe your pilot.
