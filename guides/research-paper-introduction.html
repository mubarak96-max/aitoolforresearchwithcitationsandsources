How to write an introduction for a research paper, with a worked example

The introduction has one job: convince the reader that your question matters and has not been answered. Most introductions fail by reviewing everything instead of building toward the gap. This guide shows the structure, the moves and the wording.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Write the introduction as a funnel. Start with the broad problem and why it matters, narrow to what research already shows, identify the specific gap or contradiction, then state your aim, question or hypothesis and briefly how you address it. For a journal article, this usually takes three to five paragraphs and ends with your aims, not your results.

What the introduction must do

A reader who finishes your introduction should be able to answer four questions: What is the problem? What do we already know? What do we not know? What did this study set out to do? If any answer is missing, the rest of the paper has to work harder.

The introduction is not a literature review. In a journal article, you cite only the work needed to build your case for the gap. A thesis chapter has room for a separate review; a paper does not. Twenty citations in the introduction of a short empirical article is usually a sign that it lacks focus.

The CARS model

The most widely taught structure is John Swales’s “Create a Research Space” (CARS) model, developed from analysis of real article introductions. It has three moves.

MoveWhat you doTypical phrases
1. Establish a territoryShow the topic is important and summarise what is known.“X is a leading cause of…”, “Recent studies have shown…”
2. Establish a nicheShow what is missing, contradictory or unresolved.“However, little is known about…”, “These findings conflict…”
3. Occupy the nicheState your aim, question or hypothesis and how you address it.“This study examines…”, “We test whether…”

The second move is the most important and the one most often weak or missing. Without it, your study has no reason to exist.

Paragraph by paragraph

For a typical empirical paper, four paragraphs cover the moves comfortably.

Paragraph 1: the problem

Open with the specific problem, not the history of the field. Give one or two facts that show its scale or importance, with citations. “Antibiotic resistance is rising” is weak; a specific, cited figure for your context is strong.

Paragraph 2: what is known

Summarise the main findings that bear on your question. Group studies by what they show, not by author. “Three randomised trials found…” is better than “Smith (2020) found… Jones (2021) found…”.

Paragraph 3: the gap

Say precisely what that work leaves open. Then say why it matters that it is open. This is where you earn the right to do your study.

Paragraph 4: this study

State the aim and the specific questions or hypotheses. Mention the approach in a sentence. Some fields also expect a final sentence outlining the paper’s structure; many journals in the sciences do not.

Writing the gap statement

Gaps come in a few types. Naming yours helps you write it clearly.

  • Population gap: studied in adults, not adolescents.
  • Context gap: studied in high-income countries, not in low-resource settings.
  • Method gap: only cross-sectional data; no longitudinal evidence.
  • Contradiction: studies disagree, and the reason is unclear.
  • Mechanism gap: we know that it happens, not why.
  • Practical gap: the evidence exists but has not been tested in practice.

A strong gap statement is narrow and evidenced. “No research has examined X” is risky: one counterexample and a reviewer will doubt the rest. “Few studies have examined X, and those that have relied on self-reported data” is safer and more useful. Our guide to identifying a research gap covers how to check that a gap is real before you claim it.

A worked example

The topic and claims below are invented to show the moves. Citations are shown as placeholders.

[Territory] Hospital nurses working night shifts report more medication errors than day-shift colleagues [1, 2]. Fatigue is the most commonly proposed explanation, and several hospitals now limit consecutive night shifts on that basis [3].

[Known] Studies of shift length and errors have found that errors rise after the twelfth hour of a shift [4, 5]. Fewer studies have examined the number of consecutive nights, and those that have used incident reports, which undercount errors that cause no harm [6].

[Gap] It is therefore unclear whether limits on consecutive night shifts reduce errors, or whether error rates depend mainly on shift length. This matters because the two policies have different costs for staffing.

[This study] We used pharmacy audit data, which capture errors regardless of harm, to examine whether medication error rates rise with each consecutive night shift after accounting for shift length. We hypothesised that error rates would increase from the third consecutive night.

That is around 170 words, and every sentence moves toward the hypothesis. Notice how the gap is tied to a practical decision. Reviewers respond to gaps that matter to someone.

Length and tense

  • Journal article: usually 10 to 15% of the word count; often 400 to 800 words in a 6,000-word paper.
  • Conference paper: shorter, often two or three paragraphs.
  • Thesis chapter one: much longer, including background, rationale, aims, significance and a chapter outline.

Use the present tense for established knowledge (“Fatigue impairs attention”), the past tense for specific earlier studies (“Lee et al. found”) and either the present or past for your aims, following your journal’s style.

Thesis, conference and review introductions

The funnel stays the same across document types, but the proportions change.

  • Thesis or dissertation introduction. Several thousand words, usually covering background, the problem statement, aims and research questions, significance, scope and a chapter outline. The detailed literature goes in a separate chapter, so keep the introduction’s review short and focused on the gap.
  • Conference paper. Two or three paragraphs. Many computer science and engineering venues expect a bulleted list of contributions at the end of the introduction.
  • Review article. The gap is a gap in synthesis: the evidence exists but has not been brought together, or earlier reviews are out of date. State the review question and, for systematic reviews, the framework (such as PICO).
  • Grant proposal. The introduction becomes the case for funding. Lead with the problem and its cost, and make the gap something the funder cares about. See our guide to research proposal structure.

When to write it

Many experienced authors write the introduction after the methods and results, and revise it again after the discussion. By then you know exactly what the study shows, so you can build the funnel toward that and cut background that turned out not to matter. A practical order is: methods, results, discussion, introduction, abstract, title.

Keep a short working version from the start, though: one sentence each for the problem, the gap and the aim. It keeps the whole project pointed at one question.

Mistakes reviewers flag

  1. Starting too broad. “Since ancient times, humans have…” wastes the most-read sentence.
  2. A literature review in disguise. Paragraph after paragraph of studies with no gap.
  3. A vague gap. “More research is needed” is not a gap.
  4. Overclaiming novelty. “First study ever” is often wrong.
  5. Aims that do not match the methods. Reviewers check the aims against what you actually did.
  6. Results in the introduction. In most fields, keep findings for the results and abstract.
  7. Missing or fake citations. Check every reference exists and says what you claim. See how to spot hallucinated citations.

When you write the discussion section, return to the gap you named here and say how far you filled it. A paper whose introduction and discussion mirror each other reads as one argument. Frequently asked questions

How long should a research paper introduction be?

In a journal article, usually 10 to 15% of the total length, often three to five paragraphs or 400 to 800 words. Conference papers are shorter. Thesis introductions are much longer because they include background, significance and a chapter outline.

Should the introduction include results?

In most fields, no. The introduction ends with your aim, question or hypothesis. Results belong in the results section and the abstract. Some fields, such as parts of computer science and mathematics, summarise contributions at the end of the introduction; follow the norms of your target venue.

How many citations should an introduction have?

Enough to support each factual claim and to show the gap is real, but not so many that it becomes a literature review. For a short empirical paper, 10 to 25 citations is common; check recent articles in your target journal.

What is the CARS model?

CARS stands for Create a Research Space, a model of article introductions developed by the linguist John Swales. It has three moves: establish the territory, establish a niche or gap, and occupy the niche by stating what your study does.
