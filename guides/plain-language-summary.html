How to write a plain language summary of your research

Funders, journals and ethics committees increasingly ask researchers for a plain language summary, also called a lay summary. It is harder than an abstract: you must keep the facts exact while dropping the jargon. Here is a structure, the rules and a full before-and-after example.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer A plain language summary explains your research to a non-specialist in about 150 to 400 words. Say why the research was done, what question it asked, what you did, what you found and what it means for people, in everyday words and short sentences. Avoid jargon or explain it, use numbers people understand, be clear about uncertainty, and never overstate the findings.

Who reads a lay summary

Plain language summaries are written for people who are intelligent and interested but not experts in your field. That includes patients and the public, policy makers, journalists, funders’ lay reviewers, research participants and researchers from other disciplines.

You will be asked for one in several places:

  • Grant applications. Many funders score the lay summary, and lay panel members may read nothing else.
  • Journal articles. A growing number of journals, especially in health, publish a plain language summary alongside the abstract.
  • Ethics applications and participant information. Participants must understand what they are agreeing to. Our guide to research ethics and informed consent covers this.
  • Trial results. Clinical trial sponsors in some regions must provide lay summaries of results to participants.
  • Theses and reports, for public repositories and press offices.

Always check the specific brief. A funder’s lay summary is about what you will do; a journal’s is about what you found.

A six-part structure

Answer these six questions in order, one to three sentences each. Headings in the form of questions work well when allowed.

  1. Why was this research needed? The everyday problem, in terms the reader recognises.
  2. What did the researchers want to find out? The question, in one sentence.
  3. What did they do? Who took part or what was studied, how many, and what happened, in plain terms.
  4. What did they find? The main result, with a number people can picture.
  5. What does this mean? Who it matters to, and what might change.
  6. What are the limits? How sure we can be, and what still needs to be found out.

The last part is often left out, and it matters most. Lay readers cannot judge a study’s strength themselves, so you must tell them.

Ten readability rules

  1. Short sentences. Aim for an average of 15 to 20 words.
  2. Everyday words. “Use” not “utilise”, “start” not “initiate”, “heart attack” not “myocardial infarction”.
  3. Explain any term you must keep. “A placebo (a dummy treatment with no active ingredient)”.
  4. No abbreviations unless they are better known than the full term, such as DNA.
  5. Active voice. “We asked 300 parents” not “300 parents were surveyed”.
  6. Talk to people, about people. “People with diabetes” rather than “diabetic subjects”.
  7. One idea per sentence.
  8. Concrete examples over abstract categories.
  9. No hype. Avoid “breakthrough”, “cure” and “proves”.
  10. Consistent terms. Do not switch between “participants”, “volunteers” and “patients” for the same group.

Readability formulas such as Flesch Reading Ease give a rough guide, and many health organisations aim for a reading age of about 12. Treat the score as a warning light, not a target; short words can still make a confusing summary.

Common jargon swaps

Research writing has its own vocabulary that feels normal after a few years in the field. These swaps cover terms that appear in most lay summaries.

Research termPlain alternative
Participants, subjectsPeople who took part
Randomised controlled trialA fair test in which people were put into groups by chance
Cohort studyA study that followed a group of people over time
InterventionThe treatment, programme or change being tested
Statistically significantUnlikely to be due to chance alone
EfficacyHow well it works
Adverse eventsSide effects or harms
Qualitative interviewsIn-depth conversations with people about their experiences
Systematic reviewA study that gathered and combined all the research on a question
PrevalenceHow common something is

The US federal plain language guidelines include longer word lists and are useful for any audience, not only government writing.

Explaining numbers and risk

Numbers are where lay summaries most often mislead.

Instead ofWriteWhy
“Risk reduced by 50%”“The risk fell from 2 in 100 people to 1 in 100”Relative risk alone exaggerates small absolute changes.
“p < 0.05”“This difference was unlikely to be due to chance alone”P-values mean little to lay readers. See our guide.
“0.3 SD improvement”“A small improvement, about the same as…”Standardised units need a familiar comparison.
“OR = 1.8”“People who… were more likely to…”, with natural frequencies where possibleOdds ratios are widely misread, even by professionals.

Use natural frequencies (“3 in 1,000”) with the same denominator throughout. Say how many people took part, because readers rightly trust a study of 20,000 more than one of 20.

Before and after

An invented study, first as an abstract-style summary, then rewritten.

Before: This RCT evaluated the efficacy of a school-based mindfulness intervention on adolescent depressive symptomatology. 640 participants aged 13–15 were randomised to intervention or usual curriculum. At 12 months, no significant between-group difference was observed in PHQ-9 scores (adjusted mean difference −0.4, 95% CI −1.1 to 0.3).

After: Many teenagers experience low mood, and schools want to know whether teaching mindfulness can help. We wanted to find out whether weekly mindfulness lessons reduce symptoms of depression in 13- to 15-year-olds.

We worked with 640 students. Half, chosen at random, had mindfulness lessons for a school term. The other half had their usual lessons. A year later, all students filled in a standard questionnaire about their mood.

Students who had the mindfulness lessons had about the same mood scores as the others. The lessons did not seem to make a noticeable difference to symptoms of depression.

This suggests that mindfulness lessons on their own may not be enough to improve teenagers’ mood. They might still help some students, or work better in a different form. Our study took place in one region, so the results may differ elsewhere.

The rewrite is longer, which is normal. It keeps the design (random allocation), the size, the timing and the null result, and adds the limits. It does not hide the disappointing finding: a null result told honestly is just as useful to schools.

A writing process that works

Do not start by simplifying your abstract sentence by sentence; you will keep its structure and its jargon. Instead, imagine explaining the study to a curious neighbour. Say it out loud or record yourself, then transcribe and tidy what you said. Spoken explanations are naturally shorter, more concrete and better ordered for a lay listener. Then check every fact against the paper, add the limits, and cut anything the reader does not need.

Test it on a real reader

The only reliable test is a person from your intended audience. Give the summary to a friend, relative or patient representative outside your field and ask them three questions:

  1. What was this research about?
  2. What did it find?
  3. Which word or sentence was hardest to follow?

If their answers to the first two do not match your study, rewrite. Fix every word they flag. Many universities and funders have public involvement groups who review lay summaries; ask your research office. In health research, the EQUATOR Network also lists guidance on reporting research to patients and the public.

Checklist

  • It answers why, what question, what you did, what you found, what it means and how sure you are.
  • It fits the word limit and the brief (planned research or findings).
  • No unexplained jargon or abbreviations.
  • Average sentence length under about 20 words.
  • Risks given as absolute numbers or natural frequencies.
  • The number of people or cases is stated.
  • Uncertainty and limits are stated plainly.
  • No hype words; the claim matches the evidence.
  • A non-specialist has read it and explained it back correctly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an abstract and a plain language summary?

An abstract summarises a study for specialists and uses technical terms and statistics. A plain language summary explains the same study to non-specialists in everyday words, with numbers people can picture and a clear statement of the limits.

How long should a lay summary be?

Usually 150 to 400 words, but funders and journals set their own limits, sometimes in characters. A lay summary is often a little longer than the abstract because explaining terms takes space.

What reading level should a plain language summary aim for?

Many health and public organisations aim for text that a 12-year-old could follow. Use readability scores as a rough guide only, and test the summary on a real person from the intended audience.

Should a lay summary include limitations?

Yes. Lay readers cannot judge a study’s strength themselves, so say plainly how sure we can be, what the study could not show and what still needs to be found out.
