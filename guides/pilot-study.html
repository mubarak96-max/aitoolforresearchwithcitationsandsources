Pilot study: what it is for, how big it should be, and how to report it

A pilot study is a small rehearsal of your main study. Its job is to find the problems, such as slow recruitment, confusing questions or an unworkable protocol, while they are still cheap to fix. This guide explains what a pilot can and cannot tell you, how to set objectives and sample size, and how to write it up.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20266 min read
Short answer A pilot study tests whether a larger study is feasible: whether you can recruit and keep participants, deliver the intervention or procedures, and collect the data as planned. Set clear feasibility objectives and progression criteria in advance, such as “recruit at least 70% of the target”. Do not use a pilot to test whether an intervention works: it is too small, and effect sizes from pilots are unreliable.

What a pilot study is (and is not)

The terms vary between fields, but most methodologists now use feasibility study as the umbrella term for work done before a main study, and pilot study for a feasibility study that runs the planned study, or part of it, on a small scale. In surveys and interview research, “piloting” often means testing the instrument with a handful of people from the target group.

A pilot answers questions like:

  • Can we recruit enough eligible participants, and how fast?
  • Do participants stay in the study and complete the measures?
  • Can staff deliver the intervention as intended?
  • Do the questionnaires make sense to the people answering them? How long do they take?
  • Do randomisation, data collection and data entry work in practice?
  • How variable is the main outcome, so we can calculate the main study’s sample size?

A pilot does not answer “does it work?” With small samples, an estimated effect is imprecise, and a promising or disappointing result in a pilot is often wrong. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health explains this well in its guidance on common uses and misuses of pilot studies.

Set feasibility objectives and progression criteria

Write objectives about the process, not the outcome. Then decide, before you start, what results would mean “go ahead”, “go ahead with changes” or “stop”. These are progression criteria, and many funders now expect them.

ObjectiveMeasureGoAmendStop
RecruitmentParticipants recruited per month vs target≥ 80%50–79%< 50%
RetentionCompleted follow-up at 3 months≥ 75%60–74%< 60%
AdherenceSessions attended≥ 70%50–69%< 50%
Data completenessPrimary outcome recorded≥ 90%75–89%< 75%

The thresholds above are illustrations. Set yours from what the main study needs and what similar studies achieved.

How big should a pilot study be?

There is no single correct number, because the right size depends on what you need to estimate. Some guides that are widely cited:

  • Twelve per group is a rule of thumb proposed by Julious (2005) for estimating the variability of a continuous outcome in a two-arm pilot.
  • Larger pilots, often 30 or more per arm, have been recommended when you need a more precise estimate of the standard deviation to calculate the main trial’s sample size, especially if the expected effect is small.
  • Feasibility proportions such as recruitment or retention can be justified by the precision you want: with 50 participants, a 70% retention rate has a 95% confidence interval of roughly 56% to 82%.
  • Instrument piloting for a questionnaire often uses 5 to 15 people for cognitive interviews, then a small field test.

Whatever you choose, justify it in the protocol. “We chose 30 participants to estimate retention within ±15%” is a justification; “30 is the usual number” is not.

How to run a pilot, step by step

  1. Write a short protocol with feasibility objectives, progression criteria, sample size justification and procedures. Use the same protocol you plan for the main study wherever possible.
  2. Get ethics approval. Pilots with participants usually need it, even if small. Say in the consent information that this is a pilot.
  3. Recruit as you will in the main study, through the same sites and routes, or the recruitment estimate means nothing.
  4. Collect process data: screening logs, reasons for declining, time per session, missing data, staff notes.
  5. Ask participants and staff what was hard, through brief interviews or a short questionnaire.
  6. Compare results with progression criteria, and list every change you will make to the main protocol.

What to analyse, and what not to

Report feasibility outcomes descriptively: counts, percentages and confidence intervals. For example, “Of 112 patients screened, 64 were eligible and 41 consented (64%, 95% CI 52% to 75%).”

Avoid hypothesis tests of effectiveness. If you do report outcome data, present it descriptively with confidence intervals and say clearly that the pilot was not powered to test effects. Reviewers and examiners look for that sentence.

How to report a pilot study

For randomised pilot and feasibility trials, use the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials (Eldridge et al., 2016), available through the EQUATOR Network. Its main points apply to any pilot:

  • Say in the title that it is a pilot or feasibility study.
  • State feasibility objectives, not effectiveness hypotheses.
  • Give the sample size rationale.
  • Report progression criteria and whether they were met.
  • Report the changes you will make to the main study.
  • Present any outcome data as descriptive and exploratory.

In a thesis, the pilot often becomes a short section of the methods chapter or a chapter of its own. Link it to the main study: what you learned and what you changed. Our guide to research proposal structure shows how to describe a planned pilot in a proposal, and the data management plan guide covers what to do with pilot data.

Common mistake: using the pilot’s effect size to calculate the main study’s sample size. Pilot effect estimates are imprecise and often too optimistic. Base sample size on the smallest effect that would matter clinically or practically, and use the pilot only for the variability estimate.

Frequently asked questions

What is the purpose of a pilot study?

To test whether a larger study can be done as planned: whether you can recruit and retain participants, deliver procedures and collect complete data. It finds problems early, when they are cheap to fix. It is not designed to show whether an intervention works.

What is a good sample size for a pilot study?

It depends on what you need to estimate. Twelve per group is a common rule of thumb for estimating outcome variability, and larger pilots of 30 or more per arm are recommended for more precise estimates. Justify the number by the precision you need for your feasibility measures.

Is a pilot study the same as a feasibility study?

They overlap. Feasibility study is often used as the umbrella term for any work asking whether a future study can be done. A pilot study is a type of feasibility study that runs the planned study, or part of it, on a small scale.

Can I publish a pilot study?

Yes. Several journals publish pilot and feasibility studies, and publishing them helps others plan. Report feasibility outcomes and progression criteria, follow the CONSORT pilot extension for randomised pilots, and avoid claims about effectiveness.

Do pilot study participants count in the main study?

Usually not, because procedures change after the pilot. Some internal pilots are designed from the start to continue into the main trial without changes, and then the data can be kept. Decide and state this in the protocol before the pilot begins.
