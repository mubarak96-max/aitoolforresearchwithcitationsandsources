How to create an interview guide for qualitative research

An interview guide is the plan for a conversation, not a script. A good one makes sure every interview covers what your research question needs, while leaving room for participants to tell you things you did not think to ask.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20266 min read
Short answer List the topics your research question needs, turn each into one or two open main questions, and add probes to go deeper. Order them from easy and descriptive to more reflective or sensitive, with a warm-up at the start and a closing question at the end. Aim for 8–12 main questions for a 45–60 minute interview, then pilot the guide with one or two people and revise.

Structured, semi-structured or unstructured?

TypeWhat the guide looks likeBest for
StructuredFixed questions in a fixed order, often with set answer optionsComparable answers across many interviews; close to a spoken survey
Semi-structuredMain questions and probes; order and follow-ups can flexMost qualitative research: depth with consistent coverage
UnstructuredA few topics or a single opening questionLife histories, exploratory and ethnographic work

This guide focuses on semi-structured interviews, the most common choice for dissertations and applied research. A systematic review by Kallio and colleagues in the Journal of Advanced Nursing (2016) proposed a five-phase framework for developing a semi-structured interview guide, and the steps below follow the same logic.

Six steps to an interview guide

1. Check that interviews fit your question

Interviews are good for experiences, meanings, decisions and processes: how people understand something and why they act as they do. They are weak for measuring how common something is. If your question is about prevalence, a survey fits better. Our guide to writing survey questions covers that route.

2. List the topics your research question needs

Break the research question into three to six topic areas, using your literature review and any theory you are applying. For “How do early-career teachers decide whether to stay in the profession?” the topics might be: route into teaching, workload, support and mentoring, school culture, and thoughts about the future.

3. Write one or two main questions per topic

Main questions should be open, short and in everyday language. They invite a story or an explanation rather than a yes or no.

4. Add probes under each main question

Probes are prompts you use if the answer is short or misses something important. They are not extra questions to ask every time.

5. Order the questions

Start with an easy, factual warm-up, move to descriptive questions, then to reflective and sensitive ones, and close with an open question that lets participants add anything you missed.

6. Pilot, revise and record changes

Test the guide in one or two practice interviews and revise. Keep each version: your methods chapter should explain how the guide developed.

Writing good questions and probes

Good main questions

  • Experience: “Can you walk me through a typical week in your first term?”
  • Example: “Tell me about a time when you felt supported at work.”
  • Meaning: “What does a ‘good day’ in the classroom look like for you?”
  • Process: “How did you go about deciding to apply for this role?”

Questions to avoid

  • Closed: “Was the workload hard?” → “How would you describe the workload?”
  • Leading: “How stressful was the inspection?” → “What was the inspection like for you?”
  • Double: “How did your mentor and head teacher help?” → Ask about each one separately.
  • Jargon or theory: do not ask participants about your theoretical framework. Ask about their experience and do the theorising in analysis.
  • “Why” too early: “why” can sound like a challenge. “What led you to…” is gentler.

Types of probe

  • Elaboration: “Can you tell me more about that?”
  • Example: “Could you give me an example?”
  • Clarification: “When you say ‘they’, who do you mean?”
  • Feeling or meaning: “How did that feel at the time?”
  • Silence: a pause of a few seconds often brings the most useful part of an answer.

Consent comes first. Confirm consent, recording and data handling before the warm-up, and remind participants they can stop at any time. Our guide to informed consent for research lists what the information sheet needs to cover.

Piloting and revising

Run one or two pilot interviews with people similar to your participants, or at least with a colleague. Afterwards, ask:

  • Which questions produced short or confused answers?
  • Where did I need a probe that is not in the guide?
  • Did the order feel natural, or did I jump between topics?
  • Was there time for every topic, or did I run over?
  • Did anything feel uncomfortable for the participant, or for me?
  • Did the recording work, and is the audio clear enough to transcribe?

Small changes between early interviews are normal in qualitative research. Keep a log of what you changed and why. Reporting standards such as COREQ, listed by the EQUATOR Network, ask you to say whether the guide was pilot-tested and whether questions were provided to participants.

Using the guide in the interview

  • Follow the participant, not the page. If they answer topic 3 while talking about topic 1, tick it off and move on.
  • Listen for the unexpected. Some of the best findings come from things nobody planned to ask about. Follow them briefly, then return.
  • Keep notes short. Write key words so you can probe later, but keep eye contact.
  • Watch the time and protect the closing question.
  • Write field notes straight after, while the context is fresh.

Frequently asked questions

What is an interview guide in qualitative research?

It is a list of the main questions, probes and topics an interviewer plans to cover, usually in a suggested order. In semi-structured interviews it ensures consistent coverage across participants while allowing flexibility to follow up on what each person says.

How many questions should an interview guide have?

For a 45–60 minute semi-structured interview, about 8–12 main questions with probes is typical. Fewer, broader questions give participants more space; more questions risk turning the interview into a spoken survey.

What is a probe in an interview?

A probe is a follow-up prompt used to get more depth or clarity, such as “Can you give me an example?” or “What happened next?” Probes are used when needed rather than asked every time.

Should I send interview questions to participants in advance?

Some researchers share the topics or the main questions so participants can prepare, especially for professional or expert interviews. Others prefer spontaneous answers. Either is acceptable if you decide deliberately and report what you did.

Can I change my interview guide during the study?

Yes. In qualitative research it is common to refine questions after early interviews. Keep each version, note what changed and why, and check whether your ethics approval requires you to report substantial changes.
