Dissertation timeline: how to plan backwards from the deadline

Most dissertations that run late do not run late in the writing. They run late waiting for ethics approval, participants or a supervisor’s comments. A timeline that plans for those waits is the difference between a calm final month and a panicked one.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Start from your submission date and work backwards. Reserve the last 10–15% of the time for editing and formatting, book ethics approval and data collection early because they depend on other people, and add a buffer of two to four weeks before every external dependency. Break each phase into weekly tasks and review the plan every two weeks with your supervisor.

Why dissertation timelines slip

Students tend to plan the work they control, such as reading and writing, and forget the work they do not. The usual causes of delay are:

  • Ethics review. Committees meet on fixed dates and often ask for changes, which can add weeks.
  • Recruitment and access. Participants reply slowly, gatekeepers go on holiday, datasets need data-sharing agreements.
  • Feedback loops. A supervisor may need two weeks to return a chapter. Three chapters, two rounds each, is three months of waiting.
  • Analysis surprises. Missing data, a failed test assumption or a coding scheme that does not work the first time.
  • Formatting. References, figures, tables of contents and appendices always take longer than a weekend.

A good timeline does not pretend these will not happen. It gives each one a slot and a buffer.

There is also a quieter cause: the plan lives in your head. A timeline written down, shared with your supervisor and checked every week turns vague worry into specific decisions. You stop asking “am I behind?” and start asking “which task moved, and what does that do to the next milestone?”

Plan backwards in five steps

1. Fix the hard dates

Write down the submission deadline, any interim deadlines (proposal, progress review, draft chapter), the ethics committee’s meeting dates, and periods when you or your supervisor will be away. These are the anchors everything else hangs from.

2. Reserve the end

Block the last 10–15% of your time for full-draft revision, proofreading, reference checks and formatting. On a 12-month project that is about six weeks. Treat it as untouchable. If it gets eaten, you submit a first draft.

3. Place the dependencies early

Anything that depends on someone else goes as early as possible: ethics, data access, recruitment, lab or equipment bookings. Add a buffer of two to four weeks after each. Our guide to research ethics and informed consent explains what committees usually ask for, so your first submission is more likely to pass.

4. Split phases into weekly tasks

“Write the literature review” is not a task. “Draft the section on sleep and screen time, 1,200 words” is. Weekly tasks are small enough to finish and specific enough to tell whether you did.

5. Schedule the feedback

Agree turnaround times with your supervisor and put chapter hand-ins on the timeline, with the expected return date. While one chapter is with them, work on another.

Three timeline templates

Adjust the proportions to your field. Lab and fieldwork-heavy projects need more time in data collection; theoretical or secondary-data projects need less.

Rule of thumb: estimate how long each task will take, then add half again. Most people underestimate writing by about that much.

Track progress weekly

A timeline only helps if you look at it. Fifteen minutes every Friday is enough:

  • Mark this week’s tasks as done, partly done or not started.
  • Write one line on what blocked anything that slipped.
  • Move unfinished tasks forward and check whether a milestone is now at risk.
  • Note the word count for each chapter. Numbers reveal drift that feelings hide.
  • Send your supervisor a three-line update every two weeks, even when nothing is due.

Keep the plan, your notes and your files in one place. Pre-registering a study or storing your protocol on the free Open Science Framework also gives you a dated record of what you planned, which helps when you write the methods chapter.

A sample working week

A timeline sets the months; a weekly routine gets the work done. Many students find a fixed pattern easier than deciding every morning what to do. Here is one that works alongside part-time work or teaching:

DayFocusWhy
MondayPlan the week; reading and notes (2–3 hours)Reading early gives ideas time to settle before writing
TuesdayWriting block (2–3 hours, no email)Protected time for the hardest task
WednesdayData collection or analysisFits around participants’ and labs’ availability
ThursdayWriting block; references for the week’s textAdd citations while sources are fresh
FridayAdmin, supervisor update, 15-minute reviewCloses the loop and sets up next week

Short, regular writing sessions beat occasional marathons. Two hours a day, four days a week, adds up to a full chapter draft in most months. Write first and edit later: a rough paragraph you can fix is worth more than a perfect sentence you have not written.

Cite as you write, too. Leaving references to the end turns the final weeks into a hunt for “that paper about sleep from March”. A reference manager or a writing tool that inserts citations from your library saves days at the formatting stage, and our guide to citation styles covers the formatting rules you will need.

What to do when you fall behind

Almost everyone falls behind at some point. What matters is spotting it early and responding on purpose rather than by working nights.

  1. Find the real cause. Is it one blocked dependency, too much scope, or time lost to other work? Each needs a different fix.
  2. Protect the end buffer. Cut scope before you cut revision time.
  3. Cut scope deliberately. Drop a secondary research question, reduce the number of cases, or move an analysis to “future research”. Write down what you cut and why: it belongs in your limitations section.
  4. Tell your supervisor early. A supervisor told four weeks ahead can help. One told four days ahead can only sympathise.
  5. Ask about extensions properly. If illness or circumstances outside your control are the cause, use your institution’s formal process and keep evidence.

If the topic itself is the problem, for example because the data never arrived, revisit the feasibility tests in our guide to choosing a dissertation topic and agree a narrower question with your supervisor.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to write a dissertation?

It depends on the level and your programme. Undergraduate and taught master’s dissertations are usually planned over four to six months, research master’s over about a year, and a full-time PhD over three to four years. Writing itself often takes a third or more of the total time.

How much time should I leave for editing a dissertation?

Reserve roughly the last 10–15% of the project for revision, proofreading, reference checks and formatting. On a six-month dissertation that is two to three weeks; on a twelve-month project about six weeks.

When should I submit for ethics approval?

As early as your design allows, usually in the first month or two. Committees meet on fixed dates and often request changes, so leave two to four weeks of buffer between the expected approval date and the start of data collection.

What should a dissertation timeline include?

Hard deadlines, ethics and access dates, data collection and analysis periods, a draft date for each chapter, supervisor feedback slots, a protected revision period at the end, and buffers before every task that depends on someone else.

Is a Gantt chart necessary for a dissertation?

Not necessarily. A Gantt chart helps when tasks overlap, which is common in longer projects. A simple table of months, tasks and milestones, reviewed weekly, works just as well for most undergraduate and master’s dissertations.
