How to calculate sample size for a survey

The right sample size depends on what you want the survey to do. Estimating a percentage needs one calculation; comparing groups or testing a relationship needs another. This guide covers both, with worked numbers you can check by hand.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer To estimate a percentage, use n = z² × p(1 − p) / e². With 95% confidence (z = 1.96), the most cautious p = 0.5 and a ±5% margin of error, n = 385. For small populations, apply the finite population correction; for example, a population of 2,000 needs about 323. Then divide by your expected response rate to get the number of people to invite. If you will compare groups or test a relationship, run a power analysis instead.

First decide what the survey must do

Sample size questions have no single answer because surveys have different goals. Before you calculate anything, decide which of these describes your main research question:

  • Describe a population. “What percentage of students at this university use generative AI for coursework?” You need a sample big enough for an acceptable margin of error.
  • Compare groups. “Do first-year and final-year students differ in how often they use it?” You need enough people in each group to detect a difference of the size you care about.
  • Test a relationship. “Is use associated with lower confidence in writing?” You need enough cases to detect a correlation or regression effect of a given size.

Also check how you will sample. The formulas below assume a probability sample, where everyone in the population has a known chance of selection. Convenience samples (whoever answers a link on social media) can be large and still biased, and no formula fixes that. The AI for Research explainer on probability vs non-probability sampling covers the difference.

Estimating a percentage: the formula

For a large population, the sample size needed to estimate a proportion is:

n = z² × p × (1 − p) / e²
  • z is the z-score for your confidence level: 1.645 for 90%, 1.96 for 95%, 2.576 for 99%.
  • p is your best guess at the proportion. If you do not know, use 0.5, which gives the largest (safest) sample.
  • e is the margin of error you can accept, as a decimal: 0.05 for ±5 percentage points.

Worked example

95% confidence, p = 0.5, margin of error ±5%:

n = 1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5 / 0.05²
  = 3.8416 × 0.25 / 0.0025
  = 384.16  → round up to 385

Halving the margin of error roughly quadruples the sample. At ±3% the same calculation gives about 1,068; at ±10% it gives 97. That is why national polls often settle around ±3%: going further costs a lot for little gain.

Small populations: the finite population correction

If your population is small, for example all 2,000 nurses in one health board, you need fewer responses than the formula suggests. Apply the correction:

n_adjusted = n / (1 + (n − 1) / N)

where N is the population size. With n = 384.16:

  • N = 2,000: 384.16 / (1 + 383.16 / 2,000) ≈ 323
  • N = 500: 384.16 / (1 + 383.16 / 500) ≈ 218
  • N = 100,000: the correction changes almost nothing (about 383)

Above a population of roughly 20,000, the correction makes little difference. This surprises people: a survey of a city and a survey of a country need about the same sample for the same margin of error.

Subgroups need their own numbers. If you plan to report results separately for, say, four faculties, each faculty needs a sample large enough for the margin of error you will claim for it. A total of 385 split four ways gives about ±10% per faculty, not ±5%.

From sample size to invitations

The formula tells you how many completed responses you need. To find how many people to invite, divide by your expected response rate:

invitations = required responses / expected response rate
            = 385 / 0.25  = 1,540

Base the response rate on similar surveys of similar people, not on hope. Then add a margin for incomplete or unusable answers. Low response rates do not only shrink your sample; they raise the risk that responders differ from non-responders. Report your response rate and, where you can, compare responders with the population on known characteristics.

Comparing groups or testing relationships: power analysis

If your main question is a comparison or an association, margin-of-error formulas do not apply. You need a power analysis, which depends on four things:

  1. The smallest effect you care about (effect size), from earlier studies or practical importance.
  2. Significance level, usually α = 0.05.
  3. Power, usually 0.80: an 80% chance of detecting the effect if it is real.
  4. The test you will run, such as a t-test, chi-square or regression.

Rough figures for a two-group comparison of means at α = 0.05 and 80% power: about 64 per group for a medium effect (Cohen’s d = 0.5), about 176 per group for d = 0.3, and about 394 per group for a small effect (d = 0.2). Detecting a correlation of r = 0.3 needs roughly 85 cases. These numbers grow quickly as effects get smaller, which is why pilot data and earlier studies matter. They match the tables in Jacob Cohen’s classic “A power primer” (Psychological Bulletin, 1992), which is still a useful quick reference for conventional small, medium and large effects.

How to report your sample size

Examiners and reviewers want to see that the sample size was planned, not just accepted. In your methods section, report:

  • The goal (estimate, comparison or relationship) and the calculation you used.
  • Every assumption: confidence level, margin of error or effect size, power, expected proportion, population size.
  • Where the effect size or proportion came from (a pilot, a named earlier study, or a convention).
  • The number invited, the number who responded, the response rate and the number of usable responses.
  • What the achieved sample means for precision, if it fell short of the target.

A single sentence often covers it: “Assuming 95% confidence, a ±5% margin of error and a conservative proportion of 50%, with a finite population correction for 2,000 eligible staff, we required 323 responses; we invited all 2,000 and received 412 usable responses (20.6%).”

When a smaller sample is fine

Not every survey needs hundreds of responses:

  • Pilot surveys test wording and logistics; 10–30 responses is typical.
  • Census of a small group: if you can survey everyone (say, 40 staff), aim for as many as possible and report the response rate rather than a margin of error.
  • Exploratory or qualitative designs aim for depth, not estimates, and are judged by different standards.
  • Student projects sometimes have practical limits. Say what precision your sample gives, and treat findings as indicative.

Whatever size you end up with, state the calculation, the assumptions and the achieved sample in your methods section. Before you launch, make sure the questions themselves are sound: our guide to writing survey questions covers the wording rules, and choosing a dissertation topic explains how to test whether a survey-based project is feasible in your time frame.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good sample size for a survey?

For estimating a percentage in a large population with 95% confidence and a ±5% margin of error, about 385 completed responses. Smaller populations need fewer after the finite population correction. If you are comparing groups, the right size comes from a power analysis and depends on the effect you want to detect.

Is 100 responses enough for a survey?

It gives a margin of error of roughly ±10% at 95% confidence for a large population, which may be enough for a pilot, an exploratory study or a student project. It is usually too small for precise estimates or for detecting small differences between groups.

Why is 385 the magic number in surveys?

Because 1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5 / 0.05² = 384.16. That is the sample needed for a ±5% margin of error at 95% confidence when you assume the most cautious proportion, 50%, in a large population. Rounded up, it gives 385.

Does population size affect survey sample size?

Only when the population is small. The finite population correction reduces the required sample noticeably below a few thousand people, but above roughly 20,000 the required sample barely changes.

How do I account for low response rates?

Divide the number of completed responses you need by your expected response rate to find how many people to invite, then add a margin for unusable answers. Also check whether responders differ from non-responders, because low response can introduce bias as well as reduce precision.
