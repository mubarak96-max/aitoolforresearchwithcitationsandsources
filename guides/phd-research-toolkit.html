Research tools for PhD students: the toolkit, year by year

A doctorate is a four-year project with one big document at the end. The right tools, set up early, save you months. The wrong ones, or too many, eat the time you should spend thinking. Here is what to set up, when, and why.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202611 min read
Short answer Every PhD student needs five things: a reference manager, a dependable literature search routine, a writing setup that handles long documents and citations, an analysis tool for your data, and automatic backups. Set up the first two in your first month. Add AI tools for searching, reading and checking, not for writing the thesis for you.

Three rules before you install anything

  1. Fewer tools, used every day. Ten apps you half use are worse than four you know well. Every tool adds a place where something can get lost.
  2. Your data must leave. Prefer tools that export to open formats: BibTeX or RIS for references, .docx or .tex for writing, CSV for data. A tool you cannot leave is a risk over four years.
  3. Copy your lab. If your supervisor and group use a tool, use it too, unless it is truly bad. Shared tools make shared work and feedback easier.

Year one: reading and setting up

Start a reference library on day one

Every paper you read before you have a reference manager is a paper you will have to find again later. Pick one now. Organise by project or chapter with collections, add a short note to each paper on why it matters, and tag papers you have read in full.

AI-enabled libraries now go further than classic reference managers: you can ask a question across a whole collection and get answers that point to the page. The AI for Research library fills in title, authors and DOI when you upload a PDF, imports BibTeX and RIS on every plan, and can bring in an existing Zotero or Mendeley library on paid plans, so you do not have to choose between old and new tools.

Build a search routine

Set alerts in Google Scholar or your main database for your key terms and for the five most important authors in your area. Once a week, skim new results for 30 minutes. That is how you avoid the classic late-PhD shock of finding a paper that did your study two years ago.

For a first map of your field, an AI deep research report is a fast start, as long as every claim is cited and you read the key papers yourself. Our AI literature review workflow explains how to use one without losing control of the review.

Find your gap

By the end of year one you need a clear research question and a reason it matters. That usually means finding a gap: a group not studied, a method not tried, a contradiction not resolved. Our guide to identifying a research gap has five methods and the evidence each one gives you.

Years two and three: data and drafting

Make your analysis reproducible

Write analysis as code or a saved script, not as clicks you cannot repeat. Keep raw data read-only and do all cleaning in the script. Use Git, even if you are the only person on the project: it gives you a full history and a free backup on a remote server. A free Open Science Framework (OSF) project is a good home for your protocol, materials and data plan. Your future self, and your examiners, will thank you. Our reproducible research workflow guide covers the setup step by step.

Write from the start

Do not wait until year four to write. Turn every piece of work into a draft section: the literature review becomes chapter 2, your pilot study becomes part of the methods chapter. A chapter plan helps you see what is missing. The thesis chapter planner from AI for Research turns your topic and field into a chapter-by-chapter outline with typical word counts, which is a useful thing to bring to a supervisor meeting and argue about.

Publish along the way

In many fields, you are expected to publish before or soon after the defense. A thesis chapter is not a journal article: it needs to be shorter, have a narrower claim and stand alone. The AI for Research team has a practical guide on turning a thesis chapter into a journal article, including what to cut first. Before you submit, check the journal’s authorship and AI-use rules against the ICMJE recommendations (in medicine) or COPE guidance, and read our guide to choosing a journal.

Final year: assembling and defending

The last year is about consistency. Chapters written years apart use different terms, tenses and citation habits. Before you submit:

  • Merge chapters into one document and rebuild the reference list from your library, not by hand.
  • Check every in-text citation has a reference entry and every entry is cited.
  • Search for terms you renamed during the PhD and make them consistent.
  • Check figures, tables and cross-references after the merge.
  • Check your university’s formatting rules: margins, fonts, title page and declarations.
  • Read your AI-use declaration requirements and write an honest one.

Prepare for the defense

Examiners ask predictable kinds of questions: why this question, why this method, what are the limits, what would you do differently, how does it add to the field. Practise answering them aloud, and practise defending your weakest chapter, because that is where the questions will go. AI tools can generate likely examiner questions from your own thesis text; the thesis defense prep workspace does this from your chapters and lets you rehearse answers. Use it alongside a mock viva with real people, not instead of one.

The tool most PhD students forget: a notes system

Your reference manager stores what other people wrote. You also need a place for what you think: reading notes, ideas, supervisor feedback, decisions and the reasons behind them. Four years is long enough to forget why you chose a method or dropped a variable, and examiners will ask.

The tool matters less than the habit. A plain folder of text files, a note-taking app or a lab notebook all work. What matters:

  • One note per paper you read closely, linked to the reference in your library. Write the main claim in your own words, the method in one line, and one question it raises.
  • A decisions log. Date, decision, reason, who agreed. “14 March: dropped the 2019 wave because the questionnaire changed; agreed with supervisor.” This becomes gold when you write your methods chapter.
  • A meeting log. After each supervisor meeting, write three lines: what was discussed, what you agreed to do, by when. Email it to your supervisor. Misunderstandings drop sharply.
  • A weekly review. Fifteen minutes on Friday: what moved, what is stuck, what is next. Small, steady progress beats bursts before deadlines.

Keep notes in a format you can search and export. Plain text and Markdown will still open in twenty years; some proprietary formats will not.

A realistic budget

Many PhD students pay for tools they barely use. Before you subscribe, check what your university already licenses: reference managers, statistics software, qualitative analysis tools, cloud storage and sometimes AI tools are often free through the library or IT services. Then pay only for the gap. Most AI research tools have free plans that are enough to test them properly; upgrade in the months you need heavy use, such as during a systematic review or the final write-up, and downgrade afterwards.

Where AI fits, and where it does not

AI tools are now part of most doctorates. Used well, they save hours of searching and reformatting. Used badly, they produce citations that do not exist and prose that is not yours. A simple rule: use AI to find, organise and check; do the thinking and the final writing yourself.

  • Good uses: search across indexes, first-pass summaries, extracting details into a table, checking your draft for unsupported claims, formatting references, practising for the defense.
  • Risky uses: taking references from a chatbot without search, pasting AI paragraphs into your thesis, letting AI interpret your results.

Before you rely on any assistant, run the tests in our guide to choosing an AI research assistant.

Agree it with your supervisor: write down which AI uses are fine for your project and keep a short log of what you used and when. If you work with participant or patient data, check your ethics approval before uploading anything to any tool.

Frequently asked questions

What tools does every PhD student need?

Five things: a reference manager, a reliable way to search the literature, a writing setup that handles long documents and citations, an analysis tool suited to your data, and automatic backups with version history. Everything else is optional.

Should PhD students use AI tools?

AI tools are useful for searching, first-pass reading, outlining and checking drafts. They are risky for writing text you submit as your own and for citations taken from a chatbot without search. Agree the boundaries with your supervisor and follow your university’s policy.

Word or LaTeX for a PhD thesis?

Use what your field and supervisor use. LaTeX is standard in mathematics, physics and computer science and handles equations and cross-references well. Word is common elsewhere and easier for supervisor comments. Many tools now export to both, so the choice matters less than it did.

When should I start using a reference manager?

On the first day. Every paper you read before you set one up is a paper you will have to find again. Moving a few hundred references later is possible but tedious, and it is when errors creep in.

How should I back up my thesis?

Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one off-site. In practice that means your laptop, a university or cloud drive with version history, and an external drive or second cloud service. Test a restore at least once.
