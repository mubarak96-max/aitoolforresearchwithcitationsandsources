Searching · Beyond journals

How to search grey literature without losing a week

Some of the most important evidence never appears in a journal: unpublished trial results, government evaluations, theses and conference abstracts. This guide shows where to look, how to search each source, and how to record what you did so a reviewer can follow it.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Decide first which kinds of grey literature matter for your question, then search them in a fixed order: trial and study registries, theses, preprint servers, government, agency and NGO websites, and conference abstracts. Use short, simple searches, because most of these sources do not support complex Boolean strings, and log every source, date, search and number of results.

What counts as grey literature

Grey literature is research and information produced outside commercial and academic publishing. The most cited definition, from the international grey literature community, describes it as material produced by government, academia, business and industry that is not controlled by commercial publishers. In practice that means:

  • Trial and study registry records, including results posted for trials that were never published.
  • Theses and dissertations, which often contain full methods and negative findings left out of later papers.
  • Preprints, which are papers shared before peer review.
  • Reports from governments, regulators, health technology assessment agencies, think tanks, charities and international organisations.
  • Conference abstracts and posters.
  • Working papers, common in economics and policy.
  • Standards, guidelines and technical documents.

Why it matters: publication bias

Studies with positive or striking results are more likely to be published, published faster and published in journals that databases index. Studies with null results more often stay in a drawer, a registry or a thesis. If you only search journal databases, your review can overstate how well something works.

Grey literature also matters for practical reasons. Policy questions are often answered best by government evaluations. New fields move through preprints months before journals. And in some disciplines, such as education and social policy, much of the applied research is published as reports.

Searching the web for reports

General web searches return too much, and the results change with your location and history. You can make them more useful with a few operators that work in most search engines:

  • site:gov.uk or site:who.int limits results to one domain or a type of domain.
  • filetype:pdf finds reports rather than news pages.
  • "evaluation" OR "final report" targets the right kind of document.

For example: site:gov.uk filetype:pdf "school meals" evaluation. Decide in advance how far you will read, for example the first 100 results for each search, and record that rule. Without a stopping rule, web searching never ends and cannot be reported.

Our guide to Boolean operators explains why simple strings work best here: most websites and repositories ignore brackets and truncation.

A grey literature search plan

  1. Pick the types that matter. A drug question needs registries and regulatory documents. A policy question needs government and think tank reports. Do not try to search everything.
  2. List specific sources. Name each registry, repository and website. Ask a subject librarian or experts in the field what they would check.
  3. Write short searches. Two or three key terms per source, adapted to its search box.
  4. Set a stopping rule for web searches and long result lists.
  5. Search and log in one sitting per source, saving the results you keep.
  6. Screen with the same criteria you use for journal articles, and appraise quality, because grey literature has not been peer reviewed.
  7. Contact authors of promising abstracts or registry entries for full results.

A search log you can copy

Grey literature is the part of a review that is hardest to reproduce, so record more, not less. Reporting guidance such as PRISMA 2020 asks for every source and date searched. The Cochrane Handbook has a full chapter on searching that covers grey sources.

Worked example: a policy question

Suppose a local authority analyst is asked: what is known about the effects of free school meal programmes on attendance? Journal databases return a few dozen evaluations, mostly from the United States. The grey literature changes the picture.

  1. Types chosen: government evaluations, think tank reports, NGO reports and theses. Trial registries are less relevant because few evaluations are registered trials.
  2. Sources named: the education ministry and its research publications page, the national statistics office, two education research charities, the OECD library and the national thesis repository.
  3. Searches: short and consistent, such as "free school meals" attendance and "universal free meals" evaluation, plus site:gov.uk filetype:pdf "free school meals" evaluation on the web.
  4. Stopping rule: all results on organisation sites; the first 100 web results per search.
  5. Result: several government pilot evaluations with attendance data that never appeared in journals, and two theses with local data. Two of the evaluations found smaller effects than the published studies, which is exactly the kind of evidence a journal-only search would miss.

The analyst logs every search, screens with the same criteria as the journal articles, and notes in the report which findings come from non-peer-reviewed sources. Total extra time: about a day and a half, against a report that would otherwise have overstated the benefit.

How to judge grey literature

No peer review does not mean poor quality. Many government evaluations are more rigorous than journal articles. But you have to do the checking yourself. The AACODS checklist, developed for grey literature, asks about:

  • Authority: who produced it, and are they credible on this topic?
  • Accuracy: is the method described, and are the sources cited?
  • Coverage: are the limits of the work stated?
  • Objectivity: does the producer have a stake in the result?
  • Date: is it current enough for your question?
  • Significance: does it add something to your review?

Treat preprints with extra care: a later published version may differ, and some are never published. Our guide on reading research papers faster includes a quick check for methods you can apply to any report.

Time check: for a student literature review, two or three well-chosen grey sources are usually enough. For a systematic review in health or policy, plan days, not hours, and agree the source list with your team before you start.

Frequently asked questions

What is an example of grey literature?

Common examples are a government evaluation report, a PhD thesis, a trial record with results on ClinicalTrials.gov, a preprint on medRxiv, a conference abstract and a working paper from a research institute. None of them went through a commercial journal publisher.

Is grey literature peer reviewed?

Usually not in the journal sense. Theses are examined, and some agency reports are reviewed internally or externally, but you cannot assume it. Appraise grey literature yourself with a checklist such as AACODS or the same critical appraisal tool you use for published studies.

Do I need grey literature in a systematic review?

Most guidance recommends it, especially for health interventions, because it reduces publication bias. At minimum, search trial registries for intervention reviews. Report what you searched and when, and explain any grey sources you chose not to search.

How do I cite grey literature?

Cite it as the document type it is: a report, thesis, preprint or dataset. Include the organisation as author when no person is named, the report number if there is one, the date and a stable URL. Our citation styles guide shows the common formats.

Is Google Scholar good for grey literature?

It finds some theses, preprints and reports, but its coverage is uneven, results cannot be reproduced exactly and it shows at most 1,000 results per search. Use it as one source with a stopping rule, alongside registries and organisation websites.
