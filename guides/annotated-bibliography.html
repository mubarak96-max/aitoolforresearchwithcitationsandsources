How to write an annotated bibliography, with APA and MLA examples

An annotated bibliography is a list of sources with a short paragraph under each one. Done well, it is the first draft of your literature review. Here is how to choose sources, what to write about each, and how to format the entries.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer For each source, write the full citation in your required style, then a paragraph of about 100 to 200 words below it. The paragraph summarises the source’s argument and method, evaluates its strengths and limits, and explains how it relates to your own research question. Order the entries alphabetically by author unless your instructor asks for themes.

What an annotated bibliography is for

A plain bibliography tells a reader what you read. An annotated one tells them what each source says, how good it is and why it matters to your project. Instructors set them for three reasons: to check that you are finding credible sources, to see whether you can read critically, and to make you start synthesis early instead of the night before a deadline.

Researchers keep them for their own reasons. A good annotation, written while the paper is fresh, saves you from rereading it six months later when you write your literature review or thesis chapter. That is why it is worth doing well even when nobody marks it.

Three types of annotation

Assignments differ in what they want under each citation. Read the brief closely and match the type.

TypeWhat it doesTypical length
Descriptive (indicative)Says what the source covers: topic, scope, method. No judgement.50–100 words
Summary (informative)Gives the main argument, method and findings in your own words.100–150 words
Evaluative (critical)Summarises, then assesses quality and relevance to your research.150–250 words

Most university assignments want the evaluative type even when they do not say so. If in doubt, include three parts: summary, evaluation and relevance.

Step by step

1. Fix your question first

An annotated bibliography without a research question is just a reading list. Write your question at the top of a document before you search. Every annotation’s final sentences will relate the source back to it. If you do not have a question yet, our guide on finding a research gap can help you shape one.

2. Choose sources, not just results

Aim for a mix: a few highly cited foundational studies, recent work from the last five years, at least one review article, and sources that disagree with each other. Check each one is peer-reviewed, or that you have a reason to use it if it is not. Free indexes such as OpenAlex and PubMed let you see publication type and citation counts before you commit.

3. Read with three questions

For each source, answer three questions in rough notes before you write anything polished:

  • What does it claim, and how does it know? The argument, the design, the sample or material, the main finding.
  • How far can I trust it? Sample size, method, bias, funding, date, who the authors are and where it was published.
  • What does it do for my question? Does it support, complicate or contradict what I expect? Does it give me a method, a definition or a gap?

4. Write the citation

Format the full reference in the style your course uses before writing the annotation. Get the DOI while you have the paper open. A free citation generator that pulls details from the DOI saves time, but check author names and capitalisation against the paper itself. Our guide to citation styles compared shows the same source in each major style.

5. Write the annotation

Use your three sets of notes as three short blocks: two or three sentences of summary, two of evaluation, one or two of relevance. Write in your own words. Quote only if a phrase is so precise that paraphrase would lose it, and keep quotes short.

Worked examples

The source below is an invented study, used to show the shape of an entry. Do not cite it.

APA 7 evaluative entry

Lindqvist, M., & Osei, K. (2024). Remote work and loneliness among early-career employees: A two-wave panel study. Journal of Occupational Wellbeing, 18(2), 145–163. https://doi.org/10.0000/example

Lindqvist and Osei surveyed 1,820 employees in their first three years of work at two points six months apart to test whether fully remote work predicts later loneliness. They found that fully remote employees reported higher loneliness at follow-up than hybrid workers, even after controlling for baseline loneliness and living situation. The two-wave design is stronger than the single-survey studies common in this area, but the sample came from technology firms in two countries, and loneliness was self-reported with a short scale. The study supports my hypothesis that the remote–hybrid distinction matters more than total hours at home, and its use of baseline controls is a model for my own analysis plan. It does not address managers, which is the group my project focuses on.

Look at the shape: two sentences summarise, two evaluate, two connect it to the writer’s project, including what it does not cover. That last point is how an annotated bibliography starts to reveal a gap.

The same source in MLA 9

Lindqvist, Maria, and Kwame Osei. “Remote Work and Loneliness among Early-Career Employees: A Two-Wave Panel Study.” Journal of Occupational Wellbeing, vol. 18, no. 2, 2024, pp. 145–63, https://doi.org/10.0000/example.

The annotation itself does not change between styles, but the layout does. In MLA, the annotation is usually indented under the citation; in APA, it sits as a new paragraph indented from the left margin. The MLA Style Center and APA Style sites both publish official guidance on annotated bibliographies.

Formatting rules

  • Order: alphabetical by first author’s surname, unless asked to group by theme or chronology.
  • Hanging indent for the citation (the first line flush left, later lines indented).
  • Annotation starts on the line after the citation, indented further, double-spaced if your style requires.
  • Title: “Annotated Bibliography”, centred, at the top of a new page.
  • Person: third person for summary and evaluation is standard; some instructors accept “I” or “my project” in the relevance section.
  • Length: keep entries roughly even. One long and five short entries look unbalanced.

Common mistakes

  1. Summarising the abstract. Annotations that only restate the abstract show you did not read the paper. Mention something from the method or results.
  2. No evaluation. “This is a useful study” is not an evaluation. Say what makes it strong or weak.
  3. No link to your question. If you cannot say how a source helps, drop it.
  4. Copying phrases. Close paraphrase in annotations still counts as plagiarism. See our guide on paraphrasing without plagiarising.
  5. Citation errors. Wrong capitalisation, missing DOIs and author names in the wrong order lose easy marks.
  6. Trusting AI-found sources. If an AI tool suggested a reference, confirm it exists before you annotate it. Our guide to hallucinated citations explains how.

Using AI without losing the point

AI tools can summarise a paper in seconds, which makes them tempting for annotated bibliographies. Used carelessly, they defeat the purpose: the assignment exists to show that you read and judged the source. Three uses are generally safe and useful:

  • Finding sources, as long as you confirm each one exists and read it.
  • Locating details in a long paper, such as the sample size or the exclusion criteria, with a page number you can check.
  • Checking your summary against the paper, by asking whether you missed a main finding.

Write the evaluation and relevance parts yourself. A tool cannot know your research question as well as you do, and its judgement of quality is often generic. If your course allows AI help, keep a note of what you used it for in case you are asked; our guide to AI rules in academic writing explains what to disclose.

From bibliography to literature review

Once you have ten or more entries, read your relevance sentences together. You will see patterns: three sources agree on one point, two disagree on another, nobody has studied a particular group. Those patterns are the themes of your literature review.

A simple way to move from one to the other is a synthesis matrix: list sources down the side and themes across the top, then fill in what each source says about each theme. Our guide to the literature synthesis matrix walks through it. Then write the review by theme, not by source, so each paragraph compares several studies instead of describing one.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an annotation be?

Usually 100 to 200 words, or about one paragraph. Descriptive annotations can be shorter, around 50 to 100 words, and evaluative annotations for graduate work can run to 250. Follow your instructor’s guidance if it gives a number.

How many sources should an annotated bibliography have?

It depends on the assignment and level. Undergraduate assignments often ask for 6 to 15 sources; graduate projects may need 20 or more. Quality matters more than number: each source should clearly relate to your research question.

Can I use first person in an annotated bibliography?

Often yes in the relevance part, for example “this study supports my argument that”. Summary and evaluation are usually written in the third person. Check what your instructor or style guide prefers.

What is the difference between an annotated bibliography and a literature review?

An annotated bibliography discusses each source separately, one entry at a time. A literature review is an essay organised by themes, comparing and synthesising many sources in each paragraph to show what is known and what is missing.
