How to choose a dissertation topic you can actually finish

A good dissertation topic is not the most original idea you can think of. It is a question you care about, that the literature leaves open, and that you can answer with the time, data and skills you have. This guide turns those three tests into six steps.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer Start with three to five areas that genuinely interest you. For each, read recent reviews to see what is settled and what is still debated, then write one candidate question. Test every question for feasibility (data, time, skills, ethics) and narrow the best one until it fits your word count. Bring two shortlisted questions, not one, to your supervisor.

What makes a dissertation topic good

Examiners rarely fail a dissertation because the topic was not exciting enough. They fail it, or mark it down, because the question was too broad to answer properly, the data never arrived, or the argument did not connect to what was already known. A good topic passes four tests:

  • Interest. You will spend months with it. Mild curiosity does not survive the third round of edits.
  • An open question. The literature has not already answered it, or has answered it for a different group, place, period or method.
  • Feasibility. You can get the data, you have (or can learn) the methods, and the study fits your deadline and ethics rules.
  • Fit. It matches your programme’s expectations and someone in your department can supervise it.

Clinical researchers use a similar checklist called FINER: feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant. Our guide to research proposal structure shows how the same tests reappear when you write the proposal.

The six steps

1. List what you already care about

Write down every module, lecture, news story, job experience or argument that kept your attention. Do not filter yet. Aim for ten items, then circle the three to five you would still want to read about on a bad day. Topics that come from your own experience, such as a placement, a job or a community you belong to, often give you access to data that other students cannot get.

2. Read the most recent reviews first

For each area, find two or three review articles from the last five years. Reviews tell you what is settled, what is contested and, usually in the discussion section, what the authors think should be studied next. Those “future research” paragraphs are the fastest map of open questions you will find.

Search a free index such as OpenAlex for any field or PubMed for health topics, and filter by publication type and year. If your area is crowded, our guide on how to identify a research gap walks through five ways to find the space that is left.

3. Write one candidate question per area

Turn each area into a single question. A question forces you to decide what you will actually measure, compare or explain. Compare:

  • Topic: Social media and mental health.
  • Question: Is time spent on short-video apps associated with sleep quality among first-year university students in the UK?

The second version tells you the population, the exposure, the outcome and the setting. You can already see what data you would need.

4. Check what has been done, including other theses

Search for your exact question. If a recent paper already answers it well, you have three options: change the population, change the method, or replicate it deliberately (replication is a respectable dissertation in many fields, if you say that is what you are doing). Search previous dissertations too. The free Open Access Theses and Dissertations index covers millions of theses and shows what students at your level have managed in the time available.

5. Test feasibility honestly

For each candidate, answer these in writing:

  • Where exactly will the data come from, and do I have permission to use it?
  • How many participants, documents or cases do I need, and can I reach them in time?
  • Which methods will I use, and have I used them before?
  • Does the study need ethics approval, and how long does my committee take?
  • Does any part depend on someone else (a gatekeeper, a company, a lab slot)?

If two or more answers are “I am not sure”, the topic is a risk. That does not rule it out, but you need a fallback design. For survey-based topics, our guide to survey sample size helps you check whether the numbers are realistic before you commit.

6. Shortlist two and talk to your supervisor

Bring two questions, each on one page: the question, why it matters, what is already known (with five or six key references), the method you have in mind, and the main risk. Two options give your supervisor something to compare and stop the meeting turning into a vague brainstorm.

Score your shortlist

Rate each candidate from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong). The topic with the highest total is not automatically the winner, but a score of 1 on feasibility should worry you more than a 1 on novelty.

How to narrow a broad topic

Almost every first topic is too broad. Narrow it along one or more of these dimensions until the question fits your word count:

  • Population: from “young people” to “first-year undergraduates at two universities”.
  • Place: from “Africa” to “urban Kenya” to “Nairobi’s informal settlements”.
  • Time: from “the history of” to “between 2015 and 2025”.
  • Variable: from “wellbeing” to “self-reported sleep quality”.
  • Method: from “how do people feel” to “a thematic analysis of 15 interviews”.

A useful check: an undergraduate dissertation usually answers one main question, a master’s dissertation one question with two or three sub-questions, and a doctoral thesis a programme of linked questions. If your question needs three separate studies, it is a PhD.

Rule of thumb: if you cannot explain your question to a friend in one sentence, it is still a topic, not a question.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing for originality alone. A modest question answered well beats an ambitious one answered badly.
  • Ignoring data access. “I will survey nurses” is not a plan until a hospital has agreed.
  • Picking a topic you feel strongly about. Strong opinions make it hard to report results that disagree with you. Interest is good; a fixed conclusion is not.
  • Waiting for the perfect idea. Topics improve as you read. Pick a working question and refine it.
  • Changing topic late. After your proposal is approved, adjust the question rather than replacing it.

Where AI helps, and where it does not

AI tools are good at the slow parts of steps 2 and 4: scanning many abstracts, grouping papers by theme and pointing out what recent reviews say is missing. They are bad at judging whether a gap matters to your field, and a general chatbot may invent the papers it says support a gap. Use a tool that searches real indexes and shows you the papers, then read them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a dissertation topic quickly?

List three to five areas you care about, read one recent review in each, and write one question per area. Test each for data access, methods and time, then take the two strongest to your supervisor. This can be done in a week if you read reviews rather than individual papers.

How narrow should a dissertation topic be?

Narrow enough that you can answer it fully within your word count and deadline. Most undergraduate and master’s topics specify a population, a setting, a time period and one main variable or phenomenon. If the question needs several separate studies, it is too broad.

Can I change my dissertation topic?

Usually yes, early on, but check your programme’s rules and deadlines. After a proposal or ethics approval, it is normally better to adjust the question within the approved project than to start again, because new ethics approval can take weeks.

Does my dissertation topic need to be original?

It needs to make a contribution, which is not the same as being completely new. Applying a known question to a new population, setting or method, or replicating a study carefully, is a legitimate contribution at undergraduate and master’s level.

Can AI choose my dissertation topic for me?

AI can suggest areas and show which questions recent papers flag as open, which speeds up reading. The choice should be yours: you need to judge interest, feasibility and fit, and you will have to defend the topic. Check every paper an AI cites before relying on it.
