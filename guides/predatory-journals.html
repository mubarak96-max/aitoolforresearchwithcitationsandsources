How to identify predatory journals before they cost you a paper

A predatory journal looks like a real one, charges like a real one, and publishes almost anything. Once your paper is in it, getting it back out can be difficult. Here is how to tell the difference in about fifteen minutes.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Predatory journals put fees ahead of quality: they promise fast publication, do little or no real peer review, and often hide their fees, editors or location. Be suspicious of unsolicited emails, vague or very broad scope, promised review times of days, fake or unverifiable metrics, and editorial boards you cannot confirm. Then check: is the journal in DOAJ or a major index your field uses, does its publisher belong to COPE, and do researchers you trust publish there?

What makes a journal predatory

There is no single legal definition. In 2019, a group of researchers and publishers reached a consensus definition published in Nature: predatory journals and publishers are “entities that prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship” and are characterised by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and aggressive, indiscriminate solicitation (Grudniewicz et al., 2019).

The key point is the last one: open access and article fees are not the problem. Many excellent journals charge fees. The problem is a journal that takes the fee and skips the scholarship.

Why it matters

  • Your work loses credibility. Hiring panels, funders and examiners may discount publications in known predatory venues.
  • It is hard to withdraw. Some predatory journals refuse to withdraw a paper or demand a fee to do so, which blocks you from submitting it to a legitimate journal.
  • It may disappear. Predatory journals often lack proper archiving. Papers can vanish when the website goes down.
  • It pollutes the literature. Unreviewed claims get cited as if they were peer-reviewed, including by AI tools that do not check where a paper was published.

Twelve warning signs

One sign on its own proves little. Three or more together should stop you.

In the invitation email

  1. You were invited to submit without any prior contact, often with flattering language about a paper you published.
  2. The email promises acceptance or publication within days.
  3. The journal’s topic has little to do with your field, or covers an implausibly wide range of subjects.
  4. The email uses a free webmail address rather than an institutional or publisher domain.

On the website

  1. The fees are hard to find, or appear only after acceptance.
  2. The editorial board lists people you cannot verify, or people who do not mention the journal on their own pages.
  3. The journal claims an impact factor or metric from an organisation you have never heard of.
  4. The physical address is missing, or does not match the country the journal claims.
  5. Published papers contain obvious errors, poor English throughout, or topics far outside the stated scope.
  6. The journal title closely imitates a well-known journal, with one word changed.

In the process

  1. The “peer review” comes back within a day or two with no substantive comments.
  2. You are asked to pay before the paper has been reviewed, or to transfer copyright before you see the reviews.

Hijacked and cloned journals

A newer trick is the hijacked journal: a fake website that copies the name, ISSN and look of a real, respected journal, often one that is small or has a dated website of its own. Authors think they are submitting to the real journal and pay a fee to the copy.

To protect yourself:

  • Reach the journal’s site from its publisher’s homepage or a trusted index, not from a link in an email or a search ad.
  • Compare the domain with the one listed in the index entry. A slightly different domain is a red flag.
  • Check that the ISSN on the site matches the ISSN in the index, and that the editor-in-chief named on the site is the one named by the publisher.
  • Look at the back issues. A hijacked site often shows recent papers on topics the real journal never covered.

If you suspect a clone, contact the real journal through its publisher. They will want to know.

A two-minute decision rule

If you only have a moment, ask three questions. Did the journal find you, rather than you finding it? Is it missing from every directory and index you trust? Does nobody you know publish there? If the answer to all three is yes, do not submit.

Predatory conferences

The same model exists for conferences. Warning signs include a very broad title (“International Conference on Science, Engineering and Management”), many unrelated conferences run by the same organiser in the same hotel, high registration fees with no clear review process, and invited “keynote” slots offered to anyone who pays. Check whether a professional society in your field sponsors the event and whether people you know have attended.

Do not cite them by accident either

Predatory journals also affect what you cite. When you find a paper through a broad search engine or an AI tool, check where it was published before you rely on it. A study in an unreviewed venue is not necessarily wrong, but it has not passed the checks you are assuming it passed.

This matters more now that AI tools summarise literature for you. A summary that cites a paper does not tell you whether the journal reviewed it. Our guide on spotting hallucinated citations covers fake references; the venue check is the next step after confirming a reference is real.

What to do if you already submitted

  1. Do not pay any further fees and do not sign a copyright transfer.
  2. Withdraw in writing. Email the journal clearly stating that you withdraw the manuscript and do not consent to publication. Keep a copy.
  3. Tell your co-authors and supervisor so everyone knows the status of the paper.
  4. If it was published anyway, ask your institution’s library or research integrity office for advice. Some legitimate journals will consider a paper that appeared in a predatory venue if you explain the situation openly in the cover letter; others will not. Be transparent.
  5. Choose more carefully next time, using our guide to choosing a journal.

Be wary of lists. Lists of “bad” journals go out of date and can include borderline cases. Use them as a prompt to investigate, not as a final verdict, and rely on the positive checks above: inclusion in reputable directories and indexes, transparent policies, and verifiable editors.

The grey zone

Not every low-quality journal is predatory. Some are simply new, small, or run by volunteers with limited resources. A new journal may not yet be indexed. In those cases, look for transparency: clear fees, named and verifiable editors, a stated peer review process, a policy on corrections and retractions, and a plan for long-term archiving. A new journal that is open about all of this is taking a different path from one that hides it.

When in doubt, ask whether you would be comfortable listing the paper on a job application and explaining the venue to a sceptical panel. If not, choose another journal.

Frequently asked questions

What is a predatory journal?

A predatory journal charges publication fees while skipping real peer review and editorial checks. It typically uses misleading information about its metrics, editors or location and sends aggressive, untargeted invitations to submit.

Are all open access journals with fees predatory?

No. Many respected journals are open access and charge article processing charges. A journal is predatory because it does not provide the review and editorial service it claims, not because it charges a fee.

How can I check if a journal is legitimate?

Check whether it is listed in DOAJ or indexed in the databases your field uses, whether its publisher is a COPE member, whether its editors are real and acknowledge their role, and whether colleagues in your field know it. The Think. Check. Submit. checklist covers these steps.

Can I republish a paper that appeared in a predatory journal?

Sometimes, but it is difficult. Many journals will not consider work that has already been published. Withdraw the paper in writing if you can, and be fully transparent with any new journal about what happened.
