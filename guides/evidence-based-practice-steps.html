Evidence-based practice steps: the 5 As, with a worked example

Evidence-based practice means combining the best research evidence with clinical expertise and the patient’s values. It sounds abstract until you see it as five steps you can run on a single question in an afternoon. This guide walks through each one with a nursing example.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer The five steps of evidence-based practice are Ask a focused clinical question (usually in PICO form), Acquire the best evidence, Appraise it for validity and relevance, Apply it with clinical expertise and patient preferences, and Assess the outcome. Some models add a step zero, building a culture of inquiry, and a final step, sharing results.

What evidence-based practice means

The classic definition, from David Sackett and colleagues in 1996, describes evidence-based medicine as the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients. Nursing and allied health adopted the same idea as evidence-based practice (EBP). It rests on three things together:

  • The best available research evidence, weighted by study design and quality.
  • Clinical expertise, including judgement about this patient and this setting.
  • Patient values and preferences, which can rightly lead to a different choice than the evidence alone suggests.

EBP is not cookbook care. It is a disciplined way of asking “is what we do still the best option?” and finding out.

Step 1: Ask a focused question

Vague questions produce vague searches. PICO gives a question structure:

  • Population or problem: who are the patients?
  • Intervention or exposure: what are you considering?
  • Comparison: what is the alternative, often usual care?
  • Outcome: what result matters to the patient?

Worked example. A ward nurse notices that patients with peripheral IV catheters often have them replaced every three days by routine, which is uncomfortable. The PICO question: In adult inpatients with peripheral IV catheters (P), does replacing catheters only when clinically indicated (I), compared with routine replacement every 72–96 hours (C), change rates of phlebitis and bloodstream infection (O)?

Questions about diagnosis, prognosis or experience fit variants such as PICo for qualitative questions. The AI for Research team’s guide to writing a research question with PICO and FINER covers the variants.

Step 2: Acquire the evidence

Busy clinicians should start at the top of the evidence pyramid and work down only when needed:

  1. Guidelines and summaries from national bodies and professional organisations.
  2. Systematic reviews, especially in the Cochrane Library.
  3. Individual studies in PubMed or CINAHL, starting with randomised trials for intervention questions.

For our example, a Cochrane review on clinically indicated versus routine replacement of peripheral catheters exists, which saves hours. A Boolean search in PubMed would combine the PICO concepts: ("peripheral venous catheter*" OR "peripheral intravenous") AND (replac* OR "dwell time") AND (phlebitis OR "bloodstream infection").

If you are new to the database, the AI for Research guide to using AI with PubMed shows how to search medical literature in plain language and still check every source.

Step 3: Appraise the evidence

Appraisal asks three questions of each source: Is it valid? (was the study designed and run to avoid bias?) What are the results? (how big is the effect and how precise?) Is it relevant? (are these patients and settings like mine?)

Start with study design. For intervention questions, a well-conducted systematic review of randomised trials sits at the top, then individual trials, then cohort and case-control studies, then case series and expert opinion. The Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine publishes levels-of-evidence tables and free appraisal worksheets, and AI for Research has a clear explainer on levels of evidence and study types.

Then use a checklist matched to the design. CASP and JBI checklists are widely taught in nursing programmes and walk you through each question.

In our example: the evidence from randomised trials, summarised in the Cochrane review, found no clear difference in phlebitis or bloodstream infection between the two approaches, while clinically indicated replacement used fewer catheters. Check the current version of the review before acting, because Cochrane reviews are updated.

Step 4: Apply it

Evidence alone does not change practice. Applying it means:

  • Checking fit: are your patients similar to those studied? Are there groups, such as neonates or patients with central lines, the evidence does not cover?
  • Talking with patients: explain options and respect their preferences.
  • Working through the system: local policy, the infection control team, equipment and staff training. A practice change usually needs a short proposal to a practice council or manager.
  • Planning monitoring before you start, so step 5 has data.

Step 5: Assess the outcome

Measure whether the change worked in your setting. For the IV example: phlebitis scores, catheter-related infection rates, number of catheters used and patient comfort, before and after. Use a simple audit or a plan–do–study–act cycle. Also assess your own process: was the question answerable, did the search find what you needed, how long did it take?

Share what you learned: at a team meeting, in a poster or in a short report. That last step turns one nurse’s question into the unit’s practice.

A one-page EBP worksheet

Copy this into a document and fill it in for each question. It doubles as the record you need when proposing a practice change.

CLINICAL PROBLEM:   What did you notice, and why does it matter?
PICO QUESTION:      P:            I:            C:            O:
QUESTION TYPE:      therapy / diagnosis / prognosis / harm / experience

SEARCH
  Sources searched:   guidelines / Cochrane / PubMed / CINAHL / other
  Search terms:       ...
  Date searched:      ...

EVIDENCE FOUND (best first)
  1. Source, design, level, key result, relevance to my patients
  2. ...

APPRAISAL SUMMARY:  How much do we trust this? Main limitations?
PATIENT VIEW:       What matters to the patients affected?
LOCAL FACTORS:      Policy, staffing, equipment, cost

DECISION:           Change / no change / more evidence needed
MONITORING PLAN:    What, how often, who
REVIEW DATE:        ...

Teams that use a shared worksheet find journal clubs more productive: each meeting starts from a filled-in sheet instead of a paper nobody has read. Keep completed sheets in a shared folder so the next person asking the same question does not start from scratch.

Barriers, and how to get past them

  • No time: start with pre-appraised sources such as guidelines and Cochrane reviews; one focused question a month is enough to build the habit.
  • No access to journals: use your hospital library, and open-access sources indexed in PubMed Central.
  • Unsure about statistics: learn to read relative risk, confidence intervals and number needed to treat; most appraisal decisions turn on these.
  • Culture: find an EBP champion or journal club, and bring a well-formed question rather than a complaint.

Frequently asked questions

What are the 5 steps of evidence-based practice?

Ask a focused clinical question, acquire the best evidence, appraise it critically, apply it together with clinical expertise and patient preferences, and assess the outcome. They are often called the 5 As.

What are the 7 steps of evidence-based practice?

The seven-step model widely taught in nursing adds a step zero, cultivating a spirit of inquiry, before asking the question, and a final step, disseminating the results, after evaluating the outcome. The middle steps match the 5 As.

What is a PICO question?

A PICO question breaks a clinical question into population, intervention, comparison and outcome. For example: in adult inpatients with peripheral IV catheters, does clinically indicated replacement compared with routine replacement change phlebitis rates?

What is the highest level of evidence?

For questions about treatment effects, a well-conducted systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials is usually ranked highest. The best design depends on the question: prognosis questions rely on cohort studies, and experience questions on qualitative research.

What is a journal club and how does it support EBP?

A journal club is a regular meeting where clinicians appraise a study or review together, usually linked to a real question from practice. It builds appraisal skills, spreads the reading load and turns evidence into team decisions. Short, focused meetings with a pre-filled worksheet work best.

How is evidence-based practice different from research?

Research creates new knowledge by running studies. Evidence-based practice uses existing research to make care decisions in a specific setting, alongside clinical expertise and patient preferences. Quality improvement then checks whether the change works locally.
