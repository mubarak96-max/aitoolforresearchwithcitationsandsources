How to write a cover letter for journal submission

The editor may read your cover letter before your abstract, and often in under two minutes. Its job is narrow: show that the paper fits this journal, say what is new, and confirm the paperwork. Here is how to do that on one page.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20268 min read
Short answer Address the editor by name, state the title and article type, and say in two or three sentences what question the paper answers and what it found. Then explain why it fits this journal and its readers, confirm the required statements (original work, not under review elsewhere, all authors approved, conflicts and funding declared), and suggest or exclude reviewers if the journal allows. Keep it to one page and never paste the abstract.

What an editor reads a cover letter for

Editors at busy journals screen many submissions. At this stage they are not judging whether your paper is correct; reviewers do that. They are deciding whether it is worth sending to reviewers at all. The cover letter helps them answer three questions quickly:

  1. Is this paper within our scope?
  2. Is the contribution new and important enough for our readers?
  3. Is the submission complete and ethically in order?

A letter that answers these three questions clearly makes the editor’s job easier. A generic letter that could be sent to any journal makes it harder, and suggests the paper might be a resubmission from somewhere else.

The five paragraphs

1. The submission

Name the editor, the journal, the manuscript title and the article type. One or two sentences.

2. The question and the finding

In plain language, say what problem the paper addresses and what you found. Include the single most important result, with a number if you have one. This is not the abstract; it is the sentence you would say to a colleague in a lift.

3. Why it matters and why here

Explain what the finding changes, and why this journal’s readers in particular should care. Refer to the journal’s scope or to related papers it has published recently. This paragraph is what makes the letter specific.

4. The declarations

Confirm what the journal requires. Typically: the work is original, it has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere, all authors have approved the submission, and competing interests and funding are declared. Mention preprints, prior conference presentations, or related papers by the same authors. The ICMJE Recommendations describe what many medical and science journals expect authors to disclose.

5. Reviewers and close

If the journal invites it, suggest two to four reviewers with relevant expertise and no conflicts, and list anyone you ask to exclude, with a brief, professional reason. Undisclosed relationships with suggested reviewers are treated as a serious ethics issue; the Committee on Publication Ethics has documented cases of fake reviewer suggestions. Close with the corresponding author’s name and contact details.

A fill-in template

Dear Dr [Editor surname],

Please consider our manuscript, "[Title]", for publication as a
[Research Article] in [Journal name].

[One sentence on the problem.] We [design: e.g. analysed data from
2,400 adults in a prospective cohort] and found that [main finding,
with the key number]. [One sentence on what this adds to existing
evidence.]

These findings will interest readers of [Journal] because [link to
scope, readership or recent related papers in the journal]. [One
sentence on practical or theoretical implications.]

This manuscript is original, has not been published, and is not under
consideration elsewhere. [A preprint is available at ... / An earlier
version was presented at ...] All authors have approved the submission.
[Competing interests statement.] [Funding statement.]

We suggest the following reviewers: [Name, institution, email, one
line on expertise]. We request that [Name] not review this manuscript
because [brief, professional reason].

Sincerely,
[Name], on behalf of all authors
[Institution, email, ORCID]

A short example

Here are paragraphs 2 and 3 for a hypothetical education study:

We report a randomised trial in 312 primary school classrooms showing that a ten-minute daily retrieval practice routine improved end-of-year maths scores by 0.21 standard deviations, with the largest gains among pupils in the lowest attainment quartile. Previous trials of retrieval practice have mostly been small laboratory studies with older students; ours tests it at scale in ordinary classrooms. Given the journal’s focus on evidence for classroom practice, and its recent special issue on learning strategies, we believe the findings will be of direct use to its readers.

Notice what the example does: one design sentence, one number, one sentence on novelty, one on fit. Nothing is copied from the abstract, and nothing claims to be “groundbreaking”. An editor can decide in thirty seconds whether to read further.

Adjusting the letter by article type

  • Systematic reviews and meta-analyses: state the registration number of the protocol, the number of studies included, and confirm that the paper follows PRISMA 2020. Editors want to know the review is not a duplicate of a recent one, so say how it differs.
  • Clinical trials: give the trial registration number and confirm that the CONSORT checklist is attached.
  • Short communications: explain why the finding needs rapid publication or why a full article is not warranted.
  • Methods papers: say who will use the method and what it does that existing methods cannot.
  • Papers based on a thesis: mention that the work comes from a thesis if the journal asks, and confirm the thesis is not counted as prior publication under its policy.

Before you submit

  • The journal name and editor name are correct (check twice if you are resubmitting from another journal).
  • The letter fits on one page.
  • The main finding is stated with a number or concrete outcome.
  • There is at least one sentence that could only apply to this journal.
  • All required declarations are present and match the manuscript.
  • Suggested reviewers have no recent collaboration with any author.
  • You have not used words like “novel”, “first” or “groundbreaking” without evidence.

Templates and formatting

Ready-made research emails and journal formatting

AI for Research keeps a set of research email templates covering cover letters, pre-submission enquiries and responses to editors, which you can adapt rather than starting from a blank page. When the letter is done, their journal formatter checks the manuscript itself against the target journal’s structure and reference style, which is where many desk rejections really come from.

Mistakes that hurt your chances

  • Pasting the abstract. The editor already has it. Use the letter to say what the abstract cannot: why this journal.
  • Overclaiming. Calling a cross-sectional study “definitive proof” undermines trust before the review begins.
  • The wrong journal name. It happens more often than you would think, and it signals a resubmission sent without care.
  • Burying conflicts. Declare anything that could look like a conflict. Discovered later, it is far more damaging.
  • Suggesting friends as reviewers. Editors check, and some journals now treat undisclosed relationships as misconduct.

Cover letters for revisions and resubmissions

A revision needs a different letter. Thank the editor and reviewers, summarise the main changes in a few bullet points, and point to the separate response document that answers each comment. Keep the tone constructive even if you disagree with a reviewer; explain your reasoning with evidence.

If you are submitting to a new journal after a rejection, write a new letter for the new journal. You do not usually need to mention the previous rejection, but if the new journal asks, or if you are transferring through a publisher’s cascade system, be honest.

Before the letter comes the journal. A good cover letter cannot rescue a paper sent to the wrong journal. If you have not chosen yet, start with our guide on how to choose a journal, and check your target against the predatory journal warning signs.

Using AI to draft the letter

A cover letter is a reasonable place to use AI help, because it summarises your own work rather than generating new claims. Give the tool your abstract, the journal’s aims and scope, and a note on why you chose the journal, and ask for a one-page draft in the five-paragraph structure. Then check every claim against the manuscript, remove any inflated language, and make sure the declarations are accurate. Some journals ask you to disclose AI use in preparing submissions; read our guide to AI policies in academic writing for the usual expectations.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a cover letter for a journal be?

One page, roughly 250 to 400 words. Editors read many letters; a short, specific one that states the finding and explains the fit is more effective than a long one.

Is a cover letter required for journal submission?

Most journals ask for one or provide a field for it in the submission system. Even where it is optional, a short letter that explains the fit with the journal helps the editor and costs you little.

Should I suggest reviewers in my cover letter?

If the journal invites suggestions, yes. Suggest experts without recent collaboration or other conflicts with any author, and give a line on each one’s expertise. Editors may or may not use them.

Should I copy my abstract into the cover letter?

No. The editor has the abstract. Use the letter to summarise the main finding in plain language and explain why it suits this specific journal and its readers.

Who should I address the cover letter to?

Address the editor-in-chief or the handling editor for your subject area by name, as listed on the journal website. Avoid generic greetings such as “Dear Sir” where you can find a name.
