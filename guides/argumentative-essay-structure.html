Argumentative essay structure: an outline, a worked example and a checklist

An argumentative essay takes a position on a debatable question and defends it with evidence. The structure is simple once you see it; the hard part is choosing a claim worth arguing and treating the other side fairly. This guide covers both.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer An argumentative essay has an introduction that ends with a clear, debatable thesis; body paragraphs that each make one reason for the thesis and support it with evidence; at least one paragraph that states the strongest counterargument and answers it; and a conclusion that shows why the argument matters. Each body paragraph follows claim, evidence, explanation, link.

Start with an arguable thesis

Everything in an argumentative essay serves the thesis, so test it before you outline. A good thesis is:

  • Debatable. A reasonable person could disagree. “Exercise is good for health” is a fact, not an argument.
  • Specific. It names who, what and under what conditions. “Social media is bad” is too broad to defend in 2,000 words.
  • Supportable. Evidence exists that could back it up, and you can find it.
  • Directional. It hints at your reasons, which gives the reader a map.

Compare: “Universities should restrict AI tools” (vague) with “Universities should allow AI tools for finding and summarising sources but require students to disclose any AI-written text, because blanket bans are unenforceable and disclosure protects assessment” (arguable, specific, directional). AI for Research has a fuller guide on what makes a strong thesis statement.

Three models of argument

Most essays use the classical model, but knowing the other two helps with hard topics.

ModelHow it worksBest for
Classical (Aristotelian)State the claim, give reasons with evidence, refute the opposition, conclude.Most assignments; a clear position on a familiar debate.
ToulminClaim, grounds (evidence), warrant (why the evidence supports the claim), backing, qualifier, rebuttal.Complex or technical claims where the link between evidence and claim needs spelling out.
RogerianDescribe the opposing view fairly, find common ground, then propose a position both sides can accept.Divisive topics where the reader starts out disagreeing.

The Toulmin idea of the warrant is worth borrowing even in a classical essay. Many weak paragraphs give evidence and a claim but never say why one supports the other.

The outline

Short essay (about 1,000 words)

  1. Introduction: hook, context, thesis.
  2. Reason 1 with evidence.
  3. Reason 2 with evidence.
  4. Counterargument and rebuttal.
  5. Conclusion: restate the thesis in new words, show why it matters.

Longer essay (2,000 to 4,000 words)

  1. Introduction (10%): context, the debate, key terms defined, thesis, roadmap.
  2. Background (10%, optional): what readers need to know to follow the argument.
  3. Three or four reasons (55%), each a section of one to three paragraphs, ordered from strongest to most nuanced or building logically.
  4. Counterarguments (15%): the strongest one or two objections, stated fairly, each answered.
  5. Conclusion (10%): what your argument establishes, its limits and its implications.

Some writers place the counterargument straight after the introduction, then build their case. That works well when the opposing view is the common one and readers expect it to be addressed first.

Building a body paragraph

Each body paragraph makes one point. A reliable pattern is CEEL:

  • Claim: a topic sentence stating the reason, linked to the thesis.
  • Evidence: data, a study, an example or an expert source, cited.
  • Explanation: why this evidence supports the claim (the warrant). This is where marks are won.
  • Link: a sentence connecting back to the thesis or forward to the next point.

Weak paragraphs drop the explanation and let the evidence speak for itself. It rarely does. Two sentences of explanation per piece of evidence is a good minimum.

Good evidence for academic essays comes from peer-reviewed research, official statistics and primary documents, not blogs or opinion pieces. If you are not sure how to judge a source, our guide to critical appraisal tools explains how researchers do it.

Handling the counterargument

Markers look for this paragraph, and it is where most essays are weakest. Four rules:

  1. Pick the strongest objection, not an easy one. Arguing against a weak version of the other side is a straw man, and readers notice.
  2. State it fairly, in terms its supporters would accept, with evidence.
  3. Respond by showing it is wrong, outweighed, or compatible with your thesis under some conditions.
  4. Concede where honest. “This is true for small classes, but…” makes you more credible, not less.

Useful phrases: “Critics argue that…”, “A strong objection is…”, “This concern is valid where…, but…”, “Even if we accept…, it does not follow that…”.

A worked example

Question: Should cities ban cars from their historic centres?

Thesis: Cities should ban private cars from historic centres, with exemptions for residents, deliveries and disabled drivers, because doing so reduces air pollution, protects fragile buildings and, when paired with public transport, keeps the centres economically active.

  • Reason 1: air quality, with measured pollution data from cities that introduced restrictions.
  • Reason 2: heritage protection, with evidence on vibration and pollution damage to old stone.
  • Reason 3: economic activity, with footfall and retail data from pedestrianised streets.
  • Counterargument: businesses lose customers who drive. Response: evidence on footfall, conceding that the effect depends on transport alternatives and the transition period.
  • Conclusion: the case is strongest where public transport exists; cities without it should build it first.

Notice that the qualifier (“with exemptions”, “when paired with public transport”) is part of the thesis. Qualified claims are easier to defend and more persuasive.

Writing the introduction and conclusion

The introduction and conclusion frame the argument, and markers read them first and last.

Introduction

  1. Hook with something specific to your topic: a striking fact, a recent case or a sharp statement of the debate. Avoid dictionary definitions and sweeping claims about history.
  2. Context: two or three sentences on why the question is contested and who holds which view.
  3. Key terms: define anything the argument depends on. If you argue about “remote work”, say whether hybrid work counts.
  4. Thesis: usually the last sentence or two of the introduction.
  5. Roadmap (in longer essays): a sentence saying what each section will do.

Conclusion

A strong conclusion does more than repeat. It restates the thesis in light of the argument you have made, draws together the reasons in a sentence, and answers “so what?”: what should change, who should care, or what question your argument opens. It can acknowledge the limits of your case. It should not introduce new evidence or a new argument.

Transitions carry the argument between these frames. Start each body paragraph with a sentence that links back to the thesis or the previous point: “A second reason…”, “Beyond the environmental case…”, “Even if cost were not a concern…”. Readers should never wonder why a paragraph is there.

A useful test: if a reader read only your introduction and conclusion, would they know your position, your main reasons and why it matters? If yes, the frame works.

Common mistakes

  • A thesis that is a fact or a question, not a position.
  • Listing evidence without explaining why it supports the claim.
  • Ignoring the other side, or attacking a weak version of it.
  • Emotional language in place of reasons. Persuasion in academic essays comes from logic and evidence.
  • A conclusion that adds new evidence. Use it to draw out implications, not to start a new point.
  • Uncited claims. Every factual claim needs a source. Our guide on paraphrasing without plagiarising covers how to use sources in your own words.

Marking checklist

  • The thesis is debatable, specific and stated in the introduction.
  • Each body paragraph makes one reason that supports the thesis.
  • Each piece of evidence is cited and explained.
  • The strongest counterargument is stated fairly and answered.
  • Paragraphs are linked by clear transitions.
  • Key terms are defined.
  • The conclusion restates the thesis in new words and shows its significance.
  • References are in the required style. See citation styles compared.

Frequently asked questions

How many paragraphs is an argumentative essay?

A short essay usually has five paragraphs: an introduction, two or three reasons, a counterargument and a conclusion. Longer essays of 2,000 words or more have sections, with several paragraphs for each reason and counterargument. Length should follow the argument, not a fixed count.

Where does the counterargument go?

Most often just before the conclusion, after you have made your case. It can also go straight after the introduction if the opposing view is the common one, or inside each body paragraph if each reason has its own objection.

What is the difference between an argumentative and a persuasive essay?

An argumentative essay relies on evidence and logic and must address opposing views fairly. A persuasive essay can use emotional appeals and may ignore the other side. Academic assignments almost always want argumentative essays.

Can I use “I” in an argumentative essay?

It depends on your instructor and field. Many humanities and social science courses accept “I argue that”, while others prefer the third person. When unsure, ask, or write the thesis without the first person.
