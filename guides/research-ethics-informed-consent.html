Informed consent for research: what the form must include

Informed consent is not a signature. It is a process in which people understand what taking part involves and choose freely whether to do it. The form is the record of that process, and it is the document ethics committees read most closely.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 20267 min read
Short answer A research consent form, usually paired with a participant information sheet, should explain in plain language: the purpose of the study, what participants will do and for how long, risks and benefits, how data will be stored, used, shared and anonymised, that taking part is voluntary and they can withdraw (and until when), who is running and funding the study, and who to contact with questions or complaints. Participants then confirm each point and sign or record consent.

The principles behind consent

Modern research ethics rests on a few shared principles. The US Belmont Report (1979) names three: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Informed consent is how respect for persons is put into practice. For consent to be valid, it has to be:

  • Informed: people receive the information a reasonable person would need to decide.
  • Understood: the information is presented in a way they can follow.
  • Voluntary: there is no pressure, undue reward or power imbalance pushing them to say yes.
  • Competent: the person has the capacity to decide, or an appropriate representative decides with them.
  • Ongoing: they can ask questions and withdraw later, not just at the start.

Specific legal rules vary by country and field. In the US, human-subjects research funded or regulated by federal agencies follows the Common Rule, 45 CFR 46, which lists required consent elements. In the UK, health and social care research goes through the Health Research Authority, which publishes consent guidance and templates. Universities add their own rules. Always use your institution’s template if it has one.

What an informed consent form must include

Most institutions split the paperwork into a participant information sheet (the explanation) and a consent form (the confirmation). Between them, they should cover:

ElementWhat to say
Study title and researchersWho is running the study, their institution, and the supervisor for student projects
PurposeWhat the study is trying to find out, in one or two sentences
Why they were invitedHow participants were identified and why they fit
What taking part involvesTasks, number of sessions, time required, location, recording
Risks and discomfortsAny foreseeable risks, including emotional ones, and how they will be managed
BenefitsDirect benefits, if any, and wider benefits; do not overstate
Payment or incentivesWhat is offered and whether it depends on completing the study
Voluntary participationThey can decline or stop at any time without giving a reason or losing anything
WithdrawalHow to withdraw, and until when their data can be removed (e.g. before analysis or publication)
Confidentiality and anonymityWho sees identifiable data, how quotes are anonymised, limits of confidentiality (e.g. risk of harm)
Data storage and useWhere data is stored, for how long, who can access it, whether it may be shared or reused
ResultsHow results will be published and whether participants can receive a summary
Funding and approvalWho funds the study, and which committee approved it (with reference number)
Contacts and complaintsResearcher contact, supervisor contact, and an independent contact for complaints

The consent statements

The consent form itself is usually a list of statements participants initial or tick, for example:

  • I have read the information sheet dated [date, version] and had the chance to ask questions.
  • I understand that taking part is voluntary and I can withdraw at any time without giving a reason.
  • I agree to the interview being audio recorded.
  • I agree that anonymised quotes may be used in publications.
  • I understand how my data will be stored and for how long.
  • I agree to be contacted about future studies (optional).
  • I agree to take part in this study.

Keep optional items, such as future contact or data sharing, separate from the core consent, so people can say yes to the study and no to the extras.

Write it in plain language

Committees send back more consent forms for unclear language than for missing content. Practical rules:

  • Write for a reading age of about 12 unless your participants are specialists.
  • Use “you” and “we”, short sentences and active verbs.
  • Replace jargon: “randomised” becomes “chosen by chance, like tossing a coin”; “anonymised” becomes “we will remove your name and details that could identify you”.
  • Use headings phrased as questions: “What will I be asked to do?”, “What happens to my information?”
  • Put the most important points first. The US Common Rule now asks for a concise summary of key information at the start of longer forms, which is good practice everywhere.
  • Translate the documents for participants who need it, and say how you checked the translation.

Do not promise what you cannot keep. “Your data will be completely anonymous” is often untrue for interviews or small groups. Say what you will do (remove names, change details) and be honest that someone who knows you might still recognise a quote.

Written, online and verbal consent

  • Written consent: a signed form, standard for interviews, experiments and clinical research.
  • Online consent: for web surveys, an information page followed by a required tick box (“I have read the information above and agree to take part”) before the first question. Keep a record of consent with the response.
  • Verbal consent: for phone interviews or low-literacy settings, read each statement aloud, record agreement on audio, and note it in a log. Check that your committee accepts this.
  • Implied consent: for some anonymous surveys, returning the questionnaire counts as consent. State this clearly on the first page.

Data protection law can add its own requirements, separate from ethical consent. In the UK and EU, for example, data protection rules may require a specific legal basis for processing research data and a privacy notice. Your institution’s data protection officer can tell you which applies.

Children and other groups that need extra care

  • Children: usually parental or guardian consent plus the child’s own assent, with an age-appropriate information sheet.
  • Adults who may lack capacity: specific legal frameworks apply in most countries; seek specialist advice.
  • People in dependent relationships (your students, employees, patients): explain how you will avoid pressure, for example by having someone else recruit them.
  • Communities and groups: some settings expect community or gatekeeper agreement as well as individual consent.
  • Online and secondary data: public posts and existing datasets raise separate questions about consent and privacy. Check your committee’s guidance before collecting them.

Getting through ethics review faster

  1. Use your institution’s templates and keep their headings.
  2. Make every document match. The procedure in the application, the information sheet, the consent form and the interview guide or survey must describe the same study. Mismatches are the most common reason for revisions.
  3. Version and date every document.
  4. Explain how you handle the risks you name, such as distress protocols and signposting to support services.
  5. Attach your instruments. Committees want to see the actual questions. Our guides to the interview guide and survey questions help you get those ready.
  6. Plan the timing. Build approval time and a buffer into your dissertation timeline.

Frequently asked questions

What should an informed consent form include?

The study’s purpose, what participants will do and for how long, risks and benefits, how data will be stored, used and anonymised, that participation is voluntary and they can withdraw, who runs and funds the study, which committee approved it, and contacts for questions and complaints, followed by consent statements the participant confirms.

What is the difference between an information sheet and a consent form?

The participant information sheet explains the study in detail. The consent form is a shorter document where the participant confirms they have read and understood the information and agree to specific points, such as recording or the use of quotes.

Do online surveys need informed consent?

Yes, in most cases. The usual approach is an information page followed by a required tick box confirming consent before the survey starts. Some anonymous, low-risk surveys use implied consent, where submitting the survey counts as agreement, if the committee approves.

Can participants withdraw from a study after taking part?

They should be able to stop at any time. Withdrawing data after collection is usually possible until a stated point, such as before analysis or anonymisation, because data cannot always be removed afterwards. State that point clearly in the information sheet.

Is verbal consent acceptable in research?

Often, yes, for phone or online interviews or where written forms are impractical, if the ethics committee approves. The researcher reads the consent statements, records the participant’s agreement, and keeps a log.
