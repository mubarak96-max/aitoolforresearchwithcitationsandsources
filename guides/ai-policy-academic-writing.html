Is it OK to use AI for academic writing? What universities and journals allow

The rules are less mysterious than they seem. Almost every policy rests on two ideas: you are responsible for what you submit, and you must be honest about how it was made. Everything else is detail.

By the Methods & Sources editorial teamUpdated 14 November 202611 min read
Short answer It depends on your institution, but a common pattern has emerged. Using AI to find and understand sources, brainstorm and fix language is usually allowed, sometimes with disclosure. Submitting AI-generated text, analysis or ideas as your own work is usually not. AI can never be an author, you remain responsible for every claim and citation, and when in doubt you should disclose.

This is a general guide, not your rulebook. Policies vary between universities, departments, courses and journals, and they change often. Your module handbook, thesis regulations, supervisor or journal guidelines always take priority over anything here.

The two principles behind almost every policy

1. You are responsible for everything you submit

AI tools cannot be held accountable, so they cannot be authors. This is the position of publishing ethics bodies such as the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), it is reflected in the ICMJE recommendations used by many medical journals, and most large publishers say the same. In practice it means that if an AI-assisted paragraph contains a wrong claim or a fabricated reference, it is your error, with the same consequences as any other.

2. You must be transparent about how the work was made

Most policies require you to say when, and how, AI contributed beyond basic spelling and grammar. Journals typically ask for a statement in the methods or acknowledgements. Universities may ask for a declaration on the submission form or in the thesis front matter. Undisclosed use is where most misconduct cases start, even when the use itself might have been allowed.

The common policy positions

University policies tend to fall into one of four broad positions. Many universities let each course choose its own, so two modules in the same degree can differ.

PositionWhat it meansTypical where
ProhibitedNo generative AI for the assessment at allExams, some essays testing writing skill
LimitedAI for named tasks only (e.g. search, grammar); no AI-written textMany theses and dissertations
Permitted with disclosureAI allowed if you state what you used and howMany coursework assignments, most journals
IntegratedAI use expected and part of the taskSome courses teaching AI-assisted methods

Journals have converged more than universities. The usual pattern is: AI cannot be an author, use beyond language editing must be disclosed, authors are responsible for accuracy, and AI-generated images are restricted or banned. A good summary of how specific universities and publishers phrase this is the AI for Research overview of AI policies at universities and journals.

A traffic-light guide to AI uses

This is how uses are commonly treated where AI is not banned outright. Check your own rules for anything amber or red.

Usually allowed

  • Searching for literature with a tool that returns real, citable papers.
  • Asking for explanations of concepts, methods or difficult passages.
  • Spelling, grammar and punctuation checks.
  • Formatting citations and reference lists.
  • Converting files, such as Word to LaTeX.

Often allowed with disclosure or permission

  • Brainstorming topics, research questions or outlines.
  • Summarising papers for your own notes.
  • Feedback on your draft, such as structure, clarity or gaps in the argument.
  • Help writing analysis code that you check and understand.
  • Paraphrasing or rewriting your own sentences for language, especially for non-native speakers.

Usually not allowed

  • Submitting AI-generated paragraphs, sections or chapters as your own writing.
  • Having AI interpret your results or generate your conclusions.
  • Citing references you have not checked, or that the AI produced from memory.
  • Using AI to disguise copied text or to evade detection.
  • Uploading confidential participant data to tools your ethics approval does not cover.

How to disclose AI use

A good disclosure is specific: the tool, what you used it for, and what you did to check the output. Vague statements such as “AI was used in preparing this work” help nobody. Here is a sample you can adapt:

Use of AI tools
I used [tool name] to search for and summarise literature
during the review stage, and to check grammar in Chapters 2-4.
All sources were read and verified by me. No text generated
by AI has been included without being rewritten by me, and
all analysis and interpretation are my own. A record of the
drafting process is available on request.

Where to put it: journals usually specify the methods section, acknowledgements or a dedicated declaration. For theses, the front matter or a declaration page is common. Follow the template if one is given.

Keep evidence of how you wrote

AI detectors are unreliable. They produce probabilities, not proof, and they are known to flag human writing, particularly by non-native English speakers. If your work is ever questioned, the best defence is a record of your process: drafts, version history, notes, and the sources you read.

  • Write in a tool with version history, and do not paste in a finished draft from elsewhere.
  • Keep your reading notes and literature matrix.
  • Save the prompts and outputs of any AI use you disclose.
  • Keep data, code and analysis files with dates.

Special cases: data, code, images and peer review

Some uses of AI raise issues beyond authorship and need their own checks.

Research data and participants

Uploading interview transcripts, survey responses or patient records to an AI tool can breach your consent forms, your ethics approval or data protection law, even if AI use is otherwise allowed. Before uploading, check what participants agreed to, what your ethics approval says about third-party processing, and whether the tool stores or trains on uploads. Anonymise first, and if in doubt, ask your ethics committee or data protection officer. Our guide to research ethics and informed consent covers what consent forms usually need to say about data processing.

Analysis code

Using AI to help write analysis code is increasingly common and often allowed. The responsibility is the same as for text: you must understand what the code does, check that it runs the analysis you intended, and report it accurately. Keep the final code with your data, for example in an OSF project, so others can reproduce your results, and mention AI assistance if your journal asks.

Figures and images

Many publishers restrict or ban AI-generated images in research papers because of the risk to the integrity of the scientific record. Diagrams you draw yourself with software are fine; images generated or altered by AI, especially anything that looks like data, usually are not. Check the journal’s policy before including any AI-made figure.

Peer review and examining

If you review manuscripts, grant applications or theses, the material is confidential. Many journals and funders forbid uploading it to AI tools, because that shares unpublished work with a third party. Some allow limited language help with your own review text. Read the reviewer guidelines; if they are silent, ask the editor before you use any AI tool.

Talking to your supervisor

The simplest way to avoid trouble is to agree on AI use early. Tell your supervisor which tools you plan to use and for what, ask whether that fits the regulations, and confirm the agreement by email. It takes ten minutes and removes most of the risk.

A safe AI-assisted workflow for a thesis or paper

  1. Check the rules first. Find the written policy for your course, thesis or target journal. If it is unclear, ask your supervisor or editor in writing.
  2. Use AI for discovery and understanding. Find papers, then read them yourself. Our guide to reading research papers faster shows how to do this without relying on summaries.
  3. Write your own first draft. Your argument, your structure, your words.
  4. Use AI for feedback, not replacement. Ask where the argument is weak or a claim needs a source, then fix it yourself.
  5. Verify every reference. Open each one and check it supports the sentence. See how to spot hallucinated citations.
  6. Disclose precisely and keep your process records.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use AI to write my thesis?

Most universities do not allow AI to write thesis text that you submit as your own. Many do allow AI for searching literature, understanding sources, brainstorming and language editing, sometimes with disclosure. Your university's thesis regulations and your supervisor have the final word.

Can AI be listed as an author on a paper?

No. Major publishing ethics bodies, including COPE and the ICMJE recommendations, and most large publishers, state that AI tools cannot be authors because they cannot take responsibility for the work. Human authors are accountable for everything in the paper.

Do I have to disclose that I used AI?

Usually yes, if you used it for anything beyond basic spelling and grammar. Journals typically ask for a statement in the methods or acknowledgements; universities may ask for a declaration on the cover sheet. When in doubt, disclose.

Can AI detectors prove I used AI?

No. AI detectors produce probabilities, not proof, and they can flag human writing, especially from non-native English writers. A record of how your document was written, such as version history or an authorship report, is stronger evidence than a detector score either way.

Is using AI for grammar checking allowed?

In most cases, yes. Spelling and grammar tools are widely accepted, and many policies exempt them from disclosure. Rewriting whole paragraphs with AI is a different matter and is often treated like AI-generated text.
