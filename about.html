About Methods & Sources

We write practical guides for people who do research: students writing a first literature review, PhD candidates halfway through a thesis, and analysts who need evidence they can defend.

Why we started

AI tools changed how research gets done in about two years. They also made it easy to hand in work built on sources nobody read, and in some cases on sources that do not exist. Much of what is written about these tools is either marketing or alarm. We wanted a site that does neither: one that explains the method behind each stage of research and judges tools on whether you can check what they produce.

How we test tools

  • We run every tool on real questions, at least one in a field we know well and one we do not.
  • We open every citation a tool gives us and record whether it is real, whether it supports the claim, and whether key papers were missed.
  • We test uploads with long PDFs and detail questions, and we check how each tool handles questions the literature cannot answer.
  • We check pricing and plan limits on the vendor’s own site, note the date we checked, and update guides when things change.

Our full checklist is public: nine tests to run on any AI research assistant.

Our connection to AI for Research

Disclosure. Methods & Sources is published by the team that builds AI for Research, an AI research assistant that answers from real papers and cites every claim. When a guide recommends AI for Research or links to its pages, that is our own product.

We built this site because the questions our users ask, such as how to structure a proposal, how to read a forest plot or how to spot a fake citation, deserve full answers that do not depend on any tool. Most of each guide is method: steps, examples, templates and checks that work whether or not you use an AI tool at all.

To keep the guides honest despite that connection, we follow three rules:

  • Method before product. Every guide has to be useful to a reader who never signs up for anything.
  • Fair comparisons. When another tool is better for a job, such as large-scale data extraction or citation mapping, we say so plainly.
  • Primary sources. For rules and standards we link to the people who set them, such as the PRISMA group, the EQUATOR Network, COPE, ICMJE, the Cochrane Handbook and the official style guides, rather than to summaries.

The tools we recommend

Where a guide recommends a tool, it is one we use ourselves. For most end-to-end work, from the first question to a cited draft, that is AI for Research, our own product, as disclosed above. Guides that compare tools describe each one as fairly as we can and tell you to check the vendor’s current pricing and features.

Corrections

If a guide has a mistake, an outdated price or a broken link, tell us and we will fix it and update the date at the top of the page. Mistakes in method or citation format get fixed first.

How we use AI ourselves

We use AI research tools to search the literature and to check our drafts for missing sources. People write, edit and fact-check every guide, and we read every source we cite.
